---

## Reader's notice — keep this attached

These are **analytical charging instruments**, not legal accusations. No count below asserts that any named person has committed a crime. Each count states a *theory of liability* under a real framework, lists the public-record facts supporting it, states the innocent explanation at full strength, and either defeats it or concedes.

Where a count is not supported, it is returned **NO BILL** and the reason is recorded. A charging document where every count is returned is not a charging document. It is a press release.

**Standing rules retained:** No blood guilt. No unified actor. No coordination is alleged between counts. Each count is independent; nothing here claims the parties acted together.

---

## Jury instruction on circumstantial evidence

Direct evidence of intent is almost never available in institutional cases. Nobody keeps minutes of the meeting where they agree to do the thing.

Circumstantial sufficiency requires:

1. **Every brick independently admissible.** One inadmissible brick does not sink the wall, but a wall made only of inference does.
2. **Pattern over instance.** One conflicted transaction is a coincidence. Three is a practice.
3. **The innocent explanation must be defeated, not ignored.** If a juror can say "or it could just be X" and X survives, the count fails.
4. **Elements, not themes.** "The elite are corrupt" is not an element. "A fiduciary participated in a transaction in which he held an undisclosed adverse interest" is.

The standard is *not* lower than proof. It is differently distributed — lighter on any single fact, heavier on the reasoning connecting them.

# COUNT I — Breach of charitable trust

**Party:** The OpenAI Foundation and its board
**Framework:** California charitable trust law; the 27 October 2025 MOU with the California AG; nonprofit fiduciary duty
**Theory:** Charitable assets were transferred without independent valuation, and control over the transferred enterprise was retained through an instrument decoupled from economic exposure and defined by no external authority.

### Elements

1. Assets held subject to a charitable trust
2. Transfer of those assets to a non-charitable entity
3. Absence of independent confirmation of fair value
4. Retention of governing authority separable from the economic stake
5. The governing term left undefined and unappealable

### Bricks

| # | Fact | Class |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2025 recapitalization split the entity; Foundation holds ~26% of OpenAI Group PBC | P1 |
| 2 | Class N share confers near-total board appointment power **plus sole authority over "safety and security decisions"** | P1 |
| 3 | Those decisions are **undefined in the articles of incorporation** | P1 |
| 4 | Class N control **survives divestment of the entire 26% equity stake** | P1 |
| 5 | **No independent appraiser** confirmed the Foundation received fair value | P1 (negative) |
| 6 | CA AG non-objection was **explicitly conditional** on continued genuine-nonprofit operation | P1 |
| 7 | CA AG has **confirmed an ongoing investigation** in response to a PRA request | P2 |
| 8 | The 100x profit cap was removed | P1 |
| 9 | The word *safely* was removed from the mission statement | P1 |

### The defense case, stated at its strongest

*Two attorneys general reviewed this restructuring and neither objected. The Foundation emerged holding equity valued at roughly $130B — later marked above $200B — making it one of the best-resourced philanthropies in history. That is not a looting; it is arguably the most generous outcome available. Class N exists precisely to keep mission control in charitable hands, which is what critics demanded. Leaving "safety and security decisions" broad is a feature: enumerate them and you create a checklist that a bad actor routes around. And the Foundation cannot lawfully use that control for private benefit — it remains bound by charitable trust law and by a signed MOU with an AG who is actively watching.*

### Knockdown

**On the AGs:** non-objection is not a valuation finding. Bonta's sign-off was conditional *by its own terms*, and the office has since confirmed it is still investigating — which is not the posture of a regulator who considers the question settled.

**On the $130B:** unverified. That figure is a mark, not an appraisal. Without an independent fairness opinion nobody — including the Foundation's own board — can say whether 26% was the right number. "Generous" is an assertion by the party that set the price.

**On Class N as protection:** this is the count's center of gravity. If Class N existed to keep *mission* control in charitable hands, it would be tied to the charitable stake. It is not. The Foundation can sell every share and still appoint the board. That severs governance from any economic alignment with the beneficiaries. A control right that survives the disappearance of the trust corpus is not protecting the trust.

**On leaving the term broad:** the anti-checklist argument would carry if the breadth ran toward *more* accountability. It runs the other way — undefined scope, single interpreter, no appeal, no external construction. That is not a shield against bad actors. It is a shield against review.

### Verdict

**TRUE BILL.** Elements 1–5 all supported by primary instruments. The defense case is coherent and does not survive element 4.

**What flips it:** an independent fairness opinion on the 2025 transfer, or a charter amendment enumerating "safety and security decisions" with an external appeal route.

# COUNT II — Conflicted transactions

**Party:** Sam Altman, in his capacity as an officer and fiduciary
**Framework:** Corporate and nonprofit fiduciary duty; SEC disclosure obligations attaching on registration
**Theory:** A fiduciary repeatedly steered or proposed transactions toward entities in which he held substantial undisclosed personal interests, under a governance structure that gave him no offsetting stake in the entity he owed the duty to.

### Elements

1. Fiduciary position
2. Substantial personal financial interest in a counterparty
3. Participation in, or proposal of, the transaction
4. Inadequate contemporaneous disclosure or recusal record
5. Pattern rather than instance

### Bricks

| # | Fact | Class |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Salary ~$65,000/yr; **no direct equity in OpenAI** | P3 |
| 2 | Stakes **exceeding $2B** in companies transacting with OpenAI or its partners | P3 (WSJ) |
| 3 | He and affiliated funds have invested in **400+ companies** | P3 |
| 4 | **Helion:** shareholder since 2014, ≥$375M invested, stake est. ~$1.7B, board seat — proposed OpenAI commit ~$500M | P3 |
| 5 | **Stoke Space:** shareholder via family office — approached re: OpenAI data-center partnership | P3 |
| 6 | **Merge Labs:** board seat, reportedly no equity — OpenAI announced backing | P3 |
| 7 | Six state AGs to SEC: he "has a history of self-dealing and serious conflicts of interest" | P2 |
| 8 | Same letter: with no direct equity, **his interests have only limited alignment with OpenAI's performance** | P2 |
| 9 | House Oversight demanded all records of the audit committee formed after the 2023 ouster | P2 |
| 10 | Lack of clarity about these investments was **a cited reason for the 2023 ouster** | P3 |
| 11 | Greg Brockman testified he holds stakes in Altman's family fund and Altman-founded startups | P1 |

### The defense case, stated at its strongest

*He testified under oath that he has "always been recused." Board chair Bret Taylor testified he had been forthright, proactive and transparent. No transaction has been shown to have closed on disadvantageous terms. A founder of his tenure investing in 400 companies over fifteen years will inevitably overlap with any sector his employer touches — that is unavoidable, not culpable. Helion never received the $500M. And taking no equity in OpenAI was widely praised as the opposite of self-enrichment.*

### Knockdown

**On recusal:** recusal is an assertion until the minutes exist. The audit committee formed in 2023 to police precisely this has not produced its record, and House Oversight has formally demanded it. A defendant whose exculpatory document is in his own custody and undisclosed is not in a strong position.

**On inevitability:** three named transactions in overlapping sectors, each one *proposed or approached by him*, defeats "inevitable overlap." Overlap is passive. Proposal is not. Element 5 is met.

**On the deal not closing:** irrelevant to the duty. The breach, if any, attaches to participation in the process, not the outcome.

**On taking no equity — this is where the defense inverts.** The absence of equity is not exculpatory; it is the structural core of the count, and the argument comes from six Republican attorneys general, not from critics of the industry. A fiduciary with no stake in the entity he serves and $2B in its counterparties has interests that *diverge* from the entity. He can be enriched by outcomes that leave OpenAI worse off. Recusal does not cure that. It is not a conflict inside a transaction — it is a conflict standing outside all of them.

### Verdict

**TRUE BILL** on elements 1, 2, 3, 5. **Element 4 held open** pending the audit-committee record.

**Do not overcharge this.** No specific transaction has been shown to have been improperly influenced. The chargeable object is the structure and the pattern, not a proven act.

**What flips it:** complete recusal minutes for Helion, Stoke and Merge, plus the 2023 audit-committee record.

# COUNT III — Procurement integrity and statutory circumvention

**Party:** DHS/ICE as awarding agencies; Palantir Technologies as vendor
**Framework:** Federal procurement rules on sole-source justification; the Privacy Act of 1974
**Theory:** A vendor's self-created incumbency was used to justify non-competitive awards for a system whose architecture defeats the separation of federal records that the Privacy Act was enacted to require.

### Elements

1. Non-competitive award
2. Justification resting on incumbency the vendor itself established
3. Resulting system consolidates records Congress deliberately siloed
4. Absence of accuracy, audit or error-rate disclosure
5. Cost of failure borne by parties with no route of appeal

### Bricks

| # | Fact | Class |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Federal contract value: **$4.4M (2009) → $541.2M (2024) → $970.5M (2025)** | P2 |
| 2 | **ImmigrationOS: $30M sole-source modification, Contract ID 70CTD022FR0000170**, later reported ~$60M | P2 |
| 3 | Justification cites Executive Orders 14159 and 13773 | P2 |
| 4 | Sole-source basis: Palantir has run ICE's Investigative Case Management **since 2014** | P2 |
| 5 | Vendor holds contracts on **both the enforcement (ICE) and benefits (USCIS) sides** | P3 |
| 6 | Ingestion spans IRS, SSA, passport, DMV, license-plate-reader data; ~**80M Medicaid records** | P3/P1 |
| 7 | ACLU: consolidation of statutorily siloed records into per-individual dossiers, reaching visa holders and **US citizens** | P5 |
| 8 | **DHS Inspector General:** CBP, ICE and Secret Service did not adhere to privacy policies before procuring commercial telemetry data | P2 |
| 9 | Goldman/Wyden/Velázquez oversight letter, 14 April 2026 — 11 requests | P2 |
| 10 | **No published error rate, no independent audit, no pre-deployment congressional debate** | P2 (negative) |
| 11 | Thirteen former Palantir employees publicly condemned the ICE work | P3 |
| 12 | OMB Federal CIO Gregory Barbaccia is a former Palantir employee | P2 |

### The defense case, stated at its strongest

*Palantir is a data processor. Agencies own their data, set their own access rules, and remain the responsible party under the Privacy Act — the vendor supplies software. Sole-source is lawful and routine where switching costs are genuinely prohibitive, and twelve years of integration makes that real, not pretextual. Every award was made by government officials under existing procurement law. Error rates in law-enforcement tooling are not customarily published because publication is itself an operational risk. And Congress has appropriated these funds repeatedly with full knowledge.*

### Knockdown

**On "data processor":** accurate as a role description, irrelevant as a limit on effect. The Privacy Act's concern is the *architecture* of aggregation, not the identity of the record's owner. A system that renders siloed records jointly queryable defeats the statutory purpose regardless of who holds title. The defense answers a question the statute does not ask.

**On sole-source:** this is the count's strongest brick. The justification is that no competitor can perform because the incumbent built the system the new work extends. That is bootstrapping — a recognized procurement pathology in which each award manufactures the justification for the next. Twelve years of it produces a vendor that cannot be dislodged by any process short of a decision to stop.

**On error rates:** the operational-risk argument would carry if the burden fell on the agency. It falls on people wrongly matched, who have no notice, no appeal and no knowledge the system touched them. Element 5 is met by the absence itself.

**On congressional knowledge:** the House learned about ImmigrationOS from procurement records, not from a briefing. Members are currently demanding the documents. "Congress knew" is not available as a defense while Congress is formally asking what happened.

**On Barbaccia — DO NOT OVERCHARGE.** The House Judiciary letter states it is *unclear whether* he played any role. It does not allege that he did. Plead this as an unresolved disclosure question, not as influence. Charging it harder than the source supports is how the whole count gets discredited.

### Verdict

**TRUE BILL** on elements 1–5.

**What flips it:** a competitive re-solicitation, published ImmigrationOS accuracy metrics, an independent audit, or OMB recusal records resolving element 12.

# COUNT IV — Undisclosed contact between overseers and contract holders

**Party:** Specific public officials attending Dialog convenings
**Framework:** Congressional and executive-branch ethics disclosure and recusal rules

### Elements

1. Official holding oversight, appropriations or command authority
2. Undisclosed off-record contact with a regulated or contracted party
3. **Subsequent official action materially touching that party**

### Bricks

Elements 1 and 2 are established: the verified 2026 registration list (222 registrants, 87 first-time; 12–16 August, Powerscourt) places named sitting officials and a serving combatant commander in a closed convening with executives of surveillance, data-broker and advertising-data firms, under explicit non-disclosure expectations.

**Element 3 is missing.** No documented instance of a specific official taking a specific action benefiting a specific co-attendee's firm.

### Verdict

## NO BILL.

Attendance is not an offense. Proximity is not an element. Without element 3 this is a *conflict-of-interest posture*, not a chargeable conflict — and charging it anyway is precisely the move that lets an opponent discredit Counts I–III by association.

**What would return a true bill:** any documented pairing of (a) a named attendee official and (b) a vote, appropriation, contract approval, waiver or command decision touching a co-attendee's firm within a defined window. That is a records exercise — voting records, contract award dates, roster dates — and it is genuinely doable.

**Scope discipline, non-negotiable:** restrict to public officials and executives of contract-holding firms. The leak exposed personal information private attendees were assured would stay private. Republishing it converts an accountability finding into a privacy harm and hands the defense a sympathetic victim.

# COUNT V — Retroactive pricing as an entry barrier

**Party:** none identified
**Verdict:** **NO BILL — WRONG INSTRUMENT.**

The *Bartz* settlement (~$1.5B final approval ~20 July 2026; ~$3,000 per work across ~500,000 works) does function as an incumbent moat: firms that consumed the corpus at zero cost now face a priced input that every entrant must license. But no party breached a duty by settling a case, and the court's holding — that training on lawful copies is fair use and that authors hold no entitlement to training-licensing revenue — is adverse to the creator-standing theory rather than supportive of it.

This is a **competition and copyright-policy brief**, not a charge. File it as such. Trying to force it into an indictment is the single fastest way to look like you are reasoning backward from a conclusion.

# The closing you can actually deliver

> The prosecution does not allege a conspiracy, and does not need one.

> It alleges that a charitable board holds an undefined power it cannot be made to explain and can keep after selling everything it owns. That a fiduciary with no stake in the company he runs holds two billion dollars in the companies it buys from, and the document that would clear him is in his own filing cabinet. That a vendor's twelve-year incumbency has become the legal reason no one else may compete for the contract to merge the records Congress spent fifty years keeping apart — with no published error rate, borne by people who will never know the system touched them.

> Each of these is individually lawful, or arguably so. Each was individually disclosed to someone, somewhere, in a filing nobody was expected to read.

> The question is not who was in the room. It is why, in every instance, the room holds the authority to define the terms under which everyone outside it is permitted to refuse.
