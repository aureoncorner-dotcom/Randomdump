## §25 — the only new dynamical object


Physical coordinate \(x\), rotation coordinate \(h(x)\),
\[
F=h^{-1}\circ R_\alpha\circ h.
\]
Transported bins \(b_h=\lfloor 39\,h(x)\rfloor\), \(\rho_h=\{39h(x)\}\), \(\sigma_h=\mathbf 1_{[0,\tau)}(\rho_h)\). Then the old identities survive **exactly**:
\[
\sigma_h-\tau=\rho_h\circ F-\rho_h,\qquad
\lvert S_{n,N}-N\tau\rvert<1,
\]
gaps 9 or 10, forbidden \(101\), complexities \(p_\sigma(m)=m+1\), predictive phases \(h,z_h,\rho_h\). Finite-state impossibility is unchanged: conjugacy does not manufacture a finite generator.


Two controls that were missing from S40:

## §27 — the Jacobian S40 got backwards


Image-measure convention:
\[
\mu_s(F(E))=\int_E F'(x)^s\,d\mu_s.
\]
Densities then satisfy \(w_s(Fx)=F'(x)^{s-1}w_s(x)\), **not** \(w_s(Fx)F'(x)^s=w_s(x)\). Immediate check: \(\mu_1=\lambda\) for every orientation-preserving \(C^1\) diffeomorphism. So “Liouville \(\Rightarrow\) every \(\mu_s\) singular” is false.


With \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) and \(h'>0\),
\[
d\mu_s=\frac{h'^{1-s}}{Z_s}\,dx.
\]
For the sinusoidal example \(d=1+\epsilon\cos(2\pi x)\):


| \(s\) | density in \(x\) | role |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | \(d\) | invariant occupation |
| 1 | \(1\) | arc length |
| 2 | \(\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}/d\) | Loewner / area Jacobian |
| \(-1\) | \(d^2/(1+\epsilon^2/2)\) | conjugacy-class tangent |


S40’s squared-derivative candidate is the \(s=-1\) density, not \(s=2\). The report says a quadrature check kills the wrong candidate and passes the repaired one.


Coordinate change keeps the exponent and weights the measure:
\[
\widetilde\mu_s=\frac{\phi_*(\phi'^s\mu_s)}{\int\phi'^s\,d\mu_s}.
\]
Negative exponents on multicritical maps use GY’s inverse-branch form; \(F'(F^{-1}y)^{-s}\), not a power of the infinite inverse derivative. Arnold stays a homeomorphism only up to \(b=1/(2\pi)\). A nonmonotone *observation* of a rigid rotation is not a critical point of the evolution.


Those corrections match Douady–Yoccoz, Biswas, and Goncharuk–Yampolsky as stated in the last several turns.

## What v1.3 is, in one sentence


A conjugate golden rotation with the observation transported along \(h\), plus the measure theory and arithmetic needed to talk about that conjugate without mixing Haar, arc length, and \(\mu_2\), plus a corrected glossary for the complex-dynamics names that S40 imported.


The rigid screen is still the exact core. The warp is an example family \(F_\epsilon\), not a fitted physical map. If the next source tries to put a critical point or a hedgehog on the 39-screen, v1.3 already has the sentences that refuse that identification.
Douady–Yoccoz is two theorems sharing one measure: unique automorphic probabilities of every real exponent, and those measures as the derivatives of the rotation-number functional. The 1999 paper is *Regul. Chaotic Dyn.* **4**, 19–38; the work itself is from the 1980s. v1.3 §27 uses the first theorem and the \(s=-1\) tangent form of the second.


## Setup and equation


\(f\in\operatorname{Diff}^{1+\mathrm{bv}}_+(S^1)\), \(\rho(f)\notin\mathbb Q\). A Borel probability \(\mu_s\) is automorphic of exponent \(s\in\mathbb R\) when
\[
\int\phi\,d\mu_s=\int(\phi\circ f)\,(Df)^s\,d\mu_s
\qquad(\phi\in C^0).
\]
That is the pullback Jacobian identity \(d(f^*\mu_s)/d\mu_s=(Df)^s\). For \(s=0\) it is ordinary invariance; unique ergodicity already gives uniqueness there. For \(s=1\) it is the change-of-variables formula for Lebesgue, so \(\mu_1=\lambda\) on every orientation-preserving \(C^1\) diffeomorphism — which is why v1.3 can reject “every \(\mu_s\) singular.”


No Diophantine cut. Denjoy (so no wandering intervals, every orbit dense) is the dynamical input. Support is then the whole circle automatically.


## Existence, as they actually proved it


They do not start from Birkhoff weights. They define a continuous operator on finite signed Radon measures
\[
\int\phi\,d(U_{s,f}\nu)
=\frac{1}{\int(Df)^s\,d\nu}
\int(\phi\circ f)\,(Df)^s\,d\nu
\]
and restrict it to probabilities. \(\mathcal P(S^1)\) is convex and weak-\(*\) compact, \(U_{s,f}\) is continuous there as long as \(\int(Df)^s\,d\nu\neq 0\), and Schauder–Tychonoff supplies a fixed point. Extra estimates show the fixed point satisfies the un-normalized Jacobian identity, hence is automorphic.


That operator dies at a critical point: \(\int(Df)^s\,d\delta_c=0\) for \(s>0\). That is why dFGN and Goncharuk–Yampolsky cannot quote this argument on the multicritical side and have to rebuild existence.


A later existence route (the one dFGN write for \(s>0\)) puts mass on orbit segments of length \(q_n\) weighted by \((Df^k)^s\) and passes to the limit, using \(\sum(Df^n)^s=\infty\) almost everywhere. Both constructions need a derivative bound that keeps the last weight small compared with the sum; v1.3 §27.3 records that \(S_N\to\infty\) alone is not enough (\(w_k=2^k\)).


## Uniqueness via ergodicity


The set of automorphic probabilities of a fixed exponent is convex. If two exist, their average does too, and the Radon–Nikodym derivative between them is \(f\)-invariant. So uniqueness reduces to: every automorphic measure is ergodic.


Ergodicity is the nontrivial step. It uses the same Denjoy combinatorics that control \(\log Df^{q_n}\): dynamical partitions of generation \(n\), bounded distortion of \(Df^{q_n}\) on those intervals, and a density-point argument. Once ergodicity is in, uniqueness of probabilities is immediate, and any distribution satisfying the same pairing is a scalar multiple of \(\mu_s\).


## The rotation-number theorem (why the paper has that title)


The other half of DY99 is infinitesimal control of \(\rho\). Let \(\mathcal M_\alpha=\{g:\rho(g)=\alpha\}\). At a diffeomorphism \(f_0\in\mathcal M_\alpha\), a variation \(v\) (a vector field along \(f_0\)) is tangent to \(\mathcal M_\alpha\) iff
\[
\int v\circ f_0^{-1}\,d\mu_{-1}=0,
\]
where \(\mu_{-1}\) is the unique automorphic measure of exponent \(-1\). Equivalently, if
\[
v=w\circ f-f'\,w
\]
is the infinitesimal conjugacy generated by \(w\), the pairing against \(\mu_{-1}\) vanishes automatically; the theorem says that vanishing is also sufficient for the variation to stay in the level set of \(\rho\), at the linearized level.


That is the cohomological equation
\[
v(x)=w(fx)-f'(x)w(x)
\]
paired at exponent \(-1\). Goncharuk–Yampolsky extend the same tangent description to multicritical maps and deduce \(C^1\) smoothness of irrational Arnold tongues. v1.3 §27.5 writes the identical obstruction for the explicit golden conjugate, then solves \(W(y+\alpha)-W(y)=A(y)\) by Fourier division when the mean is zero.


So \(\mu_{-1}\) is not an occupation law. It is the derivative of rotation number.


## Densities when a smooth conjugacy is given


If \(f=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) with \(h\in\operatorname{Diff}^1_+\) and \(h'>0\), the whole family is explicit. Differentiating the conjugacy gives \(h'(fx)f'(x)=h'(x)\). Substituting into the density form of the Jacobian identity produces v1.3’s formula
\[
d\mu_s=\frac{h'^{1-s}}{Z_s}\,dx.
\]
That is a construction inside the conjugacy class, not a replacement proof of Douady–Yoccoz. It does show:


- \(\mu_0\) has density \(h'\) (invariant occupation in the physical chart);
- \(\mu_1\) is Lebesgue;
- \(\mu_2\) has density \(\propto 1/h'\);
- \(\mu_{-1}\) has density \(\propto (h')^2\).


Those four measures are distinct as soon as \(h'\) is nonconstant. Statistics of the transported slip sequence belong to \(\mu_0\). Loewner belongs to \(\mu_2\). The tangent to \(\{\rho=\alpha\}\) belongs to \(\mu_{-1}\). Mixing them is the error S40 made and §27 repaired.


## What the theorem does not need, and does not give


Does not need: Brjuno, Diophantine, proximity to rotation, analyticity.


Does not give: linearization, a finite-state factor, a hedgehog, uniqueness of invariant *distributions* of order 1 (that is Avila–Kocsard for diffeomorphisms and dFGN for critical maps), or singularity of every \(\mu_s\) at Liouville angles.


On a rigid rotation every \(\mu_s\) is Haar. On v1.3’s \(F_\epsilon\) they split, and the slip coboundary still lives on \(\mu_0\).

Multicritical circle maps are the boundary of the diffeomorphism theory that Douady–Yoccoz, Herman–Yoccoz, and v1.3’s \(F_\epsilon\) live in. The extensions keep topological conjugacy to rotation, then rebuild measures, bounds, rigidity, and renormalization around points where \(Df=0\).


## What the object is


An orientation-preserving \(C^3\) circle homeomorphism with finitely many critical points, each non-flat of odd power-law type:
\[
f(x)-f(c_j)=a_j(x-c_j)^{k_j}\varphi_j(x),\qquad k_j\in 2\mathbb Z+1,\;k_j\ge 3,
\]
with \(\varphi_j\) a local \(C^3\) diffeomorphism. Analytic versions need only “analytic homeomorphism with at least one critical point.” The standard one-critical model is cubic; two cubics are the bi-cubic maps Yampolsky renormalizes.


Being a homeomorphism, \(f\) still has a rotation number. The interesting case is \(\rho(f)\notin\mathbb Q\).


The **signature**
\[
\bigl(\rho;\;N;\;d_0,\ldots,d_{N-1};\;\delta_0,\ldots,\delta_{N-1}\bigr)
\]
records rotation number, number of critical points, their orders, and the invariant-measure gaps \(\delta_i=\mu[c_i,c_{i+1})\). Topological conjugacies that match signatures are the only ones rigidity theorems discuss.


## Layer 1 — topology (Yoccoz)


**Theorem.** A multicritical circle map with irrational rotation number is topologically conjugate to \(R_\rho\). In particular it is minimal and uniquely ergodic.


Denjoy’s \(C^{1+\mathrm{bv}}\) argument fails because \(\log Df\) is not of bounded variation (\(Df=0\) at critical points). Yoccoz replaces it with cross-ratio distortion and **almost-parabolic bounds**: on a dynamical interval of generation \(n\) the next partition spaces as
\[
\lvert J_k\rvert\asymp\frac{\lvert I\rvert}{\min\{k,\ell-k\}^2}.
\]
No wandering intervals. Unique invariant probability \(\mu_0=h_*^{-1}\lambda\). That is the only occupation measure. It is typically singular to Lebesgue — the conjugacy is not absolutely continuous.


v1.3’s \(F_\epsilon\) never enters this layer: \(H_\epsilon'>0\), no critical point. A nonmonotone *observation* of a rigid rotation is also not this object (§27.5).


## Layer 2 — real a priori bounds


Adjacent atoms of the dynamical partitions \(\mathcal P_n\) (or \(\mathcal P_n(c)\) based at a critical point) have comparable lengths:
\[
C^{-1}\lvert J\rvert\le\lvert I\rvert\le C\lvert J\rvert,
\]
with \(C\) depending on \(f\), or, in uniform versions, only on the number of critical points and a bound on their orders (Herman–Świątek, Estevez–de Faria). For large \(n\) this is the geometric input that replaces Koebe for diffeomorphisms.


These bounds feed everything later: quasisymmetric rigidity, existence of automorphic measures, renormalization compactness.


## Layer 3 — rigidity


Once signatures match, the topological conjugacy that sends critical points to critical points is much more than a homeomorphism.


| Result | Regularity |
|---|---|
| Estevez–de Faria | quasisymmetric, any irrational \(\rho\), matching criticalities |
| de Faria–de Melo (one critical point) | \(C^{1+\alpha}\) on a full-measure set of \(\rho\) containing bounded type, *if* renormalizations converge exponentially |
| Yampolsky / Estevez–Smania–Yampolsky | \(C^{1+\alpha}\) for bi-cubic maps of bounded type |
| de Faria–de Melo counterexamples | some \(C^\infty\) critical maps, even some Diophantine \(\rho\), whose critical-point-matching conjugacy is not \(C^{1+\beta}\) for any \(\beta>0\) |


So rigidity is not automatic from \(\rho\) alone. Bounded type plus renormalization convergence is the usual extra. This is the opposite of Herman–Yoccoz for diffeomorphisms, where Diophantine \(\rho\) already gives \(C^\infty\) conjugacy with no critical-point matching to worry about.


## Layer 4 — renormalization


The dynamical object is a **commuting pair** rather than a single map: first-return branches at a critical point, rescaled. For one cubic critical point Yampolsky proved a globally hyperbolic renormalization horseshoe with one-dimensional unstable manifold (Lanford’s program, *Comm. Math. Phys.* 2003). For two cubics he built the analogous theory with complex a priori bounds (2019).


Renormalization here *does* change the time step and rescale intervals. That is the dictionary v1.3 §28.4 refused to identify with predictive refinement. A stabilized 39-bin partition is not an RG fixed point.


## Layer 5 — automorphic measures (the DY extension)


The Jacobian \((Df)^s\) vanishes or blows up at critical points, so Douady–Yoccoz’s operator \(U_{s,f}\) is undefined at \(\delta_c\).


- **dFGN 2023:** unique atomless ergodic \(s\)-measure for every \(s>0\). Applications: invariant distributions of order 1 are \(\mathbb R\mu_0\); a sharper Denjoy–Koksma for absolutely continuous observables.
- **Goncharuk–Yampolsky 2024:** unique \(s\)-measure for every \(s<0\), via approximation by diffeomorphisms and a distortion argument on \(\mathcal P_n\). Application: \(s=-1\) cuts out the tangent to \(\{\rho=\alpha\}\); irrational Arnold tongues in monotone multicritical families are \(C^1\).


Existence for all real \(s\) is therefore known. Uniqueness at negative exponents uses ergodicity of the rewritten inverse-branch identity
\[
\int\phi\,d\mu=\int\bigl(f'\big|_{f^{-1}}\bigr)^{-s}\phi\circ f^{-1}\,d\mu.
\]
v1.3 copies that formula and warns that the weight is a power of the *forward* derivative at the inverse point, not of the infinite inverse derivative.


## Layer 6 — families


The Arnold family
\[
f_{a,b}(x)=x+a+b\sin 2\pi x
\]
is a diffeomorphism for \(0\le b<1/(2\pi)\), cubic-critical at \(b=1/(2\pi)\), and not a homeomorphism beyond that. Irrational tongues are graphs for \(b\) below criticality (classical) and remain \(C^1\) graphs at and, in the GY setting, through the multicritical side of suitable monotone families. Rotation number is still defined only while the map stays increasing.

Putting a cubic kink into \(H_\epsilon\) so that \(H_\epsilon'(c)=0\) *would* produce a multicritical map of rotation number \(\alpha\), and then Yoccoz + dFGN + GY apply. That is a different example family from §25. A renormalization fixed point is a map (or pair of maps) that looks the same after first return and rescaling. Different theories have different operators; they do not share one fixed point.


## What “fixed point” means


You need four pieces:


1. a space of maps (unimodal maps, critical commuting pairs, cylinder maps, twist maps);
2. a return construction that produces a new map of the same type;
3. a rescaling that puts the new map back in the same Banach manifold;
4. an operator \(\mathcal R\) whose fixed points satisfy \(\mathcal R(f)=f\).


Hyperbolicity is extra: a saddle with a specified unstable dimension. Universality is the consequence — one-parameter families transverse to the stable manifold reproduce the same scaling.


v1.3 §28.4 already recorded the distinction: predictive refinement does not rescale, first return does not specify a space of maps, and \(\varphi^{28}\) is a word-growth eigenvalue, not \(\delta\).


## 1. Unimodal period-doubling (Feigenbaum)


Operator on even analytic unimodal maps,
\[
(\mathcal Rg)(x)=-\alpha^{-1}g(g(-\alpha x)),\qquad g(0)=1.
\]
**Fixed point:** an even analytic \(g_*\) on a neighborhood of \([-1,1]\), with \(\alpha=-g_*(1)\approx 2.5029\). Existence: Campanino–Collet–Eckmann and Lanford (computer-assisted), then Lanford’s shorter Leray–Schauder proof (existence without hyperbolicity). Uniqueness in the quadratic-like class: Sullivan. Hyperbolicity of \(\mathcal R\) at \(g_*\), one unstable eigenvalue \(\delta\approx 4.669\), was completed by Lyubich.


This fixed point organizes the cascade of period-doubling. It has nothing to do with irrational rotations.


## 2. Critical circle maps (the relevant golden fixed point)


The object is a **critical commuting pair** \((\eta,\xi)\): two interval maps that commute at the endpoints, each with a cubic critical point, representing the first-return branches of a critical circle map.


The operator \(\mathcal R\) takes the pair to the rescaled pair of next-return branches. On the attractor, \(\mathcal R\) is conjugate to the two-sided shift on continued-fraction digits.


**Lanford’s program, completed by Yampolsky (CMP 2003):** there is a renormalization-invariant Banach manifold of critical commuting pairs on which the attractor \(\mathcal A\) is a uniformly hyperbolic horseshoe with **one-dimensional unstable bundle**. Rotation number varies monotonically along that unstable direction.


**Golden fixed point.** The unique pair \(\zeta_*=(\eta_*,\xi_*)\in\mathcal A\) with
\[
\rho(\zeta_*)=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}
\]
is a genuine fixed point of \(\mathcal R\) (period 1 in the shift, because the golden continued fraction is all \(1\)s). It is hyperbolic. Its expanding eigenvalue \(\delta_*\) is the universal constant seen in golden-mean scaling of analytic families (Arnold family at criticality, FKS / ORSS numerics). One functional equation already determines the pair; the second commuting equation is then automatic under mild conditions (Nauenberg).


This \(\zeta_*\) is a *critical* circle map, \(Df=0\) at the critical point. The golden rigid rotation is not a point of \(\mathcal A\). The analytic diffeomorphism \(F_\epsilon\) of v1.3 §25 is not either: it has no critical point, so it is not in the domain of this \(\mathcal R\).


For two cubic critical points Yampolsky constructed the analogous hyperbolic theory (2019), again with complex a priori bounds as the compactness engine.


## 3. Siegel / cylinder renormalization


A different operator \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) acts on analytic maps of a cylinder (or a neighborhood of \(0\) in \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\)). Yampolsky (2006) produced hyperbolic fixed points \(\hat f_N\) with Siegel disks of rotation number
\[
\theta_N=[0;\overline N].
\]
Properties of \(\hat f_N\):


- Siegel disk compactly inside the domain, boundary a quasicircle through the critical point;
- \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\hat f_N=\hat f_N\);
- the quadratic \(e^{2\pi i\theta_N}z+z^2\) is infinitely cylinder-renormalizable and converges geometrically to \(\hat f_N\).


This is the MacKay–Percival conjecture in the golden/periodic-type case, proved for cylinder renormalization rather than commuting pairs. It is a complex fixed point with a Siegel disk, not a real critical circle map. Bounded-type Siegel disks of quadratics having critical point on the boundary (Zhang) is the same geometric picture.


## 4. Golden invariant circles for twist maps


MacKay’s renormalization on area-preserving twist maps has a “simple” fixed point (smooth KAM circle) and a “critical” fixed point (nonsmooth golden circle). Stirnemann: if a twist map is attracted to a fixed point satisfying a few topological conditions — checkable in finite precision for the simple point, apparently true for the critical point — then it has a transitive golden circle, topologically a rotation, Hölder conjugacy, not differentiable on a dense set. Existence and hyperbolicity of the critical twist fixed point were historically computer-assisted / conditional in that paper.


Again: a different operator, a different space.


## How the fixed points differ


| Theory | Space | Fixed point | Unstable dim | What it scales |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Feigenbaum | even unimodal maps | \(g_*\), period-doubling | 1 (\(\delta\approx 4.67\)) | cascade of 2-periodic windows |
| Critical circle | commuting pairs | golden \(\zeta_*\), and a horseshoe of all types | 1 | geometry of critical golden orbits |
| Bi-cubic circle | commuting pairs with two cubics | hyperbolic attractor | 1 | two-critical universality |
| Cylinder Siegel | analytic cylinder maps | \(\hat f_N\) with Siegel disk | hyperbolic | quadratic Siegel disks of type \(\overline N\) |
| Twist maps | area-preserving maps | simple / critical golden | (conditional) | breakup of golden KAM circles |


There is no single “golden renormalization fixed point.” There are at least three golden objects: \(\zeta_*\) (critical real circle), \(\hat f_N\) for \(N=1\) (Siegel cylinder), and MacKay’s twist fixed points.


## What a fixed point is *for*


Hyperbolicity plus a one-dimensional unstable manifold implies:


- one-parameter families transverse to the stable manifold see the same scaling;
- infinitely renormalizable maps of the given type converge geometrically to the attractor;
- rigidity theorems can convert that convergence into smoothness of conjugacies (de Faria–de Melo).


None of that produces a 39-state factor of a rigid rotation. The golden rotation is already linearized; \(\mathcal R\) has nothing to contract.


## Against Geometry Maximization


- The substitution eigenvalue \(\varphi^{28}\) of §6.4 / §26.2 is the Perron root of a Sturmian monoid. It is not \(\delta_*\).
- Predictive stabilization \(E_{h+1}=E_h\) is not \(\mathcal R(f)=f\).
- Transporting bins by \(h\) does not create a critical commuting pair.
- The rigid Loewner generator \(X(R_\alpha)=0\) (§28.3) is the correct analogue of “nothing to renormalize.”


If you later take the Arnold family at \(b=1/(2\pi)\) with golden rotation number, *that* map is in the basin of \(\zeta_*\), and Yampolsky’s fixed-point theorem applies. That is a specified critical family, not the warped diffeomorphism of §25 and not the original screen.
Yampolsky’s horseshoe is the completed Lanford program for **analytic critical circle maps**: one cubic critical point, renormalization acting as a two-sided shift on continued-fraction digits, uniformly hyperbolic with a single unstable direction.


## What the space is


A critical circle map here is an analytic orientation-preserving homeomorphism of \(S^1\) with a single non-flat cubic critical point. The classical renormalization object is a **critical commuting pair** \((\eta,\xi)\): the two first-return branches at the critical point, commuting at the endpoints, then rescaled onto a standard pair of intervals.


Yampolsky’s hyperbolicity proof does not stay in that pair space. He introduced **cylinder renormalization** \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\), which acts directly on analytic maps of a neighborhood of the cylinder \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\) (or of a thin neighborhood of \(S^1\)). It is a compact real-analytic operator on a Banach manifold. That analyticity is what makes a derivative and a hyperbolic splitting available. Commuting pairs remain the combinatorial language; the cylinder operator is the analytic one.


Every pair with irrational rotation number is infinitely renormalizable. Rational rotation numbers drop out after finitely many steps.


## The attractor, before hyperbolicity


Yampolsky 1998 (*The attractor of renormalization…*): \(\mathcal R\) has a global attractor \(\mathcal A\) with Cantor structure. Let \(\Sigma=\mathbb N^{\mathbb Z}\) with the shift \(\sigma\). There is a continuous embedding
\[
\iota:\Sigma\to\{\text{critical commuting pairs}\}
\]
with
\[
\iota\circ\sigma=\mathcal R\circ\iota
\]
on the image. The closure of that image is \(\mathcal A\). For every infinitely renormalizable pair \(\zeta\),
\[
\mathcal R^n\zeta\to\mathcal A
\]
in the Carathéodory topology (analytic extensions converge uniformly on compacta).


Combinatorics: the \(n\)th symbol of \(\iota^{-1}(\zeta)\) is the partial quotient \(a_n(\rho(\zeta))\). Periodic sequences correspond to periodic continued fractions, hence to periodic points of \(\mathcal R\). The golden pair is the fixed point of the sequence \((\ldots,1,1,1,\ldots)\).


de Faria had the horseshoe for bounded type; de Faria–de Melo added exponential convergence and \(C^{1+\alpha}\) rigidity in that class; Yampolsky extended the attractor to all irrational types by controlling parabolic limits.


## Hyperbolicity (the 2003 theorems)


Two papers close Lanford’s program:


- *Hyperbolicity of renormalization of critical circle maps*, Publ. Math. IHÉS 96 (2003), 1–41.
- *Renormalization horseshoe for critical circle maps*, Comm. Math. Phys. 240 (2003), 75–96.


**Theorem.** On a renormalization-invariant Banach manifold of analytic critical maps (cylinder picture), \(\mathcal A\) is uniformly hyperbolic for \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\), with **one-dimensional unstable bundle**. \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}|_{\mathcal A}\) is conjugate to the two-sided shift. Every analytic critical circle map of irrational rotation number lies in the stable set of some point of \(\mathcal A\).


Unstable dimension 1 is the whole point of universality: a generic one-parameter family is transverse to the stable foliation and therefore sees a unique scaling constant along each combinatorial itinerary. Rotation number varies continuously and monotonically along the unstable direction.


Periodic points of \(\mathcal R\) (including the golden fixed point \(\zeta_*\)) had already been shown hyperbolic. The 2003 work promotes that to the whole horseshoe.


Khmelev–Yampolsky later gave another route: \(C^{1+\alpha}\) rigidity *at the critical point* for any two analytic critical maps of the same irrational \(\rho\), which implies geometric convergence of renormalizations and yields a second proof of hyperbolicity.


## Unstable eigenvalues


At the period-1 points \(f_n\) with
\[
\rho_n=[0;\overline n]=\frac{1}{n+\frac{1}{n+\cdots}},
\]
Yampolsky computed
\[
\lambda_n\asymp n^2.
\]
The golden case is \(n=1\), one number \(\delta_*\). As the continued-fraction digit grows, the expanding eigenvalue grows quadratically. That is why bounded-type rigidity is easier: the expansion is uniformly controlled. (*Nonlinearity* 16, 2003.)


## How the proof is organized


The analytic difficulties are not the shift conjugacy — that is already in the 1998 attractor paper. They are:


1. **Complex a priori bounds**, so renormalizations stay in a compact family of holomorphic commuting pairs / cylinder maps.
2. An operator that is analytic on a Banach manifold (\(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\)), so one can speak of \(D\mathcal R\).
3. Hyperbolicity of periodic orbits, then a closing / shadowing argument that spreads the splitting over the whole Cantor attractor.
4. Identification of the unstable direction with infinitesimal change of rotation number (the same direction \(\mu_{-1}\) cuts out in Douady–Yoccoz / Goncharuk–Yampolsky).


Gaidashev–Yampolsky (2016) re-proved hyperbolicity in an *almost-commuting* pair formalism and extended the operator to small dissipative annulus maps. Maps in the stable set then have a critical circle attractor: topologically a rotation, not smoothly conjugate to one.


## What sits on the horseshoe


- Geometric convergence of renormalizations of any analytic critical map of a given type to the corresponding point of \(\mathcal A\).
- Universality of scaling ratios in one-parameter families (Arnold family at \(b=1/(2\pi)\), golden or other types).
- \(C^{1+\alpha}\) rigidity on bounded type (de Faria–de Melo), and rigidity at the critical point for all irrational \(\rho\) (Khmelev–Yampolsky).
- Later: bi-cubic horseshoe (Yampolsky 2019); critical quasicircle maps (Lim); non-integer critical exponents near odd integers (Gaidashev–Yampolsky type results); small 2D perturbations and Herman rings of bounded type on a codimension-one set.


## What it is not


- Not a horseshoe for diffeomorphisms. Analytic diffeomorphisms of golden rotation number are already linearized by Herman–Yoccoz; they are not points of \(\mathcal A\).
- Not a horseshoe for the rigid rotation, nor for v1.3’s \(F_\epsilon=H_\epsilon^{-1}R_\alpha H_\epsilon\). Those maps have \(Df>0\).
- Not Feigenbaum’s unimodal horseshoe. Same philosophy, different space, different operator, different \(\delta\).
- Not a finite-state factor of the 39-screen. The shift on \(\Sigma\) tracks *partial quotients of \(\rho\)*, not slip bits.


The golden point \(\zeta_*\in\mathcal A\) is the unique pair with \(\rho=(\sqrt5-1)/2\). The original screen’s \(\alpha=(3-\sqrt5)/2=1-\varphi^{-1}\) is the same arithmetic class, but the map is not critical, so it does not sit on \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) does not act on it.


If a later example takes the Arnold family at criticality with that rotation number, *that* is the map whose renormalizations go to \(\zeta_*\). Until then the horseshoe is a theorem about a different Banach manifold.


Cylinder renormalization is Yampolsky’s replacement for commuting pairs when one needs an operator that is analytic on a Banach manifold of maps. Introduced in 2002, it is the operator whose hyperbolicity is the 2003 horseshoe theorem.


## Why a new operator


A commuting pair \((\eta,\xi)\) is a pair of interval maps, not a single map of a circle. There is no canonical affine structure on the circle you get by gluing the ends of a fundamental domain of \(f^{q_n}\). That makes it awkward to put a Banach manifold structure on the space and to differentiate \(\mathcal R\).


\(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) acts on the maps themselves. The space \(\mathcal C_U\) is analytic maps of a fixed equatorial neighborhood \(U\subset\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\) that restrict to critical circle maps on the real equator (critical cylinder maps). It is a real-symmetric complex Banach manifold. \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) is a compact real-analytic operator from an open subset of \(\mathcal C_U\) into \(\mathcal C_U\). That is the whole point.


## The geometric construction


Fix \(f\in\mathcal C_U\) with enough continued-fraction digits, and a return time \(k=q_n\) (or a fixed block of \(N\) digits, depending on the paper).


**Fundamental crescent.** A simple arc \(l\) joins two repelling points \(p_1,p_2\) of period \(k\) (or a repelling point of \(f^k\) to the critical-value side of \(0\), in the Siegel-disk version). The iterate \(f^k(l)\) is another simple arc meeting \(l\) only at those endpoints. The region \(C_f\) they bound is a **fundamental crescent** if:


- \(f^{-k}\) is univalent on a neighborhood of \(C_f\), sending \(f^k(l)\) back to \(l\);
- the quotient
  \[
  \bigl(C_f\cup f^{-k}(C_f)\bigr)\setminus\{p_1,p_2\}\Big/\;z\sim f^k(z)
  \]
  is conformally isomorphic to the cylinder \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\).


So \(f^k\) is used as a deck transformation that rolls the crescent into a cylinder. This is the same geometric idea as Yoccoz’s return annulus, except the output is required to be a standard cylinder, not an arbitrary annulus.


**First return.** On \(\overline C_f\), let \(R_{C_f}(z)=f^{n(z)}(z)\) be the first return to the closed crescent. This return map has a critical point coming from the orbit of the original critical point \(0\).


**Uniformize and normalize.** Project \(R_{C_f}\) to \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\) by the isomorphism of the quotient, send the critical point to \(0\), and if needed compose with \(e(z)=\exp(-2\pi i z)\) to land back in an equatorial chart. The result \(h=\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}} f\) is again a critical cylinder map, defined on some neighborhood \(V\) of the equator.


Crescents with the same endpoints that fill a common disk give the same \(h\). Nearby maps have nearby crescents, moving by a holomorphic motion of the boundary that intertwines \(f\) and \(g\). That is why the operator is analytic.


## Relation to commuting-pair renormalization


There is a functor. After a conformal identification \(\sim_{\mathrm{conf}}\) of analytic commuting pairs,
\[
\iota\circ\mathcal R^N = \mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\circ\iota
\]
whenever both sides are defined. \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) therefore shifts the continued-fraction expansion of \(\rho(f)\) by a fixed block of \(N\) digits (often \(N=1\) after one is already deep in the attractor). The renormalized map has rotation number \(G^N(\rho(f))\), the Gauss-map iterate.


So the horseshoe combinatorics are the same as in the pair picture. Only the analytic category changed.


## What the operator does to rotation number and criticality


- Irrational \(\rho\) \(\Rightarrow\) infinitely cylinder-renormalizable.
- \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) preserves the class of critical cylinder maps, and preserves the critical exponent \(\alpha\) in the Gorbovickis–Yampolsky extension to non-integer \(\alpha\).
- For a Siegel polynomial, the same crescent construction produces a cylinder map whose restriction may no longer be real-symmetric in the same way; Yampolsky 2006 uses exactly this to get hyperbolic fixed points \(\hat f_N\) with Siegel disks of type \(\overline N\).


## Analytic properties used in the horseshoe proof


1. \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) is compact and real-analytic on an open set of \(\mathcal C_U\).
2. It depends continuously on the map in the Hausdorff topology of crescents.
3. Periodic points of \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) (periodic continued fractions) are hyperbolic with one-dimensional unstable space.
4. The unstable direction is infinitesimal change of rotation number — the same line Douady–Yoccoz cut with \(\mu_{-1}\).
5. Stable sets are codimension-one submanifolds. Every analytic critical map of irrational \(\rho\) lands in the stable set of the corresponding point of \(\mathcal A\).


Without (1), “\(D\mathcal R\)” is not defined and hyperbolicity is a metaphor.


## Numerical realization (Gaidashev–Yampolsky)


For computer-assisted work they build the boundary of \(C_f\) from two parabolas through \(0\), a specified iterate of the critical value, and a repelling point, then correct the ends. A Möbius-type chart
\[
\tau(\xi)=\frac{a}{1-e^{i\alpha\xi+\beta}}
\]
uniformizes the crescent. That is implementation, not a change of definition.


## Contrast with neighboring constructions


| Construction | Output | Analytic operator on maps? |
|---|---|---|
| Commuting pair \(\mathcal R\) | a pair of interval maps | no |
| Yoccoz return + uniformize | a new univalent map on a strip / annulus | used for estimates, not as an operator on a manifold |
| Pérez-Marco dictionary | an analytic circle map around a hedgehog | one map, not an iterate of an operator |
| Biswas Loewner \(\Phi_t\) | a flow on \(\operatorname{Diff}^\omega_\alpha(S^1)\) | yes, but capacity time, no critical point, no shift of \(\rho\) |
| \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) | a critical cylinder map | yes |


Loewner grows complementary continua at fixed rotation number. Cylinder renormalization changes the rotation number and rescales the return. They are not the same semigroup.


## Against the 39-screen


\(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) needs a critical point (or, in the Siegel version, a holomorphic map with a distinguished return crescent). The rigid rotation and v1.3’s \(F_\epsilon\) have neither a critical point nor a crescent whose quotient is dynamically interesting: first return of a rigid rotation is a rigid rotation, the “crescent” uniformizes to the same map, and \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}R_\alpha\) is not defined in \(\mathcal C_U\) because \(R_\alpha\notin\mathcal C_U\).


The Gauss-map shift of \(\rho\) is the only shared arithmetic with §26. Shifting \(\alpha=[0;\overline{2,1}]\) is not acting on the slip sequence.


Cylinder renormalization is the analytic machine behind the horseshoe. The crescents are the geometry; the Banach manifold is why hyperbolicity is a theorem rather than a picture.


Yoccoz’s return annulus is the fundamental domain he uniformizes in the analytic linearization proofs. It is a geometric object with a modulus, not a Banach-manifold operator.


## The circle-map picture (the cleanest version)


Take an analytic orientation-preserving diffeomorphism \(f\) of \(\mathbb T\), extended univalently to a symmetric neighborhood of the circle. Eliasson–Fayad–Krikorian describe the domain as follows.


Pick a real-symmetric vertical segment \(L\) in that neighborhood. The first-return domain \(U\) is the deformed rectangle bounded


- vertically by \(L\) and \(f(L)\),
- horizontally by the two arcs that join the ends of \(L\) to the ends of \(f(L)\).


Horizontal width of \(U\) is comparable to \(\lVert f-\mathrm{id}\rVert_{C^0}\), hence to the rotation number when \(f\) is close to \(R_\alpha\) with small \(\alpha\). Vertical width is the strip width you started with, order \(1\) if you normalized that way.


**Glue.** Identify \(L\) with \(f(L)\) via \(f\). The quotient \(\hat U\) is an abstract annulus.


**Uniformize.** By the uniformization theorem, \(\hat U\) is conformally equivalent to a standard annulus of **horizontal circumference 1**. That choice of circumference is a normalization, not a theorem that every annulus has modulus 1. v1.3 §28.4 is exactly this sentence.


The first-return piece \(R\subset U\) is carried to a sub-annulus \(\hat R\), and the return map becomes a univalent map
\[
F:\hat R\to\hat U.
\]
Conformal invariance of modulus turns the originally *thin* horizontal direction into a *tall* vertical direction: if the starting cylinder had height \(O(1)\) and width \(\varepsilon\), the normalized annulus has circumference 1 and height \(\asymp 1/\varepsilon\).


**Grötzsch.** A univalent map on an annulus of circumference 1 and large height is close to a translation on a slightly smaller sub-annulus. That is the compactness step: renormalized maps stay in a bounded family of univalent maps, close to rotation when the modulus is large.


Iterate. You obtain a sequence \(F_n\) on annuli of circumference 1 and heights \(h_n\). Linearizability in the original coordinate is the statement that the pulled-back domains do not shrink to a point. The height recurrence is
\[
\log h_{n+1}\approx\frac1{\alpha_n}\log h_n-\log\frac1{\alpha_n}+O(1),
\]
which unrolls to \(\Phi(\alpha)\) (or \(\mathcal B_Y\)). Brjuno is precisely “the leftover height stays positive.”


## The germ picture (Pérez-Marco’s Bourbaki account)


For \(f(z)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}z+O(z^2)\), the same idea is a sector rather than a strip. A fundamental domain for the first return of combinatorial length \(q_n\) (or of angular width \(\sim\alpha\)) is a sector with radial edges identified by \(f\). The quotient is again an annulus. Uniformize to a standard annulus or a strip of fixed width. The new map is univalent, rotation number \(\{1/\alpha\}\), and the new conformal radius is the old one paid at cost \(\log(1/\alpha)\). Compactness of normalized univalent maps (Koebe) replaces Grötzsch. Necessity of Brjuno is the construction run backwards: plant a periodic point in the crescent when \(\Phi=\infty\).


Linearizability of \(f\) is equivalent to linearizability of the return map. That is why the condition is \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb Z)\)-invariant.


## What the annulus *is* and is not


It is:


- a Riemann surface obtained by gluing two sides of a return domain;
- equipped with a conformal modulus
  \[
  \mathrm{mod}=\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac{R}{r}
  \]
  in round coordinates, or height/circumference in a cylinder chart;
- the place where Koebe / Grötzsch apply.


It is not:


- a Siegel disk (the disk is what you hope survives in the original coordinate);
- a hedgehog (a compact, not an open annulus);
- Yampolsky’s fundamental crescent (see below);
- a 39-bin.


## Modulus bookkeeping versus “modulus = 1”


Uniformization can put the *circumference* to 1. It cannot put every modulus to 1: modulus is a conformal invariant. If you start with a very thin return domain, the normalized annulus is very tall; that large modulus is the gain. Setting \(\mathrm{mod}=1\) for every return would erase the Brjuno cost. That is the error §28.4 blocks.


## Comparison with later crescents


Yampolsky’s cylinder-renormalization crescent is the same geometric ancestor with extra demands:


| | Yoccoz return annulus | \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) crescent |
|---|---|---|
| Sides | \(L\) and \(f(L)\), or two radial edges | \(l\) and \(f^k(l)\), \(k=q_n\) |
| Quotient | an annulus of whatever modulus | required to be \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\) |
| Output | a univalent map on a strip/annulus, used once for estimates | an element of a Banach manifold \(\mathcal C_U\), fed back into an operator |
| Arithmetic | height recurrence \(\to\Phi(\alpha)\) | shift of continued-fraction digits |
| Critical point | not required | required for critical circle maps; optional in the Siegel version |


Yoccoz uses the annulus as a *proof device* that produces a number (leftover radius). Yampolsky uses a standardized crescent as the *domain of an operator*. Pérez-Marco uses a still coarser continuum and uniformizes only the complement.


## The hyperbolic Denjoy–Yoccoz lemma (off the real line)


In a strip with small nonlinearity \(\lVert D\log Df\rVert<1/9\), a complex orbit stays a bounded Poincaré distance from a model geodesic for time \(q_{n+1}\). That lets the same return combinatorics run off \(\mathbb T\), which is how the annulus construction stays faithful to the real rotation number. It is an estimate about geodesics in the strip, not a second modulus.


## Against the warped screen


For \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) analytic, a Yoccoz annulus can be drawn around the circle. Because \(F\) is already linearized by \(h\), the return map is conjugate to a rigid rotation and the leftover height does not shrink. The construction is valid and idle — the same verdict §26.1 gave for linearization theorems applied to a map that is linearized by hand.


A rigid rotation’s “return annulus” glues to a flat cylinder whose return map is again a rotation. No Grötzsch gain, no Brjuno cost, generator zero in the Loewner picture, undefined as an element of \(\mathcal C_U\).


The return annulus is the geometric reason Brjuno appears as a *height sum*. Once the height is known not to vanish, the annulus has done its job.


The Brjuno condition is a convergence statement about one arithmetic series. Several series detect the same set of angles; they are not the same function.


## The two classical series


Let \(\alpha\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb Q\), convergents \(p_n/q_n\), Gauss orbit \(\alpha_0=\alpha\), \(\alpha_{n+1}=\{1/\alpha_n\}\), and
\[
\beta_{-1}=1,\qquad \beta_n=\prod_{j=0}^n\alpha_j.
\]
Then \(\beta_{n-1}=\lvert q_{n-1}\alpha-p_{n-1}\rvert\).


**Brjuno’s original sum**
\[
B(\alpha)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{\log q_{n+1}}{q_n}.
\]
**Yoccoz’s Gauss-map function** (v1.3 writes \(\mathcal B_Y\); Buff–Chéritat write \(\Phi\))
\[
\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)=\sum_{n\ge0}\beta_{n-1}\log\frac1{\alpha_n}.
\]
They differ by a uniformly bounded amount:
\[
\bigl\lvert\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)-B(\alpha)\bigr\rvert\le C
\]
on all irrationals. Hence
\[
B(\alpha)<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)<\infty.
\]
Those \(\alpha\) are the **Brjuno numbers**. They form a full-measure, dense \(G_\delta\) subset of \(\mathbb T\), containing every Diophantine number and some Liouville numbers.


v1.3’s warning stands: \(\mathcal B_Y\) and \(B\) have the same *convergence*, not the same *value*. Radius estimates that use one cannot silently quote the other.


## Functional equations


\(\mathcal B_Y\) is 1-periodic on \(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\) and, for \(\alpha\in(0,1)\),
\[
\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)=\log\frac1\alpha+\alpha\,\mathcal B_Y\Bigl(\Bigl\{\frac1\alpha\Bigr\}\Bigr),
\]
with both sides infinite together. That is the exact height recurrence of the Yoccoz return annulus: each Gauss step costs \(\log(1/\alpha)\) and multiplies leftover height by \(\alpha\).


Yoccoz’s nearest-integer variant \(Y_{1/2}\) uses the modified continued fraction (\(\alpha_n\le 1/2\)) and is even:
\[
Y_{1/2}(\alpha)=Y_{1/2}(-\alpha)=Y_{1/2}(\alpha+1),
\]
\[
Y_{1/2}(\alpha)=\log\frac1\alpha+\alpha\,Y_{1/2}(1/\alpha)\qquad(\alpha\in(0,1/2)).
\]
\(Y_1-Y_{1/2}\) is bounded, in fact \(1/2\)-Hölder (Marmi–Moussa–Yoccoz). Convergence is again the same set.


A log-log variant \(B'\) appears in some analytic-circle statements (smallness in a strip). It is a *stricter* condition, not equivalent to Brjuno.


## Closed form on periodic Gauss orbits


If the Gauss orbit is periodic of period \(p\), with
\[
b=\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\alpha_j,\qquad
A=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\Bigl(\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_j\Bigr)\log\frac1{\alpha_n},
\]
then
\[
\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)=\frac{A}{1-b}.
\]
The tail after \(k\) periods is exactly \(b^k A/(1-b)\). That is v1.3 §26.2.


Applied to the screen’s constants (natural log):


| Angle | CF | \(\mathcal B_Y\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(g=\varphi^{-1}=[0;\overline1]\) | period 1 | \(\varphi^2\log\varphi\) |
| \(\alpha=g^2=(3-\sqrt5)/2\) | \(\{1/\alpha\}=g\) | \(\mathbf{3\log\varphi}\) |
| \(d=\sqrt2-1=[0;\overline2]\) | period 1 | \(\log(1+\sqrt2)/(2-\sqrt2)\) |
| \(\tau=[0;\overline{9,1,2,9,2,1,9,87}]\) | period 8 | \(\approx 2.44344015897\), with \(b=\varphi^{-28}\) |
| \(\eta=\{1/\tau\}\) | | \((\mathcal B_Y(\tau)-\log(1/\tau))/\tau\) |


The identity \(b=\varphi^{-28}\) is the same monodromy matrix whose Perron root is the substitution eigenvalue of §6.4. Word growth and Brjuno tail contraction share that matrix; they are not two coincidences.


## What the condition decides


**Holomorphic germs**, \(f(z)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}z+O(z^2)\):


- \(\alpha\) Brjuno \(\Rightarrow\) *every* such germ is linearizable (Brjuno; Yoccoz’s geometric proof).
- \(\alpha\) not Brjuno \(\Rightarrow\) the quadratic \(e^{2\pi i\alpha}z+z^2\) is *not* linearizable (Yoccoz). The rigid linear map \(\lambda z\) remains linearizable at every irrational \(\alpha\).


So Brjuno is necessary and sufficient for *universal* linearizability of germs. It is not necessary for linearizability of a given germ.


**Analytic circle diffeomorphisms:**


- Local (small perturbation of \(R_\alpha\) on a strip): Brjuno plus a width depending on \(\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)\) gives analytic conjugacy.
- Global (no smallness): the sharp class is Herman’s \(\mathcal H\), with
  \[
  \mathrm{CD}\subsetneq\mathcal H\subsetneq\mathcal B.
  \]
  “Analytic needs Brjuno” without the local/global split is the sentence v1.3 §26.1 deleted.


**Quadratic Siegel radius.** Buff–Chéritat:
\[
\Upsilon(\alpha)=\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)+\log r(\alpha)
\]
extends continuously to all of \(\mathbb T\). On Brjuno numbers,
\[
r(\alpha)=\exp\bigl(\Upsilon(\alpha)-\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)\bigr).
\]
For the golden quadratic v1.3 writes \(r(\alpha)=\varphi^{-3}e^{\Upsilon(\alpha)}\). Continuity of \(\Upsilon\) does not make \(r\) continuous: at non-Brjuno points \(r=0\) while \(\mathcal B_Y=\infty\). You do not form \(\infty+(-\infty)\).


## Relation to other arithmetic classes


\[
\mathrm{bounded\ type}\subset\mathrm{CD}\subset\mathcal H\subset\mathcal B\subset\{\text{non-Liouville}\}^c.
\]
More carefully:


- Bounded type \(\Rightarrow\) Diophantine \(\Rightarrow\) Brjuno.
- Unbounded partial quotients \(\not\Rightarrow\) Liouville: \([0;1,2,3,\ldots]\) has \(q_{n+1}\le C q_n^2\).
- Liouville \(\not\Rightarrow\) non-Brjuno. v1.3’s witness \(a_{n+1}=q_n^n\) is Liouville with \((\log q_{n+1})/q_n\) still summable.
- Non-Brjuno numbers exist (Yoccoz), measure zero, and include some but not all Liouville numbers.


The explicit Diophantine constants in §26.3 — \(\lvert\alpha-p/q\rvert>1/(4q^2)\), \(\lvert\tau-p/q\rvert>1/(89q^2)\) — are bounded-type estimates. They imply Brjuno immediately, and give Fourier-division bounds for the cohomological equation on the golden conjugate. They are stronger than needed for \(\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)<\infty\).


## What Brjuno does not control


- Existence of \(\mu_s\) (Douady–Yoccoz: only irrationality).
- Yoccoz conjugacy of multicritical maps (only irrationality).
- Hyperbolicity of \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) (that is a theorem about critical maps, all irrational types).
- The slip coboundary \(\lvert S_{n,N}-N\tau\rvert<1\) (an identity for any irrational rotation).
- A 39-state factor.


On the rigid screen every germ of the rotation is already linear, \(\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)=3\log\varphi<\infty\) is a true but idle fact, and the leftover height of a Yoccoz annulus never hits zero. The number becomes contentful only for a nonlinear germ or an analytic diffeomorphism that is *not* given as a conjugate of \(R_\alpha\).
Siegel radius bounds are two-sided controls on \(\log r(\alpha)\) by a Brjuno function. They are sharpest for the quadratic family
\[
P_\alpha(z)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}z+z^2.
\]
Conformal radius \(r(\alpha)\) is the derivative at \(0\) of the uniformization of the Siegel disk onto \(\mathbb D\), or \(0\) if there is no disk.


## Yoccoz: lower bound and optimality


If a germ is univalent on \(\lvert z\rvert<R\) and \(\alpha\) is Brjuno, the Siegel disk contains a round disk of radius
\[
\frac{R}{C\exp B(\alpha)}
\]
for a universal \(C\). In particular, for maps in Yoccoz’s class \(S_\alpha\) (univalent on \(\mathbb D\), \(f(0)=0\), \(f'(0)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\)),
\[
\log r(\alpha)\ge -B(\alpha)+C_-.
\]
He also proved optimality in that class: for every Brjuno \(\alpha\) there is some \(f\in S_\alpha\) with
\[
\log r(\alpha)\le -B(\alpha)+C_+.
\]
And if \(B(\alpha)=\infty\), then \(P_\alpha\) itself has \(r(\alpha)=0\). So Brjuno is necessary for the quadratic and sufficient for every germ.


## Buff–Chéritat: the matching upper bound for \(P_\alpha\)


Yoccoz conjectured that the quadratic itself saturates the upper bound. Buff–Chéritat proved
\[
B(\alpha)+\log r(\alpha)<C
\]
for a universal \(C\), first via parabolic explosion (2003), then by a more elementary return-annulus argument (2011). Combined with Yoccoz’s lower bound,
\[
\lvert B(\alpha)+\log r(\alpha)\rvert\le C
\]
on Brjuno numbers. The same statement with Yoccoz’s \(\mathcal B_Y\) (or \(\Phi\), or \(Y_{1/2}\)) holds because those functions differ from \(B\) by a bounded amount.


An intermediate geometric estimate they use: if \(\Omega_N\) is the complement of the external ray of argument \(0\) and of all periodic points of period \(\le q_N\), then
\[
\log\mathrm{rad}(\Omega_N)+\sum_{n=0}^N\frac{\log q_{n+1}}{q_n}<16,
\]
and the Siegel disk sits in \(\bigcap_N\Omega_N\).


## The compensated size \(\Upsilon\)


Define, on Brjuno numbers,
\[
\Upsilon(\alpha)=\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)+\log r(\alpha).
\]
Buff–Chéritat (2006 / *Ann. of Math.*): \(\Upsilon\) extends to a **continuous**, 1-periodic function on \(\mathbb R\). In particular it is bounded. That is stronger than two-sided inequalities: nearby Brjuno angles have nearby compensated radii, even if the raw \(r(\alpha)\) jumps around with \(\mathcal B_Y\).


Consequences:


- \(P_\alpha\) has a Siegel disk \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(\alpha\) is Brjuno (alternative to Yoccoz’s necessity proof).
- Continuity of \(\Upsilon\) does **not** make \(r\) continuous. At non-Brjuno points one sets \(r=0\) and does not form \(\infty+(-\infty)\).
- For the golden quadratic, v1.3’s exact prefactor
  \[
  r(\alpha)=\varphi^{-3}e^{\Upsilon(\alpha)}
  \]
  is \(\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)=3\log\varphi\) rewritten. The numerical value of \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\) is not supplied by the notes and is not computed in v1.3.


Marmi–Moussa–Yoccoz conjectured that the extension is \(1/2\)-Hölder. Cheraghi–Chéritat proved that on an appropriate high-type class. It is not a global theorem, and v1.3 refuses to quote it as one.


## Which family the bound belongs to


The two-sided bound \(\lvert\mathcal B_Y+\log r\rvert\le C\) is a theorem about **this** quadratic (and, with different \(C\), about some other unicritical families such as \(z^d+c\)).


It **fails** for
\[
e^{2\pi i\alpha}(z+z^d),\qquad d>2
\]
(Buff–Chéritat 2011). Upper control by \(-\mathcal B_Y+C\) is family-dependent. Yoccoz’s *lower* bound, by contrast, is universal for univalent germs on a disk.


Avila–Buff–Chéritat later produce prescribed boundary regularity by shrinking \(r\) inside families where a compensated size is available. That uses the bounds; it is not a new bound on \(r\).


## Inner radius versus conformal radius


Yoccoz’s univalent-disk estimate is an *inner* Euclidean radius. \(r(\alpha)\) is a *conformal* radius. They differ by a Koebe factor at most \(4\). All \(\log r\) statements absorb that into \(C\). For a round Siegel disk they coincide.


Bounded-type rational Siegel disks are quasidisks with the critical point on the boundary (Zhang). Then inner radius, conformal radius, and distance from \(0\) to \(\partial\Delta\) are comparable. For the wild ABC boundaries (smooth but not quasicircle, or \(C^n\setminus C^{n+1}\)) they need not be.


## What the bounds do not say


- No numerical \(r(\alpha)\) for the golden quadratic is in the literature quoted here, and v1.3 does not compute one.
- No bound of this form holds for a Siegel compact / hedgehog: those objects need not be linearizable, so \(r=0\) by definition.
- The warped screen \(F_\epsilon=H_\epsilon^{-1}R_\alpha H_\epsilon\) is already linearized on the circle; it is not \(P_\alpha\). Its “Siegel disk” in a complex extension, if any, is a different map and does not inherit Buff–Chéritat’s \(C\).
- \(\mu_2\), Loewner capacity, and \(\varphi^{28}\) do not determine \(r(\alpha)\).


The usable statement, scoped the way v1.3 scopes it: for this quadratic, \(\log r=-\mathcal B_Y+\Upsilon\) with \(\Upsilon\) continuous and bounded; for the golden angle that is \(\log r=-3\log\varphi+\Upsilon(\alpha)\); the remainder \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\) is a finite unknown, not a free parameter.
The compensated size is
\[
\Upsilon(\alpha)=\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)+\log r(\alpha)
\]
on Brjuno numbers, with \(r(\alpha)\) the conformal radius of the Siegel disk of \(P_\alpha(z)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}z+z^2\) (and \(r=0\) off Brjuno). Buff–Chéritat proved it extends continuously to \(\mathbb R\). The remaining question is how regular that extension is.


## The Marmi–Moussa–Yoccoz conjecture


Marmi’s numerics (1989) already looked continuous. MMY (1997) conjectured that the continuous extension is **Hölder of exponent \(1/2\)**:
\[
\lvert\Upsilon(\alpha)-\Upsilon(\beta)\rvert\le C\lvert\alpha-\beta\rvert^{1/2}.
\]
Carletti’s numerical study of the complex \(1/2\)-Brjuno function and the Yoccoz function supported the picture on the upper half-plane. The conjecture is still open on the whole circle.


## Optimality: nothing better than \(1/2\)


Buff–Chéritat showed that on **every** interval \(I\subset\mathbb R\),


- \(\Upsilon\) is **not** \(\delta\)-Hölder for any \(\delta>1/2\);
- \(\Upsilon\) has **unbounded variation** on \(I\).


So if MMY is true, the exponent is sharp. The obstruction is read from the expansion at rationals
\[
\Upsilon\Bigl(\frac pq\Bigr)=\log\frac{2\pi}q+L_a\Bigl(\frac pq\Bigr)+\Phi_{\mathrm{trunc}}\Bigl(\frac pq\Bigr),
\]
where \(L_a\) is the asymptotic size of the parabolic point of \(P_{p/q}\) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{trunc}}\) is a truncated Brjuno sum. Differences of these expansions across a rational do not cancel at order better than \(\lvert\alpha-p/q\rvert^{1/2}\). An auxiliary function \(H\) built from \(\Upsilon\) inherits the same failure of Hölder-\(>1/2\) and of bounded variation.


Unbounded variation already forbids absolute continuity, so \(\Upsilon\) is not a Sobolev \(W^{1,1}\) function. Continuity is the most one can expect in the BV scale.


## What is proved: high type


Cheraghi–Chéritat (*Invent. Math.* 2015): there exists \(N\) such that the restriction of \(\Upsilon\) to the **high-type set**
\[
\mathrm{HT}_N=\{\alpha:a_n(\alpha)\ge N\text{ for all }n\}
\]
**is** \(1/2\)-Hölder.


High type is the opposite of bounded type: all partial quotients are large. The proof uses the Inou–Shishikura class \(\mathcal{IS}\), a renormalization-invariant compact family of holomorphic maps near the quadratic, in which near-parabolic renormalization is defined and compact. On \(\mathrm{HT}_N\) the continued-fraction digits are large enough that renormalizations fall into \(\mathcal{IS}\) after one step. Hölder \(1/2\) is then an estimate on how conformal radii and truncated Brjuno sums move under that operator.


Golden \(\alpha=(3-\sqrt5)/2\) is **bounded type** (\(a_n\in\{1,2\}\)), so it is **not** in \(\mathrm{HT}_N\). The Cheraghi–Chéritat theorem does not apply to the screen’s angle. That is why v1.3 refuses a global \(1/2\)-Hölder citation and does not extract a numerical modulus of continuity at \(\alpha\).


## Status, stacked


| Statement | Status |
|---|---|
| \(\Upsilon\) bounded on Brjuno | Yoccoz lower + Buff–Chéritat upper |
| \(\Upsilon\) extends continuously to \(\mathbb R\) | Buff–Chéritat 2006 |
| Not \(\delta\)-Hölder for \(\delta>1/2\), on every interval | Buff–Chéritat |
| Unbounded variation on every interval | Buff–Chéritat |
| \(1/2\)-Hölder on \(\mathrm{HT}_N\) | Cheraghi–Chéritat 2015 |
| \(1/2\)-Hölder on all of \(\mathbb T\) | open (MMY) |
| Differentiable from each side at rationals | associated conjecture, open |
| Minimum at the golden number | numerical suggestion, unproved |


Related, and settled: \(Y_1-Y_{1/2}\) (the two Brjuno functions) *is* globally \(1/2\)-Hölder. That is an arithmetic fact about continued fractions, not a fact about \(r(\alpha)\). It lets one swap \(\mathcal B_Y\) for \(Y_{1/2}\) inside \(\Upsilon\) without changing the Hölder question.


## Why \(1/2\) is the natural exponent


Near a rational, parabolic explosion makes \(\log r\) jump on the scale of the square root of the distance to the parabolic parameter. The truncated Brjuno sum jumps on the same scale. Their sum \(\Upsilon\) is the remainder after those leading terms cancel. Square-root cancellation is exactly Hölder \(1/2\). Better cancellation would require a relation between asymptotic parabolic size and the Brjuno truncation that has not been proved globally — and is known to fail at order \(>1/2\).


## Against Geometry Maximization


- Continuous \(\Upsilon\) is already enough for v1.3’s identity \(r(\alpha)=\varphi^{-3}e^{\Upsilon(\alpha)}\). Hölder is extra regularity of the remainder, not a new radius formula.
- Golden \(\alpha\notin\mathrm{HT}_N\), so the only theorem that gives exponent \(1/2\) misses the screen.
- No Hölder modulus is computed, and none is needed for the warped-screen constructions of §§25–27.
- Unbounded variation of \(\Upsilon\) is compatible with the exact slip coboundary: those are functions of different variables.


The accurate sentence: \(\Upsilon\) is continuous; it cannot be better than \(1/2\)-Hölder; it is \(1/2\)-Hölder on high type; the global conjecture is open; the golden angle is not in the proved set.
Inou–Shishikura renormalization is a **near-parabolic** operator on a compact class of unicritical holomorphic maps. It is the machine behind high-type control of quadratic Siegel disks, including Cheraghi–Chéritat’s \(1/2\)-Hölder theorem for \(\Upsilon\) on \(\mathrm{HT}_N\).


## The class \(\mathcal F_1\) (or \(\mathcal{IS}\))


Fix the model
\[
P(z)=z(1+z)^2
\]
(sometimes written \(z(1-z)^2\); same local picture). Choose explicit quasidisks \(V\subset\subset V'\) containing the fixed point \(0\) and the critical point. The class is
\[
\mathcal F_1=\bigl\{f=P\circ\phi^{-1}:\phi:V\to\mathbb C\text{ univalent},\ \phi(0)=0,\ \phi'(0)=1,
\text{ with a qc extension to }\mathbb C\bigr\}.
\]
Every \(f\in\mathcal F_1\) has a single simple critical point. The quadratic \(z+z^2\) itself is in the closure; its parabolic renormalizations land in \(\mathcal F_1\). Maps look like
\[
e^{2\pi i\alpha}\,G\circ\phi^{-1}
\]
with \(\alpha\) small and \(G\) a fixed covering model. That is why applications are quadratic (local degree 2). Yang built a degree-3 analogue; Chéritat a unicritical analogue of arbitrary degree; Kapiamba a version near other roots of unity.


## What the operator does


A map with multiplier \(e^{2\pi i\alpha}\), \(\alpha\) small, is a small perturbation of a parabolic germ of multiplier \(1\). Fatou coordinates on attracting and repelling petals, plus the horn map (Écalle–Voronin), produce a **return map** on a crescent. After normalizing the new multiplier to sit at \(0\) and rescaling,
\[
\mathcal R_\alpha f = e^{-2\pi i/\alpha}\cdot(\text{normalized return of }e^{2\pi i\alpha}f).
\]
The new rotation number is essentially \(-1/\alpha\) (Gauss). This is parabolic implosion turned into an operator.


**Inou–Shishikura theorem.** There exists \(\alpha_0>0\) such that if \(f\in\mathcal F_1\) or \(f(z)=z+z^2\) and \(\lvert\alpha\rvert<\alpha_0\) with \(\lvert\arg\alpha\rvert<\pi/4\), then both flavors of near-parabolic renormalization are defined and of the form \(e^{2\pi i\beta}g\) with \(g\in\mathcal F_1\). The correspondence \(f\mapsto g\) is uniformly contracting in a suitable metric on \(\mathcal F_1\). The operator is holomorphic in the map.


So \(\mathcal F_1\) is invariant and the operator is a contraction on it. That compactness is what high type buys: \(a_n\ge N\) large forces \(\alpha_n=1/a_{n+1}+\cdots\) into the disk \(\lvert\alpha\rvert<\alpha_0\), so after one step you never leave \(\mathcal F_1\).


## How this differs from the other renormalizations in this thread


| Operator | When it applies | Space | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yoccoz return annulus | any analytic germ / circle diffeomorphism | estimates, not a manifold | leftover height, Brjuno cost |
| Yampolsky \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) | analytic *critical circle* maps (or Siegel cylinder maps) | Banach manifold \(\mathcal C_U\) | shift of all CF digits, horseshoe |
| Inou–Shishikura \(\mathcal R_\alpha\) | unicritical maps with multiplier *near* \(1\) | compact class \(\mathcal F_1\) | one large-digit Gauss step, contraction |


IS is local in arithmetic (only small \(\alpha\)) and local in the map (only degree-2 critical point near a parabolic). Cylinder renormalization is global in arithmetic and lives on real-symmetric cylinder maps. They are not the same operator. High-type quadratics can be fed into IS; bounded-type critical circle maps are fed into \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\).


The IS fixed point of *parabolic* renormalization (\(\alpha=0\)) is a different object again. Lanford–Yampolsky computed it numerically and gave it a geometric description as a maximal-domain map with rigid covering properties.


## Why Cheraghi–Chéritat need it


On \(\mathrm{HT}_N\), every Gauss iterate is small. The quadratic \(P_\alpha\) is, after one IS step, an element of \(e^{2\pi i\beta}\mathcal F_1\). Subsequent steps stay there and contract. Conformal radii and truncated Brjuno sums then move with a Hölder-\(1/2\) modulus, because near-parabolic explosion has square-root scaling and the contraction kills the rest. That is Theorem 1 of Cheraghi–Chéritat: \(\Upsilon|_{\mathrm{HT}_N}\) is \(1/2\)-Hölder.


Golden \(\alpha\) has \(a_n\in\{1,2\}\), so the Gauss iterates are *not* small. \(P_\alpha\) never enters the IS disk. The theorem does not apply to v1.3’s angle.


## Other theorems that sit on \(\mathcal F_1\)


- Buff–Chéritat: quadratic Julia sets of positive area (IS supplies compact control of the renormalized horns).
- Cheraghi–Shishikura: progress on MLC in the high-type / satellite region, via hyperbolicity of satellite renormalization built on IS.
- Local connectivity of high-type quadratic Siegel disks as Jordan domains (later work in the same school).
- Extensions: Yang (cubic), Chéritat (unicritical any degree), Kapiamba (multiplier near other roots of unity).


## What it is not


- Not a theory of the rigid golden rotation or of \(F_\epsilon=H_\epsilon^{-1}R_\alpha H_\epsilon\). Those maps have no critical point in \(\mathbb C\) of the IS type.
- Not a substitute for Douady–Yoccoz measures or Loewner chains.
- Not a proof that \(\Upsilon\) is \(1/2\)-Hölder at the golden number.
- Not Yampolsky’s horseshoe: no shift on all of \(\mathbb N^{\mathbb Z}\), only contraction on a neighborhood of one parabolic.


The usable sentence for the corpus: Inou–Shishikura give a contracting invariant class for *near-parabolic unicritical* maps; high type is the arithmetic that keeps a quadratic inside that class; the golden screen is bounded type and stays outside.
The horn map is how two Fatou coordinates, one on each side of a parabolic point, are glued. It is the complete local analytic invariant of a simple parabolic germ, and the map that Inou–Shishikura renormalize.


## The germ


A **simple parabolic** germ at \(0\) is
\[
f(z)=z+cz^2+O(z^3),\qquad c\neq 0.
\]
After a linear change of coordinate one may take \(c=1\), or pass to infinity by \(Z=1/z\) and work with
\[
F(Z)=Z+1+\frac{b}{Z}+\cdots.
\]
Multiplier \(1\), one attracting petal and one repelling petal (the non-degenerate case). Nearby maps \(e^{2\pi i\alpha}f\) with \(\alpha\) small are the near-parabolic perturbations IS acts on.


## Petals and Fatou coordinates


Leau–Fatou: there is an attracting petal \(P^+\) on which every orbit tends to \(0\) and a repelling petal \(P^-\) on which the inverse does. In the \(Z\)-plane these are right and left half-planes, up to bounded distortion, for \(\lvert Z\rvert\) large.


An **attracting Fatou coordinate** \(\Phi^+:P^+\to\mathbb C\) is a univalent map conjugating \(f\) to translation by \(1\):
\[
\Phi^+\circ f = \Phi^+ + 1.
\]
A **repelling Fatou parametrization** \(\Psi^-:\{\operatorname{Re}W\ll 0\}\to P^-\) does the inverse job:
\[
f\circ\Psi^- = \Psi^-\circ T_1,\qquad T_1(W)=W+1.
\]
Existence is classical. Normalization (e.g. \(\Phi^+(Z)=Z+\rho\log Z+o(1)\)) kills the remaining translation freedom up to an additive constant. Dudko–Sauzin write explicit formulas
\[
v^\pm_*(Z)=Z+\rho\operatorname{Log}^\pm Z+L^\pm\hat\varphi(Z)
\]
with Gevrey remainder.


\(\Phi^+\) and \(\Psi^-\) are only defined on their petals. The petals overlap in two “horns” — upper and lower regions that wrap around \(0\) in the original coordinate, or around the ends of a large cylinder in the \(Z\)-plane.


## The lifted horn map


On the overlap of the attracting chart with the image of the repelling parametrization,
\[
H=\Phi^+\circ\Psi^-
\]
is defined on two half-planes \(\{\operatorname{Im}W>M\}\) and \(\{\operatorname{Im}W<-M\}\). Because both charts conjugate to \(T_1\),
\[
H\circ T_1=T_1\circ H.
\]
So \(H\) commutes with translation by \(1\) and descends to a pair of maps on the cylinder \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\):
\[
\mathbb h^\pm:\{ \operatorname{Im} z>\textstyle\tfrac M{2\pi}\}\to\mathbb C/\mathbb Z.
\]
These are the **horn maps**. Each extends holomorphically through the cylinder and has an essential singularity or a ramified covering picture at the ends, depending on normalization.


Fourier expansion on the cylinder:
\[
H(W)=W+2\pi i A_0 + \sum_{m\neq 0}A_m e^{2\pi i m W}.
\]
The coefficients \((A_m)_{m\neq 0}\) are the **Écalle–Voronin invariants**. Two simple parabolic germs are analytically conjugate iff their invariants differ by the coboundary of a translation, \(A_m(g)=e^{2\pi i m c}A_m(f)\). The lifted horn map is a complete local conjugacy invariant.


## From horn map to parabolic renormalization


Project the upper horn map through \(w=e^{2\pi i W}\). One obtains a germ \(h\) at \(0\) (or at \(\infty\)) whose multiplier records the *résidu itératif*. After a standard normalization,
\[
\mathcal R f = h
\]
is again a germ with a parabolic (or near-parabolic) point. That is **parabolic renormalization**. It is not automatically simple: one needs \(A_1\neq 0\) to keep the same local degree.


Near-parabolic renormalization is the same construction for \(e^{2\pi i\alpha}f\) with \(\alpha\) small. The Fatou coordinates survive as almost-Fatou coordinates (Douady–Lavaurs / Shishikura), the horns move continuously, and the output is
\[
\mathcal R_\alpha f = e^{-2\pi i/\alpha}\cdot(\text{normalized horn germ}),
\]
which is Inou–Shishikura’s operator. Invariance of \(\mathcal F_1\) is the statement that this germ, after the covering identification with \(P\circ\phi^{-1}\), stays in the class.


## Geometric picture in one paragraph


Attracting coordinate straightens the incoming petal to a right half-plane; repelling coordinate straightens the outgoing petal to a left half-plane. On the two bridges that connect those half-planes above and below the real axis, the mismatch between the two charts is a translation-commuting map — the lifted horn map. That mismatch *is* the analytic modulus of the parabolic point. Renormalization is “read the mismatch, project it to a new germ.”


## Relation to the other gluings in this thread


| Gluing | What is identified | Quotient |
|---|---|---|
| Yoccoz return annulus | \(L\sim f(L)\) | an annulus of whatever modulus |
| Yampolsky crescent | \(l\sim f^{q_n}(l)\) | required to be \(\mathbb C/\mathbb Z\) |
| Horn maps | attracting chart \(\sim\) repelling chart on the two horns | a pair of cylinder maps \(\mathbb h^\pm\) |
| Loewner / Biswas | exterior Riemann maps in capacity time | a flow of circle diffeomorphisms |


Yoccoz glues a *return* of a non-parabolic map. Horn maps glue the *two petals of one parabolic point*. Cylinder renormalization standardizes a high-period return of a critical circle map. They share the words “crescent” and “cylinder”; they are not the same identification.


Lanford–Yampolsky: if two maps are conjugate on their immediate parabolic basins by a homeomorphism continuous at the parabolic point, the horn maps are cover-equivalent (isomorphisms of the top/bottom domains intertwining \(\mathbb h\) up to a cylinder translation). Chéritat–Le Meur weaken this to local pseudo-conjugacy, still characterized by the same cover-equivalence of horns. That is the rigidity statement that makes an invariant class under \(\mathcal R\) meaningful.


## Against the screen


A rigid rotation has no parabolic point. v1.3’s \(F_\epsilon\) has multiplier \(e^{2\pi i\alpha}\) on the circle, far from \(1\). \(P_\alpha(z)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}z+z^2\) is near-parabolic only when \(\alpha\) is near an integer, i.e. high type after a Gauss step — which is exactly when IS applies, and exactly when golden \(\alpha\) does not.


The horn map is the modulus of a parabolic germ. It becomes a renormalization operator when you project it. It is not an occupation measure, not a Loewner driver, and not a 39-bin.
Alien derivatives measure the jumps of a Borel transform across its singularities. For a simple parabolic germ they reconstruct the Écalle–Voronin coefficients as explicit series.


## Borel summation, in one page


A formal series \(\tilde\varphi(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}a_n z^{-n}\) at infinity is **Gevrey-1** if \(\lvert a_n\rvert\le C R^n n!\). Its **formal Borel transform**
\[
\hat\varphi(\zeta)=\mathcal B\tilde\varphi(\zeta)=\sum_{n\ge1}a_n\frac{\zeta^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
\]
converges in a disk. If \(\hat\varphi\) continues analytically along a ray \(\arg\zeta=\theta\) with at most exponential growth, the **Laplace transform**
\[
(\mathcal L_\theta\hat\varphi)(z)=\int_0^{e^{i\theta}\infty}e^{-z\zeta}\hat\varphi(\zeta)\,d\zeta
\]
is a holomorphic function on a sector, Gevrey-asymptotic to \(\tilde\varphi\). Different rays, different sums: that is the Stokes phenomenon.


A series is **resurgent** when \(\hat\varphi\) continues to a covering of \(\mathbb C\) minus a discrete set of singularities (typically a lattice), with controlled growth. Parabolic Fatou series are the model example.


## The Fatou series of a simple parabolic germ


For \(F(Z)=Z+1+\rho Z^{-1}+O(Z^{-2})\), the Fatou equation \(v\circ F=v+1\) becomes, after \(v=Z+\rho\log Z+\varphi\), a linear difference equation for \(\varphi\). There is a unique formal solution \(\tilde\varphi\in Z^{-1}\mathbb C[[Z^{-1}]]\). Dudko–Sauzin: \(\tilde\varphi\) is \(2\pi i\mathbb Z\)-resurgent and Borel-summable. The two Laplace sums \(\mathcal L_\pm\hat\varphi\) along rays just above and below \(\mathbb R_+\) produce the two normalized Fatou coordinates
\[
v^\pm_*=\mathrm{id}+\rho\operatorname{Log}^\pm+\mathcal L_\pm\hat\varphi.
\]
Their mismatch is the lifted horn map. So the Stokes jump of \(\hat\varphi\) *is* the EV modulus.


## Alien operators


Let \(\hat\varphi\) be resurgent, with a singularity at \(\omega\neq 0\). Analytic continuation along a path that skirts \(\omega\) to the left versus to the right produces two germs at \(\omega+\varepsilon\). Their difference, transported back to \(0\) by \(e^{-\omega z}\) (the Laplace kernel of that point), is a new resurgent series. Écalle’s **alien derivative** \(\Delta_\omega\) is that operation, normalized so that it is a derivation of the convolution algebra of resurgent series:
\[
\Delta_\omega(\hat a*\hat b)=(\Delta_\omega\hat a)*\hat b+\hat a*(\Delta_\omega\hat b).
\]
The dotted variants \(\dot\Delta_\omega\) include a choice of lateral convention. Iterated aliens \(\Delta_{\omega_n}\cdots\Delta_{\omega_1}\) read the monodromy around several singularities.


On the Borel plane of the Fatou series the singularities sit at \(2\pi i\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}\). That lattice is why the horn map is periodic of period \(1\) after Laplace: each alien at \(2\pi i m\) Laplace-transforms to a Fourier mode \(e^{2\pi i m W}\).


## Reconstruction of \(A_m\)


Dudko–Sauzin: each Écalle–Voronin coefficient is a convergent series in alien data of \(\tilde\varphi\). Schematic form (their Theorems 1–2): the singularity of \(\hat\varphi\) at \(\omega=2\pi i m\) is of a controlled type, and
\[
A_m=\text{explicit linear combination of }\bigl(\Delta_{2\pi i m}\tilde\varphi\bigr)
\]
evaluated by a Laplace / residue formula along a segment from \(1\) to the singularity. When the singularity is not simple, one uses a regularized alien (or a finite jet of the local expansion). The output is a numerical series for \(A_m\), not merely an existence statement.


This is Écalle’s original route to the modulus, written with ordinary Borel summability as the only prerequisite.


## Bridge equation


The alien calculus of a *nonlinear* object (here the Fatou coordinate, which solves a conjugacy) is constrained by the fact that the object is a substitution. Écalle’s **bridge equation** says that aliens of the formal conjugacy are proportional to its ordinary derivative:
\[
\dot\Delta_\omega\tilde v = A(\omega)\,\partial_Z\tilde v
\]
(or a close variant). The proportionality factors \(A(\omega)\) *are* the EV invariants. That is why aliens compute the modulus rather than some unrelated Stokes data: the functional equation \(v\circ F=v+1\) forces the Stokes jumps to be translations in the Fatou chart, i.e. Fourier modes of a horn map.


For linear ODEs the bridge equation relates aliens to the monodromy / Stokes matrices. For parabolic germs it relates aliens to horn maps. Same formalism, different equation.


## What “resurgent” adds to “Borel-summable”


Borel-summable \(\Rightarrow\) two sectorial sums, one Stokes jump across \(\mathbb R_+\).  
Resurgent \(\Rightarrow\) that jump itself is Borel-summable, its Borel transform has singularities on a known lattice, those can be jumped again, and so on. The alien operators organize that tower. Without resurgence you can define the two Fatou coordinates; you cannot compute the full sequence \((A_m)\) from a calculus on formal series.


## Place in the nearby constructions


| Tool | What it sees |
|---|---|
| Laplace along two rays | two Fatou coordinates \(v^\pm_*\) |
| Stokes jump of those sums | lifted horn map \(H=v^+_*\circ(v^-_*)^{-1}\) |
| Alien \(\Delta_{2\pi im}\) of \(\tilde\varphi\) | Fourier mode \(A_m\) of \(H\) |
| Bridge equation | why those modes are conjugacy invariants |


Parabolic renormalization still reads the upper horn germ. Alien calculus is a *computational* description of that germ, not a different operator.


## Against Geometry Maximization

Alien calculus is a method for reading Stokes data of resurgent series. Its applications are wherever a Gevrey formal object has a Borel transform with a discrete set of singularities.


## 1. Local holomorphic dynamics (the origin)


This is the thread already in play.


- **Parabolic germs.** Aliens of the formal Fatou series at \(2\pi i\mathbb Z\) are the Écalle–Voronin coefficients. Classification and parabolic renormalization are the output.
- **Saddle-nodes and resonant saddles.** Formal normal-form series are resurgent. Aliens give the moduli of analytic classification (Martinet–Ramis, Écalle). Paralogarithmic resurgence monomials make the *synthesis* problem effective: given a prescribed modulus, build a realizing vector field (Fauvet).
- **Tangent-to-identity germs in \(\mathbb C^n\).** Écalle–Hakim Fatou flowers: a non-degenerate characteristic direction still has a petal and a Fatou coordinate, constructed resurgently even when there is no invariant curve.
- **Dulac’s problem.** Écalle’s constructive proof that a real-analytic planar vector field has only finitely many limit cycles uses analysable germs and alien calculus on the return map of a hyperbolic polycycle. The Dulac series is not a power series; it is a transseries. Aliens organize its Stokes data. (Ilyashenko gave an independent proof by different methods.)


These are theorems about *local analytic* objects. They do not apply to a rigid rotation or to \(F_\epsilon\).


## 2. Transseries and analysable functions


Écalle’s larger machine: the algebra of **transseries** (formal series in \(z^{-1}\), \(\log z\), \(e^{-z}\), iterated exponentials, with well-ordered supports) and the subalgebra of **analysable** germs (those that can be summed by a tower of Borel–Laplace and acceleration operators).


Alien derivations act on this algebra. The **bridge equation** still holds for objects defined by a conjugacy or a differential equation: aliens of the unknown are proportional to its ordinary derivative, and the factors are the moduli.


Applications:


- closedness under composition, iteration, and integration;
- a formalization principle: local analytic constructions produce objects that are entirely encoded by a discrete set of real coefficients plus a resurgence lattice.


This is the language in which Dulac is written. It is not the language of the 39-screen coboundary, which is already a closed \(C^0\) identity.


## 3. Linear ODEs and WKB


For a linear meromorphic ODE with an irregular singularity, the formal fundamental solution is a Gevrey series (or a matrix of them). Its Borel transform has singularities at the differences of eigenvalues (or Stokes values). Alien derivatives of that series *are* the Stokes matrices, up to the bridge equation.


Exact WKB (Voros, Pham, Delabaere–Pham, later Honda–Kawai–Takei) is the same calculus for the Schrödinger equation in a complex potential: the Borel plane singularities sit at action differences between turning points, and aliens compute connection formulae across Stokes curves.


The lattice of singularities is determined by the classical action, not by \(2\pi i\mathbb Z\) unless the problem is parabolic.


## 4. Nonlinear ODEs: Painlevé and beyond


Painlevé transcendents have formal solutions at infinity that are resurgent. Aliens encode the nonlinear Stokes phenomenon (Costin, Guzzetti, Its, Kapaev, …). The bridge equation is more involved because the equation is nonlinear; mould calculus (Écalle’s combinatorial package for composing aliens) keeps the bookkeeping.


This produces exact connection formulae and monodromy data from formal series plus a finite number of Stokes constants.


## 5. Quantum field theory and matrix models


Perturbative expansions in QFT and in matrix models are typically Gevrey-1 (or worse) and resurgent. Singularities of the Borel transform sit at instanton actions and at their multiples and sums (“alien towers”).


What aliens compute:


- Stokes constants = instanton / anti-instanton amplitudes;
- the transseries completion of the perturbative series (non-perturbative sectors);
- renormalization-group constraints on those constants (Bellon: two-point function of a massless theory as a resurgent solution of an RG equation).


Since the 2010s this has been a large literature (Dunne–Ünsal, Mariño, Aniceto–Schiappa, …), with a documented link to wall-crossing formulae in which the same Stokes data appear as DT invariants. Sauzin’s *Encyclopedia of Mathematical Physics* survey is the short map.


The analytic hypotheses are those of the Borel plane of a specific perturbative series. They are not supplied by a circle rotation.


## 6. Mould calculus as the composition rule


Aliens at different \(\omega\) do not commute. Écalle’s moulds (and comoulds) multiply and compose them. Applications that need several singularities — multiple instantons, several petals, several turning points — use moulds rather than a single \(\Delta_\omega\).


Without moulds one can still define each alien; with moulds one can write the full monodromy of the Borel covering as a closed formula.


## What every application shares


1. A Gevrey (or transseries) formal object coming from an analytic equation.
2. A Borel transform with discrete singularities at locations predicted by the equation (lattice, action differences, instanton actions).
3. A bridge or similar constraint that turns aliens into moduli / Stokes / instanton data.
4. A summation procedure that produces actual analytic functions on sectors.


If any of (1)–(4) is missing, alien calculus has nothing to act on.

Mould calculus is Écalle’s Hopf-algebraic bookkeeping for families of operators indexed by *words*. Alien derivations generated the first moulds; the same algebra now runs well beyond resurgence.


## What a mould is


A **mould** \(M^\bullet\) assigns to every finite word \(\omega_1\cdots\omega_r\) in an alphabet \(\Omega\) (usually a set of Borel singularities) a coefficient \(M^{\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_r}\) in a commutative ring, or a resurgent series. The empty word is included. A **comould** \(B_\bullet\) is a family of operators indexed by the same words, typically
\[
B_{\omega_1\cdots\omega_r}=\Delta_{\omega_r}\cdots\Delta_{\omega_1}
\]
or a family of ordinary differential operators.


The pairing
\[
\sum_{\text{words }\omega} M^\omega B_\omega
\]
is the mould–comould expansion of an automorphism, a conjugacy, a normal-form transformation, or a Stokes map. Convergence of that sum is the analytic problem; **arborification** (replacing linearly ordered words by trees) is Écalle’s device that often restores it.


Four primary symmetry types:


| Type | Relation | Typical role |
|---|---|---|
| symmetral | shuffle product | group-like automorphisms |
| alternal | infinitesimal shuffle / Lie | derivations (aliens) |
| symmetrel | stuffle | MZV / multiple polylogs |
| alternel | infinitesimal stuffle | corresponding Lie elements |


Operations (product, composition, inversion, flexion, …) preserve or transmute these symmetries in a fixed table. About thirty “fundamental moulds” (hyperlogarithmic \(U^\bullet,V^\bullet\), exponential, …) generate most of the ones that appear in practice.


Standard aliens themselves come from one mould:
\[
M^{\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_r}=\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{r},
\]
the homogeneous components of \(\log\) of the Stokes diffeomorphism.


## Applications inside resurgence


**Synthesis of moduli.** Given an EV-type (or Martinet–Ramis) modulus, write the realizing conjugacy as a mould–comould expansion whose mould is built from paralogarithmic resurgence monomials. Arborification makes the expansion converge on the petals. That is Fauvet’s effective synthesis for saddle-nodes, following Écalle. Without moulds one has existence; with moulds one has a formula.


**Bridge equation in several singularities.** A single alien \(\Delta_\omega\tilde v=A(\omega)\,\partial\tilde v\) is the one-singularity case. Several turning points or several petals produce a word-indexed family of aliens. The mould form of the bridge equation packages them so that the Stokes automorphism is \(\exp(\sum M^\omega\Delta_\omega)\).


**Acceleration and analysable towers.** Changing Borel level (Écalle acceleration) acts on moulds by a fixed substitution. Transseries operations become mould operations.


## Applications that left resurgence


**Normal forms in Lie algebras (Paul–Sauzin).** Abstract the conjugacy problem to “conjugate an element of a complete filtered Lie algebra to a normal form.” The unknown conjugacy satisfies a mould equation, solved by an explicit gauge group of moulds. Specializations:


- Poincaré–Dulac formal normal forms of a vector field at an equilibrium;
- infinite-order multiphase averaging for vector fields with fast angles;
- classical and quantum Birkhoff normal forms.


By-product: for harmonic oscillators, the quantum Birkhoff form converges to the classical one *without* a Diophantine condition on the frequencies. The mould expansion sees cancellations that a naive small-divisor count misses — Écalle’s “chimera of super-multiple small denominators.”


**Multiple zeta values.** Stuffle-symmetrel moulds are the generating series of MZVs. Mould relations reproduce (and organize) the double-shuffle and associator relations. This is now a standard combinatorial language for MZVs, independent of any Borel plane.


**Local differential geometry.** Scalar- or function-valued moulds with ordinary differential operators as comoulds write normalizing transformations, fractional iterates, and normal forms of analytic vector fields or diffeomorphisms. Same expansions as in the Lie-algebra paper, now on manifolds.


**Combinatorics / pre-Lie algebra.** The flexion structure (ARI/GARI) that preserves *double* symmetries is a pre-Lie / post-Lie calculus. It has been used as an abstract Hopf-algebra machine, not only as a resurgence tool.


## What moulds do not do


They do not replace analysis. A mould formula for a conjugacy is only as convergent as arborification plus the growth of the coefficients. In KAM-type problems moulds can cancel fake accumulations of denominators; they do not remove a genuine Brjuno obstruction.


They are not a substitute for Douady–Yoccoz uniqueness, Loewner existence, or Yampolsky hyperbolicity. Those theorems live on Banach manifolds of maps, not on word-indexed coefficients.

A pre-Lie algebra is the identity that grafting of rooted trees satisfies. It sits one step before a Lie algebra and one step behind the Connes–Kreimer and Écalle machines.


## Definition


A vector space \(V\) with a bilinear product \(\triangleleft\) is **pre-Lie** (left-symmetric, Vinberg, Gerstenhaber) when
\[
(x\triangleleft y)\triangleleft z-x\triangleleft(y\triangleleft z)
=(x\triangleleft z)\triangleleft y-x\triangleleft(z\triangleleft y).
\]
The associator is symmetric in the last two arguments. The commutator
\[
[x,y]=x\triangleleft y-y\triangleleft x
\]
is then a Lie bracket: the pre-Lie identity cancels the extra terms in Jacobi. Every pre-Lie algebra is a Lie algebra; the converse is false.


Right-pre-Lie is the mirror identity. Post-Lie and pre-associative algebras are nearby variants; Écalle’s flexion unit produces a pre-associative structure parallel to pre-Lie.


## Free pre-Lie algebra: rooted trees


Let \(\mathbb T\) be the span of rooted trees. Grafting
\[
\tau_1\curvearrowleft\tau_2=\sum_{s\in V(\tau_1)}\tau_1\circ_s\tau_2
\]
inserts the root of \(\tau_2\) at each vertex of \(\tau_1\) in turn. Then \((\mathbb T,\curvearrowleft)\) is the **free pre-Lie algebra on one generator**. With vertices labelled by a set \(S\), one gets the free pre-Lie algebra on \(\lvert S\rvert\) generators.


Chapoton–Livernet: the pre-Lie operad is the operad of labelled rooted trees with this grafting, and it is Koszul. The Lie algebra of the free pre-Lie algebra is a free Lie algebra (Foissy; Chapoton; Bergeron–Livernet). Chapoton’s conjecture that the pre-Lie operad, as a Lie algebra in species, is freely generated by the free operad on cyclic Lie elements is now proved.


## Why this identity appears


**Vector fields on affine space.** \((X\triangleleft Y)=\nabla_Y X\) (covariant derivative in flat coordinates) is pre-Lie. The commutator is the usual Lie bracket of vector fields. This is Vinberg’s original geometric source.


**Infinitesimal conjugacies.** If \(x\triangleleft y\) means “insert the vector field \(y\) into the formal series \(x\) at every occurrence of the linear term,” the pre-Lie identity is the chain rule for composition of formal diffeomorphisms. Poincaré–Dulac and Birkhoff conjugacies, written as mould–comould expansions, live in this algebra.


**Alien derivations.** The family \(\{\Delta_\omega\}\) is not itself pre-Lie under composition (aliens at different \(\omega\) fail associator symmetry). Arborification — replacing words by trees — *is* a passage to a free pre-Lie algebra. That is why Écalle’s arborification–coarborification transform restores convergence: it replaces a badly ordered mould sum by a grafting expansion in the free pre-Lie algebra on the alphabet of singularities.


**Flexion.** Écalle’s Flex\((E^\bullet)\) is a polyalgebra generated by a flexion unit. It admits a natural indexing by trees and uploads a pre-Lie / pre-associative structure onto those trees. ARI/GARI are the flexion Lie/group pair that preserve double symmetries; they are the operadic envelope in which moulds with two interacting shuffles live.


## Hopf algebra: Connes–Kreimer


The universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra of rooted trees, with the coproduct of admissible cuts, is the **Connes–Kreimer Hopf algebra**. Equivalently: take the free pre-Lie algebra, pass to its commutator Lie algebra, take \(U(\mathfrak g)\). That Hopf algebra organizes BPHZ renormalization of Feynman diagrams (trees as a skeleton of graphs). Schedler: CK is the canonical quantization of a pre-Lie algebra, and one gets a PBW theorem for any Lie algebra that comes from a pre-Lie product, over an arbitrary commutative ring.


So the same trees do three jobs:


| Structure | Product | Use |
|---|---|---|
| pre-Lie | grafting | free algebra, conjugacies, arborification |
| Lie | commutator of grafting | CK Lie algebra of trees |
| Hopf | admissible cuts on \(U(\mathfrak g)\) | renormalization, characters |

The Connes–Kreimer Hopf algebra is the commutative graded Hopf algebra of rooted forests with the admissible-cut coproduct. Combinatorially it is the same object as Butcher’s algebra of B-series; Lie-theoretically it is \(U(\mathfrak g)\) for the Lie algebra of the free pre-Lie algebra of trees.


## Algebra


Let \(\mathcal T\) be the set of rooted trees, \(\mathcal F\) the set of rooted forests (finite disjoint unions of trees, including the empty forest \(1\)). \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\) is the polynomial algebra \(\mathbb k[\mathcal T]\), equivalently the span of forests with disjoint union as product. It is graded by the number of vertices, connected, commutative, associative.


Decorated versions (vertices labelled by a set of residues, or by Feynman-graph types) are the versions used in QFT. The undecorated algebra is the skeleton.


## Coproduct: admissible cuts


An **elementary cut** of a tree \(t\) is the deletion of one edge. The component still containing the root is the **trunk** \(R_c(t)\); the rest is a forest, the **pruning** \(P_c(t)\).


A **cut** is a set of edges. It is **admissible** when, on the unique path from any vertex to the root, at most one cut edge appears — no stacked cuts along a lineage.


\[
\Delta t = t\otimes 1 + 1\otimes t + \sum_{c\text{ admissible}} P_c(t)\otimes R_c(t).
\]
The empty cut and the total cut (all edges adjacent to the root, in the recursive \(B_+\) picture) recover the two primitive terms. Coassociativity is the statement that two successive admissible cuts, in either order, give the same double pruning. The counit vanishes on every nonempty forest. An antipode exists by the general theory of graded connected Hopf algebras and is given recursively by Bogoliubov’s formula.


Equivalent recursive form: if \(B_+\) grafts a forest under a new root,
\[
\Delta B_+(F)=B_+(F)\otimes 1+(id\otimes B_+)\Delta F.
\]
That cocycle is the Grossman–Larson dual description.


## Dual and pre-Lie


The graded dual is (completed) the Grossman–Larson Hopf algebra of trees with a noncommutative product that grafts one tree onto all vertices of another. The primitives of the dual are spanned by trees; their product is grafting; that product is pre-Lie. Hence:


\[
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\;\simeq\; U(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}})^\circ
\]
in the graded sense, where \(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}}\) is the commutator Lie algebra of free pre-Lie. This is the sentence of the previous note.


## Characters and Birkhoff decomposition


A **character** of \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\) is an algebra map \(\varphi:\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\to A\) into a commutative algebra \(A\) (typically germs of meromorphic functions of a regularization parameter \(\varepsilon\), or formal Laurent series). Evaluation of a Feynman rule on diagrams, pushed forward along the map from graphs to trees, is such a character.


If \(A=A_+\oplus A_-\) is a Birkhoff decomposition of the target (holomorphic / polar parts), Connes–Kreimer: every character \(\varphi\) factors uniquely
\[
\varphi=\varphi_-^{-1}*\varphi_+
\]
in the character group, with \(\varphi_\pm\) landing in \(A_\pm\). The factor \(\varphi_-\) *is* the counterterm map; \(\varphi_+\) is the renormalized value. The antipode supplies the recursive formula for \(\varphi_-\). That is BPHZ, written as a factorization in a group of characters rather than as a forest formula on graphs.


The same factorization, with \(A=\mathbb R\), is Butcher’s composition of B-series: characters are numerical methods, convolution is composition of methods, and the group is the Butcher group.


## What the Hopf algebra does not know


It does not know dimensional regularization, nor a specific Lagrangian. Those enter as the choice of character. Different QFTs give different characters on the same (or a decorated) Hopf algebra. Locality of counterterms is extra analytic input that makes \(\varphi_-\) a polynomial in \(1/\varepsilon\).


It does not know Borel summation or aliens. Resurgent QFT uses *both*: CK organizes the graph combinatorics of a given loop order; aliens organize the Borel-plane singularities of the resulting series in \(\hbar\). They commute as tools; neither replaces the other.

Birkhoff decomposition in the Connes–Kreimer setting is a unique factorization in a group of characters. It is the algebraic form of BPHZ and, when the target is meromorphic functions of a regulator, a Riemann–Hilbert problem on the character group.


## The group


Let \(H\) be a connected filtered (or graded) Hopf algebra — \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\), a decorated version, or the Hopf algebra of Feynman graphs. Let \(A\) be a commutative unital algebra. Linear maps \(L(H,A)\) carry the **convolution**
\[
(\varphi*\psi)(x)=\sum_{(x)}\varphi(x')\psi(x''),\qquad \Delta x=\sum x'\otimes x''.
\]
The **character group** \(G(A)\) is the group of algebra morphisms \(\varphi:H\to A\) with \(\varphi(1)=1\), under convolution. The unit is the counit of \(H\). Inversion is convolution with the antipode:
\[
\varphi^{*-1}=\varphi\circ S
\]
when \(\varphi\) is a character.


## A renormalization scheme


A **scheme** is a splitting of the target
\[
A=A_-\oplus A_+
\]
into two subalgebras, with \(1\in A_+\). Write \(\pi:A\to A_-\) for the projection along \(A_+\).


Standard example (minimal subtraction in dimensional regularization): \(A=\mathbb C[z^{-1},z]]\), meromorphic germs at \(0\);
- \(A_-=z^{-1}\mathbb C[z^{-1}]\) (polar parts, no constant term);
- \(A_+=\mathbb C[[z]]\) (holomorphic germs).


Other schemes (momentum subtraction, BPHZ subtraction at a Euclidean point, …) are other splittings of other targets.


## The factorization theorem


**Connes–Kreimer; Manchon’s filtered formulation.** Every \(\varphi\in G(A)\) admits a unique factorization
\[
\varphi=\varphi_-^{*-1}*\varphi_+
\]
with \(\varphi_\pm\in G(A)\), \(\varphi_+(\ker\varepsilon)\subset A_+\), and \(\varphi_-(\ker\varepsilon)\subset A_-\). If \(\varphi\) is a character, so are \(\varphi_\pm\).


Recursive formulae, using the **Bogoliubov preparation map**
\[
\bar\varphi(x)=\varphi(x)+\sum_{x'\otimes x''}\varphi_-(x')\varphi(x'')
\]
(sum over the reduced coproduct):
\[
\varphi_-(x)=-\pi\bigl(\bar\varphi(x)\bigr),\qquad
\varphi_+(x)=(id-\pi)\bigl(\bar\varphi(x)\bigr).
\]
On primitives (\(\Delta x=x\otimes1+1\otimes x\)) this collapses to \(\varphi_-(x)=-\pi(\varphi(x))\), \(\varphi_+(x)=(id-\pi)(\varphi(x))\). Higher graphs / trees pick up the forest of proper subgraphs / admissible prunings through \(\varphi_-\) on the left factor. That recursion *is* Zimmermann’s forest formula, written in the character group.


\(\varphi_-\) is the **counterterm character**. \(\varphi_+\) is the **renormalized character**. In MS, the physical value is \(\varphi_+\big|_{z=0}\).


## Riemann–Hilbert picture


Connes–Kreimer’s original packaging: dimensional regularization produces a loop
\[
\gamma(z)\in G(\mathbb C),\qquad \lvert z\rvert=\varepsilon_0,
\]
the bare Feynman rules as a function of the deviation from dimension 4. The Birkhoff decomposition of that loop on the Riemann sphere —
\[
\gamma(z)=\gamma_-(z)^{-1}\gamma_+(z),
\]
\(\gamma_+\) holomorphic inside the contour, \(\gamma_-\) holomorphic outside with \(\gamma_-(\infty)=1\) — is the same factorization. Evaluating \(\gamma_+\) at \(z=0\) is minimal subtraction. Renormalization is a Riemann–Hilbert problem on the structure group of the Hopf algebra.


The algebraic theorem does not need a circle or a complex structure on the regulator. The RH language needs both.


## Renormalization group and \(\beta\)-function


When \(H\) is graded, a one-parameter family of automorphisms \(\theta_t\) of \(H\) (degree rescaling) induces a one-parameter group on \(G(A)\). The negative piece \(\varphi_-\), after a standard preparation, is independent of the sliding scale except through a **beta function**
\[
\beta=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}(\varphi_-\circ\theta_t)^{*-1}*\varphi_-
\]
taking values in the Lie algebra of infinitesimal characters. That \(\beta\) is the Connes–Kreimer beta function. It encodes the failure of \(\varphi_-\) to be scale-invariant and reproduces the usual \(\beta\) of the coupling when \(H\) is the graph Hopf algebra of a given theory.


Girelli–Krajewski–Martinetti rewrite the same factorization as a change of boundary condition for a differential equation on the character group (algebraic ERG).


## What uniqueness costs


Uniqueness uses connectedness of \(H\): every element is a finite sum of primitives and products, so the recursion on the filtration has a unique start. Without connectedness the factorization need not exist or need not be unique.


Characters are required for \(\varphi_\pm\) to stay multiplicative. For a mere linear map the same formulae still split \(\varphi\), but the factors need not be algebra morphisms, and BPHZ locality is lost.


## What the decomposition is not


- Not Borel summation. Polar parts in \(z\) (the regulator) are not singularities in the Borel plane of \(\hbar\).
- Not an Écalle–Voronin modulus. EV classifies a parabolic germ; Birkhoff classifies a character of a combinatorial Hopf algebra.
- Not Yoccoz height recurrence. No annulus modulus appears.
- Not unique without a choice of scheme. Different \(\pi\) give different \(\varphi_\pm\). Physics fixes the scheme by extra conditions (locality, minimal poles, on-shell vanishing, …).


## Against the screen


A Birkhoff decomposition needs:


1. a connected filtered Hopf algebra,
2. a character with values in a split algebra,
3. a reason to throw away \(A_-\).


v1.3 supplies none of these. The slip identity is already finite. Fourier coefficients of \(W\) are already defined. \(\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)\) is a convergent numerical series, not a meromorphic loop. There is no regulator \(z\), no character of \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\), and no polar part to subtract.


One could *declare* a Hopf algebra of tree expansions for an ODE on the torus and a character given by a divergent B-series, then Birkhoff-decompose it. That would be a new construction. It is not a reading of the golden coboundary or of \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\).


The usable sentence: Birkhoff decomposition is unique factorization \(\varphi=\varphi_-^{*-1}*\varphi_+\) in the character group of a connected Hopf algebra, relative to a splitting of the target; in QFT that is BPHZ, and on a meromorphic loop it is Riemann–Hilbert. It attaches to the 39-screen only if someone writes a character to decompose.
The Connes–Kreimer Hopf algebra is the commutative graded Hopf algebra of rooted forests with the admissible-cut coproduct. It is the combinatorial skeleton of BPHZ renormalization and of Butcher’s B-series.


## The algebra


Generators are rooted trees. A **forest** is a finite disjoint union of trees (the empty forest is \(1\)). \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}=\mathbb k[\mathcal T]\) is the polynomial algebra on trees, equivalently the span of forests with disjoint union as product. Grading is by number of vertices. The algebra is connected: the degree-zero piece is just \(\mathbb k\cdot 1\).


Decorated trees (vertices labelled by residues, or by primitive Feynman graphs) give the versions used in an actual QFT. The undecorated algebra is the universal skeleton.


## Coproduct


An **admissible cut** of a tree \(t\) is a set of edges meeting every root-to-leaf path in at most one edge. Deleting those edges splits \(t\) into a **pruning** \(P_c(t)\) (a forest of discarded crowns) and a **trunk** \(R_c(t)\) (the component of the root).


\[
\Delta t = t\otimes 1 + 1\otimes t + \sum_{c\text{ admissible}} P_c(t)\otimes R_c(t).
\]
Coassociativity is the coherence of two successive cuts. The counit vanishes on every nonempty forest. The antipode exists because the Hopf algebra is graded and connected; recursively it is Bogoliubov’s formula.


Equivalent cocycle form: if \(B_+\) hangs a forest under a new root,
\[
\Delta B_+(F)=B_+(F)\otimes 1+(id\otimes B_+)\Delta F.
\]


## Dual, pre-Lie, enveloping algebra


The graded dual is Grossman–Larson: trees with a noncommutative product that grafts one tree onto every vertex of another. That grafting product is **pre-Lie**. Its commutator is a Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}}\), and
\[
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\;\simeq\; U(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}})^\circ
\]
as graded Hopf algebras. Free pre-Lie on one generator *is* the span of undecorated rooted trees; CK is its enveloping algebra on the dual side.


## Characters and Birkhoff factorization


An algebra map \(\varphi:H\to A\) into a commutative algebra \(A\) is a **character**. Convolution of characters is composition of the expansions they represent (B-series composition; insertion of counterterms).


Given a splitting \(A=A_-\oplus A_+\) with \(1\in A_+\) (a renormalization scheme), every character factors uniquely
\[
\varphi=\varphi_-^{*-1}*\varphi_+,\qquad
\varphi_\pm\text{ characters},\quad
\varphi_\pm(\ker\varepsilon)\subset A_\pm.
\]
On primitives this is “subtract the \(A_-\) piece of \(\varphi\).” On higher trees the left factor \(\varphi_-\) recursively subtracts all admissible prunings — Zimmermann’s forest formula, written in the group \(G(A)\).


In dimensional regularization, \(A\) is meromorphic germs in the regulator \(z\), \(A_-\) is polar parts, \(A_+\) is holomorphic germs, and \(\varphi_+\big|_{z=0}\) is the MS-renormalized value. The same factorization, read on a loop \(z\mapsto\varphi(z)\in G(\mathbb C)\), is a Riemann–Hilbert problem on the character group.


The graded automorphism group of \(H\) produces the Connes–Kreimer **beta function** as the infinitesimal failure of \(\varphi_-\) to be scale-invariant.


## Two readings of the same Hopf algebra


| Reading | Character | Convolution | Birkhoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| QFT | regularized Feynman rules | insertion of subgraphs | BPHZ counterterms |
| Numerical ODE | a B-series / Runge–Kutta method | composition of methods | (usually unused; the group is already the Butcher group) |


Kreimer’s Hopf algebra of *graphs* maps onto a decorated CK by retracting graphs to trees of nested divergences. Connes–Moscovici’s Hopf algebra of transverse geometry contains a copy of the undecorated CK in dimension 1.


## What it organizes, and what it does not


It organizes *composition of tree expansions*. It does not choose a Lagrangian, a regulator, or a subtraction scheme — those are the character and the splitting of \(A\). It does not Borel-sum a series in \(\hbar\); aliens do that on a different plane. It does not classify parabolic germs; Écalle–Voronin does that with horn maps.


## Against Geometry Maximization


CK attaches when a quantity is a sum over rooted trees and composition of those sums is convolution. The golden-screen identities are not of that form:


- \(\sigma-\tau=\rho\circ T-\rho\) is one coboundary, not a forest series;
- \(\mathcal B_Y(\alpha)=3\log\varphi\) is an ordinary numerical series;
- Fourier division of the cohomological equation is linear inversion, not a character factorization;
- \(\mu_s\), Loewner generators, \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\), and IS horn maps live in other categories.


Declaring a B-series for an ODE on the torus would produce a CK character. That would be a new construction, not a theorem already in v1.3. Until then the Hopf algebra stays in the same qualified glossary as pre-Lie, moulds, and Birkhoff: the algebra of cutting trees, not a property of golden rotation.
The Connes–Moscovici Hopf algebra \(\mathcal H_n\) is the universal algebraic object that encodes **affine transverse geometry** of codimension-\(n\) foliations. In codimension 1 it contains a copy of the Connes–Kreimer algebra of rooted trees.


## Geometric origin


A foliation of codimension \(n\) has a transverse \(\mathbb R^n\). Diffomorphisms of the transverse space act on jets of frames. The Lie algebra of formal vector fields on \(\mathbb R^n\), together with the action of the affine group, produces a Hopf algebra that acts on the algebra of transverse differential forms / jets. Connes–Moscovici built \(\mathcal H_n\) so that:


- its action on that jet algebra is compatible with the product (a module-algebra structure);
- Hopf-cyclic cohomology of \(\mathcal H_n\) computes the transverse characteristic classes;
- the local index formula for a transversally hypoelliptic operator (the CM signature operator) takes values in that cyclic cohomology.


The construction is Diff-invariant, hence “background independent” in their later phrasing: the space of leaves is treated as a noncommutative space, not as a manifold with a chosen transverse metric.


## The algebra \(\mathcal H_1\) (codimension 1)


As an algebra, \(\mathcal H_1\) is the enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra spanned by \(Y\), \(X\), and \(\delta_n\) (\(n\ge 1\)) with
\[
[Y,X]=X,\qquad
[Y,\delta_n]=n\delta_n,\qquad
[\delta_n,\delta_m]=0,\qquad
[X,\delta_n]=\delta_{n+1}.
\]
So \(Y\) is a Euler / scaling generator, \(X\) is a transverse derivative that raises the index of \(\delta\), and the \(\delta_n\) commute among themselves and are eigenvectors of \(Y\).


Coproduct on generators:
\[
\Delta Y=Y\otimes 1+1\otimes Y,
\]
\[
\Delta\delta_1=\delta_1\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_1,
\]
\[
\Delta X=X\otimes 1+1\otimes X+\delta_1\otimes Y.
\]
\(\Delta\delta_n\) is then forced by multiplicativity and \([X,\delta_{n-1}]=\delta_n\). It has the form
\[
\Delta\delta_n=\delta_n\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_n+R_{n-1},
\]
with \(R_{n-1}\) in the augmentation ideal of the algebra generated by \(\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_{n-1}\). Explicitly,
\[
\Delta\delta_2=\delta_2\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_2+\delta_1\otimes\delta_1,
\]
\[
\Delta\delta_3=\delta_3\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_3+3\delta_1\otimes\delta_2+\delta_2\otimes\delta_1+\delta_1^2\otimes\delta_1,
\]
and a closed combinatorial formula for general \(n\) is in van Osdol / Moscovici-type expansions (factorials times tree-like compositions of \(\delta\)’s). An antipode exists; it is recursive on the same filtration.


A character \(\delta:\mathcal H_1\to\mathbb C\) with \(\delta(Y)=1\), \(\delta(X)=\delta(\delta_n)=0\) supplies the modular pair for Hopf-cyclic cohomology.


\(\mathcal H_n\) for \(n>1\) is the analogous enveloping algebra for formal vector fields on \(\mathbb R^n\), with a larger family of \(\delta\)-generators indexed by multi-indices.


## Relation to Connes–Kreimer


The commutative subalgebra generated by the \(\delta_n\) inside \(\mathcal H_1\), with the coproduct induced from \(\Delta\delta_n\), is isomorphic to a Hopf subalgebra of \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\). Connes–Kreimer identified it with the subalgebra of **ladders** (or, depending on normalization, with the image of the growth operator / natural growth of trees). Agarwala–Delaney built a genuine enlargement of \(\mathcal H_1\) that contains *all* of \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\), so that Hochschild / cyclic cohomology of the tree algebra can be studied by CM methods.


The inclusion is one-way in the original paper: CM contains some trees, not all of them. The geometric generators \(X,Y\) have no counterpart in undecorated CK; they are the affine transverse frame.


## Hopf-cyclic cohomology


Ordinary cyclic cohomology of an algebra does not see a Hopf action. Connes–Moscovici defined **Hopf-cyclic cohomology** of a Hopf algebra equipped with a modular pair \((\delta,\sigma)\). For \(\mathcal H_n\) this cohomology computes the Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology of formal vector fields (transverse characteristic classes of foliations). The characteristic map sends Hopf-cyclic cocycles of \(\mathcal H_n\) to ordinary cyclic cocycles of the crossed-product algebra of the foliation.


That is the computational machine behind the transverse index theorem: the local index formula is an explicit combination of these classes.


## What \(\mathcal H_n\) is not


- Not the Hopf algebra of a given QFT. CK / graph Hopf algebras organize Feynman diagrams; \(\mathcal H_n\) organizes jets of transverse diffeomorphisms.
- Not a Birkhoff-decomposition machine by itself. One *can* take characters of \(\mathcal H_1\) with values in a split algebra and factor them; that is not the original use.
- Not Écalle–Voronin. Formal vector fields appear in both stories, but EV classifies a *parabolic germ*, while \(\mathcal H_n\) classifies *transverse affine geometry of a foliation*.


## Against Geometry Maximization


A foliation, a transverse \(\mathbb R^n\), and a jet algebra are not present in v1.3. The golden rotation lives on a circle, not as the leaf space of a codimension-1 foliation with a CM action.


Points of contact that look tempting and fail:


- \(Y\) scales like a Euler field; the Gauss map scales \(\alpha\). Different objects.
- \(\delta_n\) raise index like a derivative of a generating function. The Fourier modes of \(W\) already solve the cohomological equation without a Hopf coproduct.
- Hopf-cyclic classes are characteristic classes of foliations, not occupation measures \(\mu_s\).


One *could* view the torus as a single leaf of a trivial foliation and write a jet expansion of \(h\) in v1.3’s conjugacy \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\). That expansion would be a character of a jet algebra, hence of something like \(\mathcal H_1\). It is not a computation already in the notes, and the CM coproduct would not compute the slip coboundary.


The accurate sentence: \(\mathcal H_n\) is the Hopf algebra of affine transverse jets of a codimension-\(n\) foliation; \(\mathcal H_1\) contains a CK subalgebra of ladders; its cyclic cohomology is transverse characteristic classes. It belongs in the same qualified glossary as CK — a Hopf algebra of a different geometry, not a structure of the 39-screen.


Hopf-cyclic cohomology is cyclic cohomology of a Hopf algebra, twisted by a modular pair so that the cyclic operator squares correctly when \(S^2\neq\mathrm{id}\). Connes–Moscovici introduced it to compute transverse characteristic classes of foliations.


## Modular pair


Let \(H\) be a Hopf algebra over \(k\supset\mathbb Q\). A **modular pair** is


- a character \(\delta:H\to k\) (algebra map, \(\delta(1)=1\));
- a grouplike \(\sigma\in H\) (\(\Delta\sigma=\sigma\otimes\sigma\), \(\varepsilon(\sigma)=1\));


with \(\delta(\sigma)=1\).


The **twisted antipode** is
\[
\tilde S_\delta(h)=\delta(h_{(1)})S(h_{(2)}).
\]
The pair is **in involution** when
\[
\tilde S_\delta^2(h)=\sigma h\sigma^{-1}\qquad\text{for all }h\in H.
\]
Without involution the would-be cyclic operator fails \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\).


Examples:


- Any commutative or cocommutative Hopf algebra: \(S^2=\mathrm{id}\), so \((\varepsilon,1)\) works.
- \(\mathcal H_1\): \(\delta(Y)=1\), \(\delta(X)=\delta(\delta_n)=0\), \(\sigma=1\). This is the original nontrivial pair.


## The cocyclic module


Set \(C^0=k\) and \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\) for \(n\ge 1\). Faces, degeneracies, and the cyclic operator are the standard ones on a Hopf algebra, twisted by \((\delta,\sigma)\). The only face that sees the pair is the last one; the cyclic operator is
\[
\tau_n(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)
=\tilde S_\delta(h_1)\cdot(h_2\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n\otimes\sigma),
\]
where \(\cdot\) is the diagonal adjoint action
\[
h\cdot(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)=h_{(1)}h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{(n)}h_n.
\]
Connes–Moscovici: these operators satisfy the cocyclic identities if and only if \((\delta,\sigma)\) is in involution. The cohomology of that cocyclic module is
\[
\mathrm{HC}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H).
\]
There is a periodic theory \(\mathrm{HP}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\) and an \(SBI\) sequence relating Hochschild and cyclic, as in ordinary cyclic cohomology. Hopf–Hochschild cohomology can also be written as a derived functor of bicomodules.


## Characteristic map


Let \(A\) be an \(H\)-module algebra, and let \(\tau:A\to k\) be a \(\delta\)-invariant \(\sigma\)-trace:
\[
\tau(h(a))=\delta(h)\tau(a),\qquad
\tau(ab)=\tau\bigl(b\,\sigma(a)\bigr).
\]
The **characteristic map**
\[
\chi_\tau:H^{\otimes n}\to\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\otimes(n+1)},k),
\]
\[
\chi_\tau(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)(a_0,\ldots,a_n)
=\tau\bigl(a_0\,h_1(a_1)\cdots h_n(a_n)\bigr)
\]
sends Hopf-cyclic cochains to ordinary cyclic cochains of \(A\), and induces
\[
\chi_\tau^\ast:\mathrm{HC}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\to\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(A).
\]
This is the device that turns a universal class on \(H\) into a concrete cyclic cocycle on the crossed product of a foliation (or on any module-algebra with a twisted trace).


## What the classes of \(\mathcal H_1\) are


On \(\mathcal H_1\) with the modular pair above:


- \(\delta_1\) is a Hopf-cyclic **1-cocycle**. Its class generates \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{odd}}(\mathcal H_1)\) and maps to the **Godbillon–Vey** class of a codimension-1 foliation.
- A second generator \([\Pi]\) spans \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{even}}(\mathcal H_1)\) and is the Connes–Chern character of the transverse spectral triple.


Hopf-cyclic cohomology of \(\mathcal H_n\) is, under the expected identifications, Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology of formal vector fields on \(\mathbb R^n\) — the transverse characteristic classes. The local index formula for the CM hypoelliptic signature operator is an explicit combination of these classes, pushed forward by \(\chi_\tau\).


## Variants


- **Relative** Hopf-cyclic cohomology for a Hopf pair \((H,K)\), including noncompact isotropy (Connes–Moscovici 2005 survey).
- Coefficients in a SAYD module (stable anti-Yetter–Drinfeld): Hajac–Khalkhali–Rangipour–Sommerhäuser, which recovers the original theory when the module is the modular pair itself.
- Dual Hopf-cyclic *homology* for coalgebra-type objects (quantum groups and their duals; ribbon / coribbon algebras).


The theory applies to any Hopf algebra that admits a modular pair in involution — quantum groups included — not only to \(\mathcal H_n\).


## What it is not


- Not ordinary cyclic cohomology of \(H\) as an algebra. The cochains are tensors of *elements of \(H\)*, not cochains on \(H\).
- Not Hochschild cohomology of a module-algebra (though \(\chi_\tau\) lands there).
- Not a Birkhoff factorization. Characters of \(H\) with values in a split algebra can still be factored; that uses the algebra structure of \(H\), not \(\mathrm{HC}_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\).
- Not Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology until one identifies \(\mathcal H_n\) with the Hopf algebra of formal vector fields.


## Against the screen


Hopf-cyclic cohomology needs a Hopf algebra, a modular pair in involution, and — to produce numbers on a geometric algebra — a module-algebra plus a twisted trace.


v1.3 has none of these. The circle rotation is not an \(\mathcal H_1\)-module algebra in any construction written in the notes. The slip coboundary is not a cyclic cocycle. Godbillon–Vey is a class of foliations; the golden torus is not presented as a leaf space.


A later source *could* let \(\mathcal H_1\) act on jets of \(h\) in \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) and push \([\delta_1]\) forward. That would be a new characteristic class of the conjugacy, not a computation already present, and it would not evaluate the 39-bin occupation.


The accurate sentence: Hopf-cyclic cohomology is the cyclic theory of a Hopf algebra twisted by a modular pair in involution; for \(\mathcal H_1\) its odd generator is Godbillon–Vey. It stays with CM and CK in the qualified glossary.
A **stable anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module** (SAYD) is the correct coefficient object for Hopf-cyclic (co)homology once one leaves the rank-one modular pair \((\delta,\sigma)\). Hajac–Khalkhali–Rangipour–Sommerhäuser introduced them as the modules for which the cyclic operator still works.


## Yetter–Drinfeld, then the sign flip


A (left-left) **Yetter–Drinfeld module** over a Hopf algebra \(H\) is a vector space \(M\) that is both a left \(H\)-module and a left \(H\)-comodule, with the compatibility
\[
(h\cdot m)_{(-1)}\otimes(h\cdot m)_{(0)}
=h_{(1)}m_{(-1)}S(h_{(3)})\otimes h_{(2)}\cdot m_{(0)}.
\]
YD modules are the objects of the Drinfeld centre of \(\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}H\): the action and coaction braid rather than obstruct each other. They are the coefficients of *ordinary* Hopf–Hochschild cohomology, not of the cyclic theory.


An **anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module** uses the opposite braiding — the antipode appears on the other side, equivalently one replaces \(H\) by \(H^{\mathrm{cop}}\) or \(H^{\mathrm{op}}\) in one of the two structures:
\[
(h\cdot m)_{(-1)}\otimes(h\cdot m)_{(0)}
=h_{(1)}m_{(-1)}S^{-1}(h_{(3)})\otimes h_{(2)}\cdot m_{(0)}
\]
(up to the usual left/right variants). The “anti” is that sign in the braiding, not a sign on \(M\).


## Stability


AYD is not enough for a cyclic operator. **Stability** is the extra identity
\[
m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\qquad\text{for all }m\in M.
\]
The coaction, followed by the action, is the identity. In rank one this is exactly \(\delta(\sigma)=1\) together with the involution condition on \(\tilde S_\delta\): the modular pair \((\delta,\sigma)\) makes \(k\) into a 1-dimensional SAYD module, with action \(h\cdot\lambda=\delta(h)\lambda\) and coaction \(\lambda\mapsto\sigma\otimes\lambda\).


Without stability, \(\tau^{n+1}\) fails on cochains with values in \(M\). With stability, \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\) (or the cohomological dual arrangement) becomes a cocyclic module, and one obtains
\[
\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M),\qquad\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(H,M).
\]
When \(M=k_{\delta,\sigma}\), this recovers Connes–Moscovici’s original groups.


## Where examples come from


- Modular pairs in involution: the 1-dimensional SAYD modules.
- Hopf–Galois extensions, with a *flipped* Miyashita–Ulbrich action. The coinvariants of a Galois extension produce AYD modules; stability is an extra check.
- Duals and opposites: if \(M\) is YD over \(H\), suitable twists (antipode on one structure, opposite multiplication) produce AYD over \(H\) or \(H^{\mathrm{op}}\).
- Lie–Hopf algebras: Rangipour–Sutlu give a correspondence between SAYD modules over a bicrossed-sum Lie algebra and SAYD modules over the associated Hopf algebra, plus a van Est isomorphism at the first page of a spectral sequence.
- Restriction: \(\mathcal H_1\) has **no finite-dimensional SAYD module** other than the original 1-dimensional modular pair (Rangipour–Sutlu, using the Lie algebra of formal vector fields). So for Connes–Moscovici one cannot enrich the coefficients in finite dimensions. They do construct a 4-dimensional SAYD module over the related Schwarzian Hopf algebra.


Hassanzadeh–Kucerovsky–Rangipour later defined two larger coefficient categories, of which SAYD is the smallest; the middle layer depends on both \(H\) and the algebra being acted on.


## Relation to the Drinfeld double


YD modules are modules over the Drinfeld double \(D(H)\). AYD modules are modules over a close cousin (a twist of the double, or modules over the double of \(H^{\mathrm{cop}}\)). Stability is not a double-module condition; it is a trace-type constraint on the two structures together, the analogue of \(\delta(\sigma)=1\).


## What SAYD modules are for


They let Hopf-cyclic cohomology take coefficients, so that:


- different traces / weights on a module-algebra \(A\) correspond to different SAYD modules, and the characteristic map
  \[
  \chi_{\tau,M}:\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M)\to\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(A)
  \]
  still exists;
- one can change coefficients and still have an \(SBI\) sequence;
- quasi-Hopf and Hopf-algebroid versions have a place to put coefficients (Kobyzev–Shapiro, and the contramodule variant).


They do not compute new numbers by themselves. A choice of \(M\) is a choice of twisting; the classes still have to be calculated.


## Against the screen


SAYD is a coefficient category for a Hopf-cyclic complex. It becomes relevant only after one has a Hopf algebra acting on something. v1.3 has no such action and no module \(M\). The 1-dimensional modular pair of \(\mathcal H_1\) is already the only finite-dimensional SAYD module there, and even that pair is not attached to the golden conjugacy in the notes.


The honest sentence: SAYD modules are stable anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules, the coefficients that make Hopf-cyclic cohomology work beyond a modular pair. They stay with \(\mathcal H_n\) and CK in the qualified glossary.
Hopf-cyclic cohomology is used wherever a Hopf algebra plays the role of a structure group and one wants characteristic classes or an index formula compatible with that action.


## 1. Transverse index theory (the original application)


This is why the theory exists.


A transversally hypoelliptic operator on a foliation — the Connes–Moscovici signature operator on the bundle of transverse frames — has a local index formula whose terms are commutators with \(D^2\). Those commutators generate \(\mathcal H_n\). Organizing them as Hopf-cyclic cochains, then identifying
\[
\mathrm{HP}^\bullet_{(\delta,1)}(\mathcal H_n)\;\simeq\; H^\bullet_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n)
\]
with Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology of formal vector fields, converts the local formula into a finite combination of transverse characteristic classes. The characteristic map \(\chi_\tau\) pushes those classes to cyclic cocycles on the crossed product of the foliation. That is the transverse index theorem.


Low-dimensional generators:


- \([\delta_1]\in\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{odd}}(\mathcal H_1)\) \(\mapsto\) Godbillon–Vey;
- \([\Pi]\in\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{even}}(\mathcal H_1)\) \(\mapsto\) transverse fundamental class / Connes–Chern character of the spectral triple.


Moscovici later gave a Chern–Weil-style geometric construction of all Hopf-cyclic classes of \(\mathcal H_n\) and of \(\mathcal H_n\) relative to \(O(n)\), so the identification with foliation classes is explicit, not only cohomological.


Relative Hopf-cyclic cohomology handles the pair \((\mathcal H_n,U(\mathfrak o_n))\) and noncompact isotropy.


## 2. Characteristic classes of Hopf actions


Once \(\mathrm{HC}_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\) is defined for an arbitrary Hopf algebra with a modular pair, any module-algebra \(A\) with a twisted trace receives classes via \(\chi_\tau\). Applications:


- actions of quantum groups on algebras (ribbon and coribbon Hopf algebras admit modular pairs);
- cup products in Hopf-cyclic cohomology (Khalkhali–Rangipour), so one can multiply classes coming from two Hopf actions;
- SAYD coefficients, so different weights / KMS states give different coefficient modules and different characteristic maps.


This is the “Hopf algebra as structure group” principle: Lie-algebra cohomology is replaced by \(\mathrm{HC}(H)\), group cohomology by the same when \(H=kG\).


## 3. Quantum groups and van Est


Rangipour–Sutlu: for a Lie–Hopf algebra (bicrossed sum of a Lie algebra and a Hopf algebra of functions), SAYD modules correspond on both sides and a van Est isomorphism identifies Lie-algebra cohomology of the bicrossed sum with Hopf-cyclic cohomology of the Hopf algebra, at the first page of a spectral sequence. Concrete output: a 4-dimensional SAYD module over the Schwarzian Hopf algebra and explicit representing cocycles.


\(\mathcal H_1\) itself has no other finite-dimensional SAYD module than the original line — one cannot enrich CM coefficients in finite dimensions.


## 4. Background-independent geometry


Because \(\mathcal H_n\) and its action are Diff-invariant, Connes–Moscovici proposed the same package as a model of geometry without a background metric — a possible language for the background-independence problem in quantum gravity. That is a programme, not a theorem: the Hopf-cyclic classes are defined; a gravitational theory built from them is not.


## 5. What the applications share


Every application has:


1. a Hopf algebra \(H\) acting on an algebra \(A\);
2. a modular pair (or a SAYD module);
3. a twisted trace on \(A\);
4. a reason to want characteristic classes or an index pairing.


Missing any of (1)–(4), there is nothing to pair.


## What they do not replace


- Ordinary cyclic cohomology of \(A\) still computes the pairing with \(K\)-theory; Hopf-cyclic *feeds* it through \(\chi_\tau\).
- Connes–Kreimer Birkhoff factorization organizes counterterms of a character of a combinatorial Hopf algebra; it does not use \(\mathrm{HC}(H)\).
- Gelfand–Fuchs is the identification for \(\mathcal H_n\), not a general computation scheme for an arbitrary \(H\).
- Écalle–Voronin, Loewner, \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cyl}}\) remain in other categories.


## Against Geometry Maximization


The golden screen does not present a foliation, a transverse frame bundle, a hypoelliptic operator, or an \(\mathcal H_n\)-action. Godbillon–Vey is a class of codimension-1 foliations; the rigid rotation on \(\mathbb T\) is not that foliation.


Khalkhali–Shapiro note that ordinary Hochschild cohomology of the Kronecker-foliation algebra *does* see the Diophantine nature of the rotation number, while periodic cyclic cohomology cancels it down to the expected dimensions. That is a theorem about the *noncommutative torus / Kronecker foliation*, not about v1.3’s warped screen, and it uses ordinary cyclic theory of an algebra, not Hopf-cyclic theory of \(\mathcal H_1\).


A later construction could let \(\mathcal H_1\) act on jets of the conjugacy \(h\) and push \([\delta_1]\) forward. That would be a new Godbillon–Vey-type class of that conjugacy, not a number already in §§25–28, and it would not evaluate slip occupation or \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\).


The usable sentence: Hopf-cyclic cohomology computes transverse characteristic classes and indices for Hopf actions; its founding application is the CM transverse index theorem. It attaches to the 39-screen only if someone writes the missing action and trace.
SAYD modules are the coefficient category that makes Hopf-cyclic cohomology work for a general Hopf algebra, not just for a rank-one modular pair.


## The two structures


Fix a Hopf algebra \(H\) over \(k\supset\mathbb Q\), with invertible antipode. A vector space \(M\) is a **left-left anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module** if it is a left \(H\)-module and a left \(H\)-comodule satisfying
\[
(h\cdot m)_{(-1)}\otimes(h\cdot m)_{(0)}
=h_{(1)}\,m_{(-1)}\,S^{-1}(h_{(3)})\otimes h_{(2)}\cdot m_{(0)}.
\]
(There are three other side conventions; they are equivalent up to \(H^{\mathrm{op}}\) / \(H^{\mathrm{cop}}\).) The same formula with \(S\) instead of \(S^{-1}\) is a Yetter–Drinfeld module — the object of the Drinfeld centre of \(\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}H\). The “anti” is the opposite braiding.


**Stability:**
\[
m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\qquad\text{for all }m\in M.
\]
Action after coaction is the identity. An AYD module that satisfies this is a **stable anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module**.


The rank-one case is a modular pair in involution: \(M=k\), action \(h\cdot\lambda=\delta(h)\lambda\), coaction \(\lambda\mapsto\sigma\otimes\lambda\). Stability is \(\delta(\sigma)=1\); the AYD identity is \(\tilde S_\delta^2=\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma\).


## Why these are the coefficients


On cochains \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\) (or the dual arrangement for homology), faces and degeneracies use the module and comodule structures. The cyclic operator uses the anti-YD braiding to move the last factor around, and stability to guarantee
\[
\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}.
\]
Without stability one has a para-cocyclic object; with it, a cocyclic module. Its cohomology is Hopf-cyclic cohomology with coefficients,
\[
\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M),\qquad\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(H,M).
\]
For \(M=k_{\delta,\sigma}\) this is Connes–Moscovici. The \(SBI\) sequence survives. A characteristic map
\[
\chi_{\tau,M}:\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M)\to\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(A)
\]
exists when \(A\) is an \(H\)-module algebra equipped with an \(M\)-valued twisted trace.


## Sources of examples


| Source | What you get |
|---|---|
| Modular pair in involution | 1-dimensional SAYD |
| Hopf–Galois extension + flipped Miyashita–Ulbrich action | AYD on the coinvariants; stability extra |
| Twist of a YD module (antipode on one structure) | AYD over \(H\) or \(H^{\mathrm{op}}\) |
| Lie–Hopf / bicrossed sum | correspondence with SAYD over the Lie algebra (Rangipour–Sutlu) |
| Schwarzian Hopf algebra | an explicit 4-dimensional SAYD module |


**Rigidity of \(\mathcal H_1\).** Connes–Moscovici’s Hopf algebra admits no finite-dimensional SAYD module other than the original line. The proof goes through the Lie algebra of formal vector fields on the line: finite-dimensional SAYD modules there are exhausted by the modular character. So one cannot enrich the founding example in finite dimensions.


Hassanzadeh–Kucerovsky–Rangipour interpolated two larger coefficient categories between “arbitrary modules” and SAYD; the middle layer depends on both \(H\) and the algebra \(A\) being acted on. All three layers are distinct.


## Relation to the Drinfeld double


YD modules are \(D(H)\)-modules. AYD modules are modules over a twist of the double (or over \(D(H^{\mathrm{cop}})\)). Stability is not a double-module axiom; it is a trace-type constraint on the two structures together.


For quasi-Hopf algebras the YD condition is read off the centre of the monoidal category of modules; AYD and SAYD have to be written by hand (Kobyzev–Shapiro). There is a parallel theory of anti-Yetter–Drinfeld *contramodules* for the homological side.


## What they are for, and what they are not


They let one vary the weight / KMS state / modular character without leaving Hopf-cyclic theory. They do not compute classes by themselves: a choice of \(M\) is a choice of coefficients, after which the complex still has to be calculated.


They do not replace ordinary cyclic cohomology of an algebra, Birkhoff factorization of characters, Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology, or Écalle–Voronin moduli.


## Against the screen


A SAYD module is a pair of compatible \(H\)-structures on a vector space. It becomes relevant only after a Hopf algebra is chosen. v1.3 has no \(H\), no action, and no \(M\). The unique finite-dimensional SAYD module of \(\mathcal H_1\) is the modular line already used for Godbillon–Vey; attaching that line to jets of \(h\) in \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) would be a new construction, not a reading of the slip coboundary or of \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\).


The accurate sentence: SAYD modules are stable anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules over a Hopf algebra — the coefficients of Hopf-cyclic (co)homology. They stay with \(\mathcal H_n\) in the qualified glossary.
The Connes–Moscovici Hopf algebra \(\mathcal H_n\) is the universal Hopf algebra of **affine transverse jets** for a foliation of codimension \(n\). It is the structure group of the “space of leaves” in noncommutative geometry, and the home of Hopf-cyclic cohomology in the transverse index theorem.


## What problem it solves


A foliation of codimension \(n\) has a transverse \(\mathbb R^n\). Diffeomorphisms of that transverse space act on jets of frames. To write a local index formula for a *transversally* hypoelliptic operator one must organize an infinite family of those jets. \(\mathcal H_n\) is the Hopf algebra generated by that family, built so that:


- it acts on the algebra of transverse jets as a module-algebra;
- its Hopf-cyclic cohomology is Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology of formal vector fields on \(\mathbb R^n\);
- the local index formula becomes a finite combination of those classes, pushed to the crossed product of the foliation by the characteristic map.


The construction is Diff-invariant: no background transverse metric is chosen.


## The algebra \(\mathcal H_1\)


As an algebra, \(\mathcal H_1\) is the enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra with basis \(Y\), \(X\), \(\delta_n\) (\(n\ge 1\)) and brackets
\[
[Y,X]=X,\qquad
[Y,\delta_n]=n\delta_n,\qquad
[\delta_n,\delta_m]=0,\qquad
[X,\delta_n]=\delta_{n+1}.
\]
\(Y\) scales, \(X\) differentiates and raises the index of \(\delta\), the \(\delta_n\) commute.


Coproduct on generators:
\[
\Delta Y=Y\otimes 1+1\otimes Y,
\]
\[
\Delta\delta_1=\delta_1\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_1,
\]
\[
\Delta X=X\otimes 1+1\otimes X+\delta_1\otimes Y.
\]
\(\Delta\delta_n\) is then determined by \(\delta_n=[X,\delta_{n-1}]\) and multiplicativity:
\[
\Delta\delta_n=\delta_n\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_n+R_{n-1},
\]
with \(R_{n-1}\) in the augmentation ideal of \(k[\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_{n-1}]\). The first cases are
\[
\Delta\delta_2=\delta_2\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_2+\delta_1\otimes\delta_1,
\]
\[
\Delta\delta_3=\delta_3\otimes 1+1\otimes\delta_3+3\delta_1\otimes\delta_2+\delta_2\otimes\delta_1+\delta_1^2\otimes\delta_1.
\]
An antipode exists by the connected filtration. A character \(\delta(Y)=1\), \(\delta(X)=\delta(\delta_n)=0\), with grouplike \(\sigma=1\), is a modular pair in involution — the unique finite-dimensional SAYD module of \(\mathcal H_1\).


\(\mathcal H_n\) is the same construction for formal vector fields on \(\mathbb R^n\), with a larger family of \(\delta\)-generators.


## Relation to Connes–Kreimer


The commutative subalgebra generated by the \(\delta_n\), with the coproduct inherited from \(\Delta\delta_n\), embeds into \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\) as a Hopf subalgebra of **ladders** (iterated growth of a single branch). Connes–Kreimer identified that copy. Agarwala–Delaney later enlarged \(\mathcal H_1\) so that it contains *all* of \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{CK}}\). The geometric generators \(X,Y\) have no counterpart in undecorated CK; they are the affine transverse frame.


So: CK is the Hopf algebra of cutting forests; \(\mathcal H_1\) is the Hopf algebra of scaling-plus-differentiating a transverse coordinate, and it happens to contain some trees.


## Cohomology and index


Hopf-cyclic cohomology of \(\mathcal H_1\) with the modular pair above is periodic of period 2:


- \([\delta_1]\) generates the odd group and maps to **Godbillon–Vey**;
- \([\Pi]\) generates the even group and is the transverse fundamental class / Connes–Chern character of the CM spectral triple.


In every codimension,
\[
\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(\mathcal H_n)\;\simeq\; H^\bullet_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n),
\]
and Moscovici gave a Chern–Weil-style construction of the classes, absolute and relative to \(O(n)\). That identification is the computational core of the transverse index theorem.


## What it is not


- Not the Hopf algebra of a Lagrangian. Feynman graphs live in Kreimer’s graph algebra, which maps onto decorated CK, not onto \(\mathcal H_n\).
- Not a Birkhoff-decomposition machine by design. Characters of \(\mathcal H_1\) *can* be factored if one chooses a split target; that is not the original use.
- Not Écalle–Voronin. Formal vector fields appear in both stories; EV classifies a parabolic germ, \(\mathcal H_n\) classifies transverse affine geometry of a foliation.


## Against Geometry Maximization


\(\mathcal H_n\) needs a foliation, a transverse \(\mathbb R^n\), and a jet algebra. The golden rotation in v1.3 is a rigid circle map, not a leaf space. Godbillon–Vey is not the slip coboundary. \(Y\) is not the Gauss map. \(\delta_n\) are not Fourier modes of \(W\).


One *could* let \(\mathcal H_1\) act on jets of the conjugacy \(h\) in \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) and push \([\delta_1]\) forward. That would be a new characteristic class of that conjugacy, not a number already in §§25–28.


The accurate sentence: \(\mathcal H_n\) is the Hopf algebra of affine transverse jets of a codimension-\(n\) foliation; \(\mathcal H_1\) is generated by \(Y,X,\delta_n\) with the coproduct above; its odd Hopf-cyclic generator is Godbillon–Vey. It belongs with CK and SAYD in the qualified glossary, not on the 39-screen.
Hopf-cyclic cohomology is cyclic cohomology built on tensors of a Hopf algebra, twisted by a modular pair (or a SAYD module) so that the cyclic operator is well-defined when \(S^2\neq\mathrm{id}\).


## Input data


A Hopf algebra \(H\) over \(k\supset\mathbb Q\), and either


- a **modular pair in involution** \((\delta,\sigma)\): a character \(\delta:H\to k\), a grouplike \(\sigma\in H\), with \(\delta(\sigma)=1\) and
  \[
  \tilde S_\delta^2(h)=\sigma h\sigma^{-1},\qquad
  \tilde S_\delta(h)=\delta(h_{(1)})S(h_{(2)}),
  \]
- or a **SAYD module** \(M\), which generalizes that line: a left module and left comodule with the anti-Yetter–Drinfeld braiding and the stability identity \(m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\).


Commutative or cocommutative Hopf algebras always admit \((\varepsilon,1)\). \(\mathcal H_1\) admits \((\delta,1)\) with \(\delta(Y)=1\), \(\delta(X)=\delta(\delta_n)=0\), and no other finite-dimensional SAYD module.


## The cocyclic module


For the modular pair,
\[
C^0=k,\qquad C^n=H^{\otimes n}\ (n\ge 1).
\]
Faces and degeneracies are the usual Hopf-algebra ones (product, coproduct, counit), with \(\delta\) and \(\sigma\) appearing in the last face. The cyclic operator is
\[
\tau_n(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)
=\tilde S_\delta(h_1)\cdot(h_2\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n\otimes\sigma),
\]
diagonal adjoint action. These identities hold if and only if the pair is in involution. With coefficients one replaces \(C^n\) by \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\) and uses the AYD braiding plus stability to move \(M\) around.


Cohomology of this cocyclic object is \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\) or \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M)\). Periodic theory \(\mathrm{HP}^\bullet\) and an \(SBI\) sequence exist as in ordinary cyclic cohomology. Hopf–Hochschild cohomology is a derived functor of bicomodules.


## Characteristic map


If \(A\) is an \(H\)-module algebra and \(\tau:A\to k\) (or into \(M^*\)) is a \(\delta\)-invariant \(\sigma\)-trace,
\[
\tau(h(a))=\delta(h)\tau(a),\qquad
\tau(ab)=\tau(b\,\sigma(a)),
\]
then
\[
\chi_\tau(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)(a_0,\ldots,a_n)
=\tau\bigl(a_0\,h_1(a_1)\cdots h_n(a_n)\bigr)
\]
sends Hopf-cyclic cochains to ordinary cyclic cochains of \(A\), and induces
\[
\chi_\tau^\ast:\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H)\to\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(A).
\]
That is how a universal class on \(H\) becomes a number on a geometric algebra.


## What it computes for \(\mathcal H_n\)


\[
\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(\mathcal H_n)\;\simeq\; H^\bullet_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n)
\]
Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology of formal vector fields on \(\mathbb R^n\). Explicitly in codimension 1:


- \([\delta_1]\) is a Hopf-cyclic 1-cocycle (\(b\delta_1=0\), \(\tau_1(\delta_1)=-\delta_1\)), generates \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{odd}}\), and maps to **Godbillon–Vey**;
- \([\Pi]\) generates \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{even}}\) and is the transverse fundamental class.


Moscovici’s Chern–Weil construction produces all classes of \(\mathcal H_n\) and of \(\mathcal H_n\) relative to \(O(n)\). The transverse index formula is a finite linear combination of these, pushed by \(\chi_\tau\) to the crossed product of the foliation.


## Variants


- Relative theory for a Hopf pair \((H,K)\), including noncompact isotropy.
- SAYD coefficients, and two larger coefficient categories (Hassanzadeh–Kucerovsky–Rangipour).
- Cup products (Khalkhali–Rangipour).
- Dual Hopf-cyclic homology; quasi-Hopf and Hopf-algebroid versions; anti-YD contramodules.
- van Est isomorphism for Lie–Hopf algebras (Rangipour–Sutlu).


## What it is not


- Not ordinary cyclic cohomology of \(H\) as an algebra. Cochains are tensors *of elements of \(H\)*.
- Not Hochschild cohomology of the module-algebra \(A\) (though \(\chi_\tau\) lands there).
- Not Birkhoff factorization of characters of a combinatorial Hopf algebra.
- Not Gelfand–Fuchs until \(H=\mathcal H_n\).


## Against the screen


The theory needs \(H\), a modular pair or SAYD module, a module-algebra, and a twisted trace. v1.3 has none of these. Godbillon–Vey is a foliation class, not the slip coboundary. Ordinary cyclic theory of the noncommutative torus *does* see Diophantine properties of a rotation number and then cancels them in the periodic theory; that is a theorem about the Kronecker foliation algebra, not about the warped golden screen, and it is ordinary cyclic cohomology of an algebra, not Hopf-cyclic cohomology of \(\mathcal H_1\).


Attaching \(\mathcal H_1\) to jets of \(h\) in \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) and pushing \([\delta_1]\) would be a new construction. Until that is written, Hopf-cyclic cohomology stays with \(\mathcal H_n\), SAYD, and CK in the qualified glossary.
Stability is the identity that turns an anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module into a coefficient for a *cyclic* theory, not just a para-cyclic one.


## The condition


\(M\) is already a left \(H\)-module and a left \(H\)-comodule, with the anti-YD braiding. Write the coaction as
\[
m\mapsto m_{(-1)}\otimes m_{(0)}\in H\otimes M.
\]
**Stability** is
\[
m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\qquad\text{for every }m\in M.
\]
Coaction, then action, is the identity map of \(M\).


In Sweedler notation there is no hidden sum on the right-hand side: the two structures cancel exactly, not up to a scalar or a projection.


## Why it is needed


On cochains \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\), the cyclic operator \(\tau\) uses the anti-YD braiding to pass \(M\) around the tensor factors of \(H\). After \(n+1\) applications, \(M\) has travelled once around and the coaction-then-action of \(H\) on \(M\) has been applied once. For
\[
\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}
\]
that composite must be the identity. That is stability.


Without it one still has faces and degeneracies (an AYD module is enough for a para-cocyclic or precocyclic object). The cyclic identity fails, and there is no Connes \(B\)-operator, no periodic theory, and no \(SBI\) sequence in the usual form.


## Rank one


If \(M=k\), the action is a character \(\delta\), the coaction is a grouplike \(\sigma\), and stability collapses to
\[
\delta(\sigma)=1.
\]
The remaining involution condition \(\tilde S_\delta^2=\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma\) is the AYD identity on that line, not stability. Both are required for Connes–Moscovici’s original cocyclic module. Stability is the “trace-like” half; involution is the “braiding” half.


## What it is not


- Not the YD or AYD compatibility. Those relate *how* \(H\) acts and coacts on a single element; stability relates action and coaction *of \(M\) on itself*.
- Not a Drinfeld-double module axiom. Modules over \(D(H)\) or a twist of it give YD / AYD. Stability is extra.
- Not \(\delta\) being involutive. \(S^2=\mathrm{id}\) is a property of \(H\); stability is a property of \(M\).
- Not unimodularity of \(H\). Unimodularity is about the modular character of \(H\) itself.


## Geometric reading


On a Hopf–Galois extension, the flipped Miyashita–Ulbrich action produces an AYD module of coinvariants. Stability says that this action, paired with the canonical coaction, has no monodromy — every coinvariant is fixed by “act by its own coaction.” That is the analogue of a *tracial* weight rather than a KMS weight with nonzero modular automorphism. In rank one, \(\delta(\sigma)=1\) is exactly “the modular automorphism is trivial on the scalars.”


## Against the screen


Stability is a constraint on a pair of \(H\)-structures on a vector space. No such pair is present in v1.3. The modular line of \(\mathcal H_1\) is already stable by construction (\(\delta(1)=1\)). That fact does not produce a 39-bin identity or a value of \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\).


The short sentence: stability is \(m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\); it is why \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\) on cochains with values in \(M\).


Connes’s \(B\)-operator is the chain-level map that turns Hochschild cochains into cyclic cochains. Together with the Hochschild boundary \(b\) it forms the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex, whose total cohomology is cyclic cohomology.


## From the cyclic operator to \(B\)


On the Hochschild cochain space \(C^n(A)=\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\otimes(n+1)},k)\) (or on chains \(A^{\otimes(n+1)}\)) the signed cyclic permutation is
\[
\lambda\varphi(a_0,\ldots,a_n)=(-1)^n\varphi(a_n,a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}).
\]
Set
\[
N=1+\lambda+\cdots+\lambda^n.
\]
Then \(N(1-\lambda)=(1-\lambda)N=0\). The extra degeneracy that inserts a unit is
\[
s\varphi(a_0,\ldots,a_{n+1})=\varphi(1,a_0,\ldots,a_n)
\]
(or the corresponding chain-level insertion). Connes’s operator is
\[
B=Ns(1-\lambda)
\]
on cochains of degree \(n\), landing in degree \(n-1\) (homology convention: \(B:C_n\to C_{n+1}\)). An equivalent explicit formula is \(B=NB_0\) with
\[
B_0\varphi(a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1})=\varphi(1,a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1})-(-1)^n\varphi(a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1},1).
\]
On chains,
\[
B(a_0\otimes\cdots\otimes a_n)
=\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^{nj}\bigl(1\otimes a_j\otimes\cdots\otimes a_n\otimes a_0\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{j-1}
-(-1)^n a_j\otimes\cdots\otimes a_n\otimes a_0\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{j-1}\otimes 1\bigr).
\]


The algebraic identities
\[
b^2=0,\qquad B^2=0,\qquad bB+Bb=0
\]
follow from \(N(1-\lambda)=0\), \(Nb=b'N\), and \(sb'+b's=\mathrm{id}\) on the Hochschild side. So \((C^\bullet,b,B)\) is a bicomplex.


## Three complexes, one theory


1. **Cyclic bicomplex.** Columns alternate \(b\) and \(-b'\), horizontal maps alternate \(1-\lambda\) and \(N\).
2. **\((b,B)\)-bicomplex.** Columns are Hochschild complexes, horizontal maps are \(B\).
3. **Normalized cyclic cochains.** Ker\((1-\lambda)\) with differential \(b\).


They compute the same cyclic cohomology \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(A)\). The \((b,B)\)-bicomplex is the one that pairs cleanly with \(K\)-theory (JLO cocycle, Chern character) and that survives best in entire / periodic cyclic theory. Filtering by rows, the \(E_1\) page is Hochschild cohomology and the spectral sequence converges to \(\mathrm{HC}\). The periodicity operator \(S:\mathrm{HC}^n\to\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\) is implicit in that page; \(I\), \(S\), and \(B\) fit into the long exact \(SBI\) sequence
\[
\cdots\to\mathrm{HH}^n\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^n\xrightarrow{S}\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\xrightarrow{B}\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}\to\cdots.
\]


## Periodic and entire variants


Periodic cyclic cohomology is the cohomology of the product-totalization of \((b,B)\), allowing infinite cochains in both directions. Entire cyclic cohomology restricts growth of cochains so that the JLO formula
\[
\mathrm{Ch}(D)=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(e^{-tD^2}\gamma\,a_0[D,a_1]\cdots[D,a_n]\bigr)
\]
converges; Quillen’s identification of that formula with a \((b,B)\)-cocycle uses the Bianchi identity for the superconnection.


## Hopf-cyclic analogue


Once a cocyclic module is defined — ordinary cyclic cochains, or \(H^{\otimes n}\) with \(\tau_n\) built from \(\tilde S_\delta\) and \(\sigma\), or \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\) for a SAYD module — the same recipe produces a \(B\)-operator:
\[
B=N s(1-\tau).
\]
Stability of \(M\) (or involution of the modular pair) is exactly what makes \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\), hence \(N(1-\tau)=0\), hence \(B^2=0\). Without stability one can still write \(b\); one cannot write this \(B\).


On \(\mathcal H_1\), \(B\) applied to the Hochschild class of \(\delta_1\) is how one moves between the odd and even periodic groups; the generator \([\Pi]\) lives in the even slot of the same bicomplex.


## What \(B\) is not


- Not the Hochschild boundary \(b\). \(b\) contracts adjacent factors; \(B\) inserts a unit and averages over the cycle.
- Not the antipode of a Hopf algebra. \(\tilde S_\delta\) is an ingredient of \(\tau\) in the Hopf-cyclic case; \(B\) is built from \(\tau\), not identical to it.
- Not Birkhoff factorization. That is a splitting in a group of characters.
- Not an alien derivative. Aliens jump Borel-plane singularities; \(B\) cycles tensor factors of an algebra.


## Against the screen


\(B\) is an operator on cochains of an algebra (or of a cocyclic module). v1.3 has no cochain complex. The slip coboundary \(\sigma-\tau=\rho\circ T-\rho\) is an identity of functions, not a Connes \(B\) applied to a Hochschild cochain. Fourier division of \(W(y+\alpha)-W(y)=A(y)\) is inversion of \(1-\lambda\) on the circle rotation, which is formally the same letter \(\lambda\) and a different operator: there \(\lambda\) is translation by \(\alpha\), here \(\lambda\) is cyclic permutation of tensor factors.


One *could* form the cyclic cochain complex of \(C^\infty(\mathbb T)\) or of the crossed product by \(R_\alpha\), and apply \(B\). That is ordinary cyclic theory of those algebras — well-known, Diophantine-sensitive at the Hochschild page, cancelled in \(\mathrm{HP}\) — and is not a computation in Geometry Maximization.


The short sentence: \(B=Ns(1-\lambda)\) is Connes’s operator from Hochschild to cyclic cochains; with \(b\) it gives cyclic cohomology. In Hopf-cyclic theory the same formula uses \(\tau\) in place of \(\lambda\), and needs SAYD stability for \(B^2=0\).
The \(SBI\) sequence is the long exact sequence that relates Hochschild cohomology to cyclic cohomology. The three maps are Connes’s operators \(I\), \(S\), and \(B\).


## The three maps


For an associative unital algebra \(A\) (or, more generally, for any cocyclic module),


- \(I:\mathrm{HC}^n(A)\to\mathrm{HH}^n(A)\) forgets cyclicity: a cyclic class is in particular a Hochschild class. On cochains it is the inclusion of \(\ker(1-\lambda)\) into all Hochschild cochains.
- \(S:\mathrm{HC}^n(A)\to\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}(A)\) is the **periodicity operator**. In the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex it is the shift that adds two degrees by using the extra room in the total complex. On the cyclic bicomplex it is induced by the extra columns of \(N\) and \(1-\lambda\).
- \(B:\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}(A)\to\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}(A)\) is the connecting map coming from Connes’s operator \(B=Ns(1-\lambda)\) of the previous note. On periodic theory it is (up to sign) the inverse of \(S\) on the associated graded.


## The exact sequence


\[
\cdots
\to\mathrm{HH}^n(A)
\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^n(A)
\xrightarrow{S}\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}(A)
\xrightarrow{B}\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}(A)
\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^{n+1}(A)
\to\cdots
\]


Exactness at each slot is Connes’s theorem. It is the \(E_2\)-page information of the spectral sequence of the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex, written as a long exact sequence rather than as a spectral sequence: the \(E_1\) page is \(\mathrm{HH}\), the differential on that page is \(B\), and \(S\) accounts for the filtration shift of two.


There is a homological twin with arrows reversed.


## What exactness says


- A Hochschild class lifts to a cyclic class if and only if its image under the connecting \(B\) (from the previous slot) vanishes — i.e. it is killed by Connes’s \(B\) already at the cochain level, up to a Hochschild boundary.
- Periodicity \(S\) has kernel equal to the image of \(I\): the classes that die under \(S\) are those that were already Hochschild.
- Cokernel of \(S\) injects into Hochschild cohomology in one degree lower, via \(B\). So the obstruction to extending a cyclic class two degrees up is a Hochschild class.


In periodic cyclic cohomology one inverts \(S\), forming
\[
\mathrm{HP}^n(A)=\varprojlim_k\mathrm{HC}^{n+2k}(A)
\]
(or the product-totalization of \((b,B)\)). The \(SBI\) sequence then splits into two pieces, even and odd, and \(\mathrm{HP}\) is often much smaller than \(\mathrm{HC}\) because \(S\) kills a lot. That is the cancellation Khalkhali–Shapiro recalled for the Kronecker foliation: Hochschild sees the Diophantine class of the rotation number; after enough applications of \(S\), periodic cyclic cohomology has the expected dimensions \(2\) and \(2\).


## Hopf-cyclic \(SBI\)


The same sequence exists for \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\) and for \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M)\) with SAYD coefficients. The operators are built from the same letters: \(I\) includes cyclic into Hopf–Hochschild, \(S\) shifts by two, \(B=Ns(1-\tau)\) uses the Hopf-cyclic operator \(\tau\) in place of \(\lambda\). Stability of \(M\) (or involution of the pair) is what makes \(B^2=0\) and the sequence exact.


On \(\mathcal H_1\), \(I[\delta_1]\) is the Hochschild class of the primitive \(\delta_1\), \(S\) moves it toward the even generator \([\Pi]\) after one more step in the periodic theory, and \(B[\Pi]\) recovers a multiple of that Hochschild class. That is the algebraic form of “Godbillon–Vey and the transverse fundamental class generate the two periodic groups.”


## What \(SBI\) is not


- Not a Birkhoff factorization. \(S\) is a shift of cohomological degree, not a splitting of a character.
- Not the Connes–Moscovici characteristic map \(\chi_\tau\). \(\chi_\tau\) lands in ordinary \(\mathrm{HC}(A)\); \(SBI\) relates \(\mathrm{HH}\) and \(\mathrm{HC}\) of the *same* object.
- Not a spectral sequence with many pages. It is the exact couple of a two-step filtration.
- Not a statement about Brjuno or \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\). The Diophantine cancellation in \(\mathrm{HP}\) of the noncommutative torus is a different theorem, about ordinary cyclic theory of an algebra of functions, not about Hopf-cyclic theory of \(\mathcal H_1\).


## Against the screen


\(SBI\) is a relation among cohomology groups of a cocyclic module. v1.3 has no such module. The slip coboundary does not sit in \(\mathrm{HH}\) or \(\mathrm{HC}\). Fourier division inverts \(1-e^{2\pi ik\alpha}\) on functions of the circle; \(S\) inverts (up to torsion) the passage from \(\mathrm{HC}^n\) to \(\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\). Same letter “periodicity,” different operators.


Forming \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(C^\infty(\mathbb T))\) or \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(C(\mathbb T)\rtimes_{R_\alpha}\mathbb Z)\) and writing their \(SBI\) sequences is standard noncommutative geometry of the rotation. It is not a computation in Geometry Maximization, and it does not evaluate 39-bin occupation.


The short sentence: \(I\) includes cyclic into Hochschild, \(S\) shifts cyclic degree by two, \(B\) is the connecting Connes operator; exactness of \(SBI\) is the relation between \(\mathrm{HH}\) and \(\mathrm{HC}\). The same sequence holds in Hopf-cyclic theory once \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\).
A bicomplex is a grid of modules with two anticommuting differentials. Cyclic cohomology is the total cohomology of one of two such grids built from Hochschild cochains.


## The two differentials


On cochains \(C^{n}=\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\otimes(n+1)},k)\):


- \(b:C^n\to C^{n+1}\) is the Hochschild coboundary (face maps of the simplicial module). It satisfies \(b^2=0\).
- \(B:C^n\to C^{n-1}\) is Connes’s operator \(B=Ns(1-\lambda)\). It satisfies \(B^2=0\).


They anticommute:
\[
bB+Bb=0.
\]
So one can draw a grid with \(b\) vertical and \(B\) horizontal (or the reverse, depending on cohomological convention). That grid is the **\((b,B)\)-bicomplex**.


## The \((b,B)\)-grid


Columns are Hochschild complexes. Horizontal arrows are \(B\), lowering (or raising, on chains) the Hochschild degree by one while changing the filtration index.


```
        B         B
  C² ←──── C¹ ←──── C⁰
  │ b      │ b      │ b
  ▼        ▼        ▼
  C¹ ←──── C⁰
  │ b      │ b
  ▼        ▼
  C⁰
```


A total cochain of degree \(n\) is a finite sequence \((\varphi^n,\varphi^{n-2},\varphi^{n-4},\ldots)\) with \(\varphi^{n-2k}\in C^{n-2k}\). The total differential is
\[
d_{\mathrm{tot}}=b+B.
\]
Because \(b^2=B^2=bB+Bb=0\), one has \(d_{\mathrm{tot}}^2=0\). The cohomology of \((C_{\mathrm{tot}}^\bullet,d_{\mathrm{tot}})\) is cyclic cohomology \(\mathrm{HC}^n(A)\).


Periodic cyclic cohomology uses the *product* totalization: infinite sequences in both directions, \(\prod_k C^{n+2k}\). Entire cyclic cohomology restricts growth so that JLO-type series converge.


## The cyclic bicomplex (the other grid)


The same data can be arranged with \(\lambda\) visible:


- even columns: differential \(b\), horizontal map \(1-\lambda\);
- odd columns: differential \(-b'\), horizontal map \(N=1+\lambda+\cdots+\lambda^n\).


The identities \(N(1-\lambda)=(1-\lambda)N=0\), \(Nb=b'N\), \((1-\lambda)b'=b(1-\lambda)\) make this a bicomplex. Its total cohomology is again \(\mathrm{HC}\). The two grids are quasi-isomorphic; \(B=Ns(1-\lambda)\) is the chain map that identifies them.


Normalized cyclic cochains \(\ker(1-\lambda)\) with differential \(b\) are a third, smaller complex, quasi-isomorphic to both.


## Filtration and \(SBI\)


Filter the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex by rows (or by the Hochschild degree). The associated spectral sequence has


- \(E_1=\mathrm{HH}^\bullet(A)\),
- \(d_1\) induced by \(B\),
- abutment \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(A)\).


The exact couple of that filtration *is* the \(SBI\) sequence: \(I\) is the edge map into \(E_1\), \(S\) is the filtration shift by two, \(B\) is \(d_1\). Exactness of \(SBI\) is the statement that this spectral sequence has nothing essential after \(E_2\) once it is rewritten as a long exact sequence rather than as a page-by-page computation.


## Hopf-cyclic grid


Replace \(C^n=\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\otimes(n+1)},k)\) by \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\) (or \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\)), replace \(\lambda\) by the Hopf-cyclic operator \(\tau\), and keep the same letters \(b,N,s,B\). The identities survive if and only if \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\), which is involution of the modular pair or stability of the SAYD module. The resulting \((b,B)\)-bicomplex computes \(\mathrm{HC}_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\) or \(\mathrm{HC}(H,M)\).


On \(\mathcal H_1\), \(\delta_1\) sits in bidegree \((1,0)\) as a \(b\)-cocycle killed by \(1-\tau\); \(B\) and \(S\) move it toward the even generator \([\Pi]\).


## What a bicomplex is not


- Not a double complex of a Cartan–Eilenberg resolution in homological algebra *a priori* — it is a specific grid built from one simplicial object plus one cyclic operator.
- Not the Hodge filtration on de Rham cohomology, though the analogy “\(b\) like \(d\), \(B\) like the extra differential that produces periodic theory” is the one Connes intended.
- Not Birkhoff factorization, not an alien tower, not a Yoccoz return annulus.


## Against the screen


A bicomplex is a grid of cochain groups. v1.3 has functions on the circle and exact identities among them, not a double complex. The letters \(b\) and \(B\) do not appear. Forming the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex of \(C^\infty(\mathbb T)\) or of the rotation \(C^*\)-algebra is ordinary cyclic theory of those algebras, already computed in the literature, and is not a 39-bin calculation.


The short sentence: the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex has Hochschild’s \(b\) vertically and Connes’s \(B\) horizontally; its total cohomology is cyclic cohomology, and its filtration is the \(SBI\) sequence.
The \(SBI\) sequence is the long exact sequence relating Hochschild cohomology \(\mathrm{HH}\) to cyclic cohomology \(\mathrm{HC}\). The three maps are Connes’s operators \(I\), \(S\), and \(B\).


## The maps


- \(I:\mathrm{HC}^n\to\mathrm{HH}^n\) includes a cyclic class into Hochschild cohomology. Every cyclic cochain is a Hochschild cochain; \(I\) forgets the extra symmetry \(\lambda\varphi=\varphi\).
- \(S:\mathrm{HC}^n\to\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\) is **periodicity**. In the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex it is the shift of total degree by two along the extra columns. Kernel of \(S\) consists of classes that were already visible as Hochschild classes.
- \(B:\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\to\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}\) is the connecting homomorphism induced by Connes’s operator \(B=Ns(1-\lambda)\). It measures the obstruction to lifting a cyclic class two degrees further.


## The sequence


\[
\cdots
\to\mathrm{HH}^n
\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^n
\xrightarrow{S}\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}
\xrightarrow{B}\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}
\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^{n+1}
\to\cdots
\]


Exactness at each term is a theorem of Connes. It is the exact couple of the row filtration on the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex: \(E_1=\mathrm{HH}\), \(d_1=B\), the filtration shift of two is \(S\), and \(I\) is the edge map.


There is a homological twin with arrows reversed.


## How to read exactness


| Slot | Meaning |
|---|---|
| \(\ker I=\mathrm{im}\,B\) | a cyclic class dies in Hochschild iff it is \(B\) of a class two degrees up |
| \(\ker S=\mathrm{im}\,I\) | \(S\) kills exactly the classes that came from Hochschild |
| \(\ker B=\mathrm{im}\,S\) | a Hochschild class in degree \(n+1\) is \(B\) of a cyclic class iff that cyclic class is in the image of periodicity |


So \(\mathrm{HC}\) is \(\mathrm{HH}\) plus 2-periodic information. Periodic cyclic cohomology \(\mathrm{HP}\) inverts \(S\); what remains is the stable 2-periodic part. That is why \(\mathrm{HP}\) of the noncommutative torus is small and Diophantine-insensitive, while \(\mathrm{HH}\) still sees the arithmetic of the rotation number.


## Hopf-cyclic version


The same three letters and the same exact sequence exist for \(\mathrm{HC}_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\) and for \(\mathrm{HC}(H,M)\) with SAYD coefficients. The cyclic operator \(\tau\) replaces \(\lambda\); stability of \(M\) (or involution of the pair) is what makes \(B^2=0\) and the sequence exact.


On \(\mathcal H_1\): \(I[\delta_1]\) is the Hochschild class of the primitive \(\delta_1\); \(S\) and \(B\) relate it to the even generator \([\Pi]\). That is the algebraic form of “two periodic generators, Godbillon–Vey and the transverse fundamental class.”


## What it is not


- Not Birkhoff factorization of a character.
- Not the characteristic map \(\chi_\tau\) (that lands in ordinary \(\mathrm{HC}(A)\)).
- Not a many-page spectral sequence — it is a single long exact sequence coming from a two-step filtration.
- Not Yoccoz periodicity, Gauss-map periodicity, or Brjuno recurrence.


## Against the screen


\(SBI\) relates cohomology groups of a cocyclic module. v1.3 has no such groups. The slip coboundary is not a class in \(\mathrm{HH}\) or \(\mathrm{HC}\). Forming \(SBI\) for \(C^\infty(\mathbb T)\) or for the rotation algebra is standard and already computed; it is not a 39-bin identity.


The short sentence: \(I\) includes cyclic in Hochschild, \(S\) shifts cyclic degree by two, \(B\) connects back to Hochschild; exactness of that triangle is the relation between \(\mathrm{HH}\) and \(\mathrm{HC}\).
The computation is Connes’s (smooth irrational rotation algebra), cleaned up by Nest for all noncommutative tori. It is ordinary cyclic theory of an algebra, not Hopf-cyclic theory of \(\mathcal H_1\).


## The algebra


Let \(\theta\in\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\). The **smooth noncommutative torus** \(A_\theta^\infty\) is the Fréchet algebra of rapidly decreasing series
\[
a=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb Z}a_{mn}U^mV^n,\qquad
VU=e^{2\pi i\theta}UV,
\]
with Schwartz coefficients. It is the smooth subalgebra of the rotation \(C^*\)-algebra, stable under the two canonical derivations
\[
\delta_1(U)=U,\ \delta_1(V)=0,\qquad
\delta_2(U)=0,\ \delta_2(V)=V,
\]
and under the unique normalized trace \(\tau(a)=a_{00}\).


(The algebraic torus \(\mathbb C[U^{\pm1},V^{\pm1}]/(VU-\lambda UV)\) has the same periodic theory and a coarser Hochschild theory.)


## Hochschild groups


Connes; Nest. For \(\theta\) irrational, Hochschild cohomology of \(A_\theta^\infty\) vanishes above degree 2, and
\[
\mathrm{HH}^0\simeq\mathbb C,\qquad
\mathrm{HH}^1\simeq\mathbb C^2,\qquad
\mathrm{HH}^2\simeq\mathbb C.
\]
Homology is the dual picture: \(\mathrm{HH}_0\simeq\mathbb C^2\), \(\mathrm{HH}_1\simeq\mathbb C^2\), \(\mathrm{HH}_2\simeq\mathbb C\).


Generators, cohomologically:


- \(\mathrm{HH}^0\): the trace \(\tau\);
- \(\mathrm{HH}^1\): the two classes \(\psi_j(a,b)=2\pi i\,\tau(a\,\delta_j(b))\), \(j=1,2\);
- \(\mathrm{HH}^2\): the volume class
  \[
  \psi(a,b,c)=2\pi i\,\tau\bigl(a(\delta_1(b)\delta_2(c)-\delta_2(b)\delta_1(c))\bigr).
  \]


These are the noncommutative de Rham classes of \(\mathbb T^2\). For *rational* \(\theta\), extra tempered-cocycle summands appear (the algebra is Morita equivalent to \(C^\infty(\mathbb T^2)\) plus a matrix factor); \(\mathrm{HH}^n\) can be infinite-dimensional in the smooth topology. Diophantine properties of \(\theta\) enter the *smooth* Hochschild complex through the invertibility of \(1-e^{2\pi i(k\theta-\ell)}\) on Fourier modes — the same small divisors as v1.3’s cohomological equation, now on the algebra rather than on a single function. They cancel before periodic cyclic cohomology.


## Periodic cyclic groups


Nest:
\[
\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(A_\theta^\infty)\;\simeq\;\textstyle\bigwedge^\bullet\mathbb C^2.
\]
So
\[
\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{even}}\simeq\mathbb C^2,\qquad
\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{odd}}\simeq\mathbb C^2,
\]
independent of \(\theta\) as long as \(\theta\) is irrational. Even generators: the trace and the volume class. Odd generators: \(\psi_1,\psi_2\). This is the noncommutative de Rham homology of \(\mathbb T^2\).


## The \(SBI\) sequence, term by term


Write \(h_n=\dim\mathrm{HH}^n\), \(c_n=\dim\mathrm{HC}^n\). For irrational smooth \(A_\theta^\infty\),
\[
h_0=1,\ h_1=2,\ h_2=1,\ h_{n}=0\ (n\ge 3).
\]


Exactness of
\[
\cdots\to\mathrm{HH}^n\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^n\xrightarrow{S}\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\xrightarrow{B}\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}\to\cdots
\]
plus vanishing of \(\mathrm{HH}^{\ge 3}\) forces \(S:\mathrm{HC}^n\to\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\) to be eventually an isomorphism, with stable values
\[
\mathrm{HC}^{2k}\ \longrightarrow\ \mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{even}}\simeq\mathbb C^2,
\qquad
\mathrm{HC}^{2k+1}\ \longrightarrow\ \mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{odd}}\simeq\mathbb C^2.
\]


Nest’s splitting is more precise:
\[
\mathrm{HC}^n(A)\;=\;\ker S\;+\;\mathrm{im}\,S\;+\;V_n,
\qquad
V_n=\textstyle\bigwedge^n\mathbb C^2.
\]
The summand \(V_n\) is the piece that *survives all iterates of \(S\)*: it is the image of \(I\) on those Hochschild classes that are already cyclic and remain cyclic after every \(S\). Those are exactly \(\tau\), \(\psi_1,\psi_2\), and \(\psi\), sitting in degrees \(0,1,2\). Everything else in \(\mathrm{HC}^n\) is either in \(\ker S\) (transient Hochschild junk) or in \(\mathrm{im}\,S\) (periodicity copies of lower classes).


Concretely, the first few slots:


- \(I:\mathrm{HC}^0\to\mathrm{HH}^0\). \(\mathrm{HC}^0\) is the space of traces; for irrational \(\theta\) it is \(\mathbb C\cdot\tau\). \(I\) is an isomorphism here.
- \(S:\mathrm{HC}^0\to\mathrm{HC}^2\). Sends \(\tau\) toward the even periodic plane; the volume class \(\psi\) is the new summand \(V_2\).
- \(B:\mathrm{HC}^2\to\mathrm{HH}^1\). Hits the span of \(\psi_1,\psi_2\) up to the image of \(I\) from \(\mathrm{HC}^1\).
- \(S:\mathrm{HC}^1\to\mathrm{HC}^3\to\mathrm{HC}^5\to\cdots\) stabilizes on \(\mathrm{span}\{\psi_1,\psi_2\}\).
- For \(n\ge 3\), \(B:\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\to\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}=0\) is zero, so \(S:\mathrm{HC}^n\to\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}\) is surjective, and after one more step injective: that is periodicity.


The Diophantine-sensitive part of \(\mathrm{HH}\) (when one works with less regular algebras, or with the Kronecker-foliation presentation) is killed by \(S\). That is the cancellation Khalkhali–Shapiro recalled: Hochschild can see the arithmetic of \(\theta\); \(\mathrm{HP}\) does not.


## What this is not


- Not Hopf-cyclic cohomology of \(\mathcal H_1\). No modular pair, no \(\delta_n\), no Godbillon–Vey. The volume class \(\psi\) is a cyclic 2-cocycle of \(A_\theta^\infty\), not \([\delta_1]\).
- Not a computation of \(\Upsilon(\theta)\) or of a Siegel radius.
- Not SAYD coefficients. The coefficients here are ordinary (the algebra acting on itself).


## Against Geometry Maximization


\(A_\theta^\infty\) is the algebra generated by the two unitaries implementing rotation by \(\theta\) on \(L^2(\mathbb T)\). v1.3’s map is a *single* circle diffeomorphism \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\), not that crossed product. The trace \(\tau\) of \(A_\theta^\infty\) is not the occupation measure \(\mu_s\). The derivations \(\delta_j\) are not the slip coboundary.


The one genuine shared letter is small divisors: invertibility of \(1-e^{2\pi i k\theta}\) on Fourier modes controls both \(\mathrm{HH}\) of \(A_\theta^\infty\) (in some presentations) and v1.3’s equation \(W(y+\alpha)-W(y)=A(y)\). After \(S\), cyclic theory forgets that arithmetic; the 39-screen keeps it as an exact coboundary for one function.


The short sentence: for the smooth irrational torus, \(\mathrm{HH}\) is \((\mathbb C,\mathbb C^2,\mathbb C)\) in degrees \(0,1,2\), \(\mathrm{HP}\simeq\bigwedge\mathbb C^2\), and \(SBI\) says \(S\) kills everything except the four de Rham classes \(\tau,\psi_1,\psi_2,\psi\). That is Connes–Nest, not a 39-bin theorem.
Hopf-cyclic cohomology is cyclic cohomology of a Hopf algebra, twisted so that it sees the Hopf structure and not just the underlying algebra.


## Input


A Hopf algebra \(H\) and either


- a **modular pair in involution** \((\delta,\sigma)\): character \(\delta\), grouplike \(\sigma\), \(\delta(\sigma)=1\), and \(\tilde S_\delta^2=\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma\), or
- a **SAYD module** \(M\) (stable anti-Yetter–Drinfeld): module + comodule, opposite braiding to Yetter–Drinfeld, and stability \(m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\).


The rank-one SAYD module is exactly a modular pair. Commutative or cocommutative Hopf algebras always admit \((\varepsilon,1)\). Connes–Moscovici’s \(\mathcal H_1\) admits \((\delta,1)\) with \(\delta(Y)=1\), and no other finite-dimensional SAYD module.


## The complex


Cochains are \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\) (or \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\)). Faces and degeneracies use the product, coproduct, and counit of \(H\). The cyclic operator
\[
\tau_n(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)
=\tilde S_\delta(h_1)\cdot(h_2\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n\otimes\sigma)
\]
uses the twisted antipode and the diagonal action. Involution / stability is the condition \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\). From \(\tau\) one builds
\[
N=1+\tau+\cdots+\tau^n,\qquad
B=Ns(1-\tau),
\]
and the same identities \(b^2=B^2=bB+Bb=0\) as in ordinary cyclic theory. The \((b,B)\)-bicomplex computes
\[
\mathrm{HC}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\quad\text{or}\quad\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M).
\]
Periodic theory \(\mathrm{HP}\) inverts the periodicity map \(S\). The \(SBI\) sequence relating Hopf–Hochschild and Hopf-cyclic cohomology is the exact couple of that bicomplex.


## Characteristic map


If \(A\) is an \(H\)-module algebra with a \(\delta\)-invariant \(\sigma\)-trace \(\tau\),
\[
\chi_\tau(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)(a_0,\ldots,a_n)
=\tau\bigl(a_0\,h_1(a_1)\cdots h_n(a_n)\bigr)
\]
sends Hopf-cyclic classes to ordinary cyclic classes of \(A\). That is how a universal class on \(H\) becomes a pairing on a geometric algebra.


## What it computes


For the Connes–Moscovici algebra \(\mathcal H_n\) of affine transverse jets of a codimension-\(n\) foliation,
\[
\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(\mathcal H_n)\;\simeq\; H^\bullet_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n),
\]
Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology of formal vector fields. In codimension 1:


- \([\delta_1]\) generates \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{odd}}\) and maps to **Godbillon–Vey**;
- \([\Pi]\) generates \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{even}}\) and is the transverse fundamental class.


The local index formula for the CM hypoelliptic signature operator is a finite combination of these classes, pushed by \(\chi_\tau\) to the crossed product of the foliation. Moscovici later gave a Chern–Weil construction of all the classes, absolute and relative to \(O(n)\).


Further applications: characteristic classes of quantum-group actions; cup products; van Est isomorphisms for Lie–Hopf algebras; a proposed Diff-invariant language for background-independent geometry.


## What it is not


| Theory | Object | Cohomology of |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary cyclic | an algebra \(A\) | cochains on \(A\) |
| Hopf-cyclic | a Hopf algebra \(H\) | tensors *of elements of \(H\)* |
| Connes–Kreimer / Birkhoff | characters of a combinatorial Hopf algebra | factorization in a group, not \(\mathrm{HC}(H)\) |
| Cyclic theory of \(A_\theta^\infty\) | the noncommutative torus | ordinary \(\mathrm{HC}(A_\theta^\infty)\), Connes–Nest |


Hopf-cyclic cohomology of \(\mathcal H_1\) is not the cyclic cohomology of the rotation algebra. Godbillon–Vey is not the volume class \(\psi\) of \(A_\theta^\infty\).


## Against Geometry Maximization


The theory needs a Hopf algebra, a modular pair or SAYD module, a module-algebra, and a twisted trace. v1.3 has none of these. The golden conjugacy \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) is a single diffeomorphism, not an \(\mathcal H_n\)-action on jets of a foliation. The slip coboundary is not \([\delta_1]\). Small divisors appear in \(\mathrm{HH}(A_\theta^\infty)\) and in v1.3’s cohomological equation; after \(S\) they disappear from \(\mathrm{HP}(A_\theta^\infty)\), while the 39-screen keeps an exact coboundary.


Attaching \(\mathcal H_1\) to jets of \(h\) and pushing \([\delta_1]\) would be a new characteristic class of that conjugacy, not a number already in §§25–28.


The short sentence: Hopf-cyclic cohomology is the cyclic theory of a Hopf algebra twisted by a modular pair or SAYD module; for \(\mathcal H_n\) it computes transverse characteristic classes. It stays in the qualified glossary with \(\mathcal H_n\), SAYD, and CK.
A **stable anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module** is a coefficient object for Hopf-cyclic (co)homology. Hajac–Khalkhali–Rangipour–Sommerhäuser introduced them so the cyclic operator works beyond a rank-one modular pair.


## The two structures


Fix a Hopf algebra \(H\) with invertible antipode. \(M\) is a left \(H\)-module and a left \(H\)-comodule. Write
\[
h\otimes m\mapsto h\cdot m,\qquad
m\mapsto m_{(-1)}\otimes m_{(0)}\in H\otimes M.
\]


**Anti-Yetter–Drinfeld compatibility** (left-left):
\[
(h\cdot m)_{(-1)}\otimes(h\cdot m)_{(0)}
=h_{(1)}\,m_{(-1)}\,S^{-1}(h_{(3)})\otimes h_{(2)}\cdot m_{(0)}.
\]
The same formula with \(S\) in place of \(S^{-1}\) is a Yetter–Drinfeld module (an object of the Drinfeld centre of \(\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}H\)). The “anti” is the opposite braiding. Three other side conventions exist; they amount to replacing \(H\) by \(H^{\mathrm{op}}\) or \(H^{\mathrm{cop}}\).


**Stability:**
\[
m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m.
\]
Coaction followed by action is the identity of \(M\). This is not implied by AYD.


An AYD module that is stable is a **SAYD module**.


## Rank one


If \(\dim M=1\), the action is a character \(\delta\), the coaction is a grouplike \(\sigma\), AYD becomes \(\tilde S_\delta^2=\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma\), and stability becomes \(\delta(\sigma)=1\). That is a modular pair in involution. Connes–Moscovici’s original coefficients are the unique 1-dimensional SAYD module of \(\mathcal H_1\).


## Why both conditions


On cochains \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\), faces use the module and comodule structures. The cyclic operator \(\tau\) uses the anti-YD braiding to pass \(M\) around the tensor factors of \(H\). After \(n+1\) steps, coaction-then-action of \(H\) on \(M\) has been applied once. Stability makes that composite the identity, hence \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\), hence \(N(1-\tau)=0\), hence Connes’s \(B=Ns(1-\tau)\) satisfies \(B^2=0\). Without stability one has a para-cocyclic object and no \(SBI\) sequence in the usual form.

| Lie–Hopf / bicrossed sum | correspondence with SAYD over the Lie algebra |
| Schwarzian Hopf algebra | an explicit 4-dimensional SAYD module |


**Rigidity of \(\mathcal H_1\).** Rangipour–Sutlu: \(\mathcal H_1\) has no finite-dimensional SAYD module other than the modular line. The argument goes through the Lie algebra of formal vector fields on the line. One cannot enrich the founding example in finite dimensions.


Hassanzadeh–Kucerovsky–Rangipour interpolated two strictly larger coefficient categories; the middle layer depends on both \(H\) and the algebra being acted on. All three layers are distinct.


## Relation to the Drinfeld double


YD modules are \(D(H)\)-modules. AYD modules are modules over a twist of the double (or over \(D(H^{\mathrm{cop}})\)). Stability is not a double-module axiom; it is a trace-type constraint on the two structures together.


Quasi-Hopf and Hopf-algebroid versions exist (Kobyzev–Shapiro), as do anti-YD *contramodules* for the homological side.


## Role


SAYD coefficients give \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M)\) and a characteristic map into ordinary cyclic cohomology of a module-algebra with an \(M\)-valued twisted trace. Different KMS weights correspond to different \(M\). They do not compute classes by themselves.


## Against the screen


SAYD is a pair of compatible \(H\)-structures on a vector space. v1.3 has no Hopf algebra and no \(M\). The modular line of \(\mathcal H_1\) is already stable; that fact does not evaluate the slip coboundary or \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\). Attaching that line to jets of \(h\) in \(F=h^{-1}R_\alpha h\) would be a new construction.


The short sentence: a SAYD module is an anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module with \(m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\); stability is why \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\) on cochains with values in \(M\).
Hopf-cyclic cohomology is built in four steps: a Hopf algebra with a twist, a cocyclic module, the Connes operators, and (optionally) a characteristic map.


## 1. Twist


Start with a Hopf algebra \(H\) over \(k\supset\mathbb Q\), antipode invertible.


Pick either


- a **modular pair in involution** \((\delta,\sigma)\): \(\delta:H\to k\) a character, \(\sigma\in H\) grouplike, \(\delta(\sigma)=1\), and
  \[
  \tilde S_\delta^2(h)=\sigma h\sigma^{-1},\qquad
  \tilde S_\delta(h)=\delta(h_{(1)})S(h_{(2)}),
  \]
- or a **SAYD module** \(M\): left module and left comodule, anti-Yetter–Drinfeld braiding, stability \(m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\).


The line \(k\) with action \(\delta\) and coaction \(\sigma\) is the rank-one SAYD module. Without involution / stability the next step fails.


## 2. Cocyclic module


Set \(C^0=k\) and \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\) for \(n\ge 1\) (or \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\)).


Faces \(\delta_i:C^n\to C^{n-1}\) and degeneracies \(\sigma_i:C^n\to C^{n+1}\) are the standard Hopf-algebra ones: multiply adjacent factors, apply \(\varepsilon\), insert \(1\), with \(\delta\) and \(\sigma\) appearing in the last face. The **cyclic operator** is
\[
\tau_n(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)
=\tilde S_\delta(h_1)\cdot(h_2\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n\otimes\sigma),
\]
where \(\cdot\) is the diagonal adjoint action
\[
h\cdot(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)=h_{(1)}h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{(n)}h_n.
\]
With coefficients, \(\tau\) uses the anti-YD braiding to pass \(M\) around.


**Theorem (Connes–Moscovici; HKRS).** These operators satisfy the cocyclic identities
\[
\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id},\qquad
\tau\delta_i=\delta_{i-1}\tau,\quad\ldots
\]
if and only if the pair is in involution (or \(M\) is stable). That is the whole point of the twist.


## 3. Connes operators and the bicomplex


From \(\tau\) form
\[
N=1+\tau+\cdots+\tau^n,\qquad
\lambda=\tau
\]
(up to the usual sign convention). The extra degeneracy \(s\) inserts a unit. Connes’s operator
\[
B=Ns(1-\tau)
\]
satisfies \(B^2=0\) and anticommutes with the Hochschild coboundary \(b\) of the simplicial module. The \((b,B)\)-bicomplex
\[
d_{\mathrm{tot}}=b+B
\]
has total cohomology
\[
\mathrm{HC}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M).
\]
Periodic theory \(\mathrm{HP}\) is the product-totalization (invert \(S\)). The exact couple of the row filtration is the **\(SBI\) sequence**
\[
\cdots\to\mathrm{HH}^n_{\mathrm{Hopf}}(H)
\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^n(H)
\xrightarrow{S}\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}(H)
\xrightarrow{B}\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}_{\mathrm{Hopf}}(H)\to\cdots.
\]


## 4. Characteristic map


If \(A\) is an \(H\)-module algebra and \(\tau_A:A\to k\) (or into \(M^*\)) is a \(\delta\)-invariant \(\sigma\)-trace,
\[
\chi(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)(a_0,\ldots,a_n)
=\tau_A\bigl(a_0\,h_1(a_1)\cdots h_n(a_n)\bigr)
\]
is a map of cocyclic modules, hence
\[
\chi^\ast:\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H)\to\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(A).
\]
Universal classes on \(H\) become ordinary cyclic classes on \(A\).


## What the construction produces for \(\mathcal H_1\)


On generators \(Y,X,\delta_n\) with the CM coproduct and \(\delta(Y)=1\):


- \(\delta_1\) is primitive, so \(b(\delta_1)=0\) and \(\tau_1(\delta_1)=-\delta_1\). It is a Hopf-cyclic 1-cocycle. Its class generates \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{odd}}\) and maps to Godbillon–Vey.
- An even class \([\Pi]\) generates \(\mathrm{HP}^{\mathrm{even}}\).


In every codimension the same construction yields \(\mathrm{HP}(\mathcal H_n)\simeq H_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n)\).


## What must not be skipped


- No involution / stability \(\Rightarrow\) no cocyclic module \(\Rightarrow\) no \(B\), no \(SBI\).
- Cochains are tensors *of elements of \(H\)*, not cochains *on* \(H\) as an algebra. That is the difference from ordinary cyclic cohomology of \(H\).
- The characteristic map needs a module-algebra and a twisted trace. Without them one has classes of \(H\), not numbers on a geometry.


## Against the screen


The construction needs \(H\), a twist, and optionally \(A\) and \(\tau_A\). v1.3 supplies none of these. Building the complex for \(\mathcal H_1\) and evaluating \(\chi([\delta_1])\) on jets of the conjugacy \(h\) would be a new characteristic class, not a reading of the slip coboundary.


The short sentence: pick a modular pair or SAYD module, form \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\) with cyclic operator \(\tau\) built from \(\tilde S_\delta\), then take the \((b,B)\)-cohomology of that cocyclic module. That is Hopf-cyclic cohomology.
The Connes–Moscovici index theorem computes the index of a **transversally hypoelliptic** operator on a foliation as a pairing of \(K\)-theory of the crossed product with Hopf-cyclic classes of \(\mathcal H_n\).


## The analytic object


A foliation of a manifold \(M\), of codimension \(n\), need not be Riemannian. Ordinary elliptic operators transverse to the leaves therefore need not exist. Connes–Moscovici replace the transverse manifold by its bundle \(PM\) of Euclidean frames (the Connes fibration). On \(PM\) there is a canonical vertical-horizontal splitting and a \(\mathrm{Diff}^+(M)\)-invariant volume.


The **hypoelliptic signature operator** \(D\) on \(PM\) is defined by \(Q=D|D|\), where
\[
Q=(d_V^*d_V-d_V d_V^*)\oplus\gamma_V(d_H+d_H^*).
\]
Here \(d_V\) is vertical exterior derivative along the frame fibre, \(\gamma_V\) its signature grading, and \(d_H\) is horizontal exterior derivative for a torsion-free connection. If \(n\equiv 1,2\pmod 4\) one crosses with \(S^1\) to make the vertical fibre even-dimensional.


\(Q\) is self-adjoint. The triple
\[
\bigl(A_\Gamma,\; L^2(\Lambda^\bullet V^*\otimes\Lambda^\bullet N^*,\mathrm{vol}_{PM}),\; D\bigr)
\]
— \(A_\Gamma\) the crossed product of the foliation by holonomy — is a (typically non-unital) **spectral triple**. Its dimension spectrum is discrete. The operator is hypoelliptic in the Heisenberg / Beals–Greiner calculus, not elliptic in the usual sense. That is enough for the 1995 local index formula of Connes–Moscovici to apply.


## The local formula produces \(\mathcal H_n\)


The 1995 formula writes \(\mathrm{Ch}(D)\) as a finite sum of residues
\[
\int\!\!\!\!\!\!- a_0[D,a_1]^{(k_1)}\cdots[D,a_m]^{(k_m)}\,|D|^{-(m+2|k|)}
\]
of spectral zeta functions. Each iterated commutator with \(D^2\) is a transverse differential operator. The algebra generated by those operators, with the coproduct forced by Leibniz, *is* \(\mathcal H_n\). Transverse geometry has been reduced to a universal affine jet algebra.


## The cohomological identification


Hopf-cyclic cohomology of \(\mathcal H_n\) with its modular pair is Gelfand–Fuchs cohomology of formal vector fields on \(\mathbb R^n\):
\[
\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(\mathcal H_n)\;\simeq\; H^\bullet_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n).
\]
The characteristic map \(\chi_\Gamma\) sends these classes to ordinary cyclic cocycles on \(A_\Gamma\). The diagram
\[
\mathrm{HP}(\mathcal H_n)
\xrightarrow{\kappa^*}
H_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n)
\xrightarrow{\chi_{\mathrm{GF}}}
\mathrm{HC}(A_\Gamma)
\]
commutes with \(\chi_\Gamma\). In codimension 1, \([\delta_1]\) maps to Godbillon–Vey and \([\Pi]\) is the Connes–Chern character of the spectral triple itself.


## The index theorem


**Connes–Moscovici, 1998.** The pairing of \(K_*(A_\Gamma)\) with \(\mathrm{Ch}(D)\) equals the pairing of \(K_*(A_\Gamma)\) with the image of the Gelfand–Fuchs / Hopf-cyclic classes under \(\chi_\Gamma\). In particular the analytic index of the transverse signature operator is a linear combination of transverse characteristic classes of the foliation — Godbillon–Vey, the transverse Pontryagin classes, the transverse fundamental class — with universal coefficients read off the local formula.


That is the solution of the “internal problem” of computing the index of transversally elliptic (here: hypoelliptic) operators on foliations. Ordinary Atiyah–Singer does not apply: there is no elliptic transverse symbol in the usual calculus, and the leaf space is not a manifold.


Later: Moscovici’s Chern–Weil construction of the Hopf-cyclic classes; Benameur–Heitsch \(L^2\)-signatures and semi-finite spectral triples for proper actions on leaf spaces, still using the CM operator as the model.


## What the theorem needs


1. A foliation (or a bifoliation / bounded-geometry analogue).
2. The frame bundle \(PM\) and the operator \(Q\).
3. The crossed-product algebra \(A_\Gamma\).
4. \(\mathcal H_n\) and its Hopf-cyclic cohomology.
5. The 1995 local index formula, to identify \(\mathrm{Ch}(D)\) with a Hopf-cyclic class.


Missing any of these, there is no CM index to compute.


## What it is not


- Not the Atiyah–Singer theorem on a closed manifold (elliptic, no foliation).
- Not the Connes–Moscovici local index formula *alone* (that is the 1995 analytic machine; the 1998 theorem is the identification of its output with \(\mathrm{HP}(\mathcal H_n)\)).
- Not Birkhoff factorization of a QFT character.
- Not an index theorem for \(R_\alpha\) or for \(A_\theta^\infty\). Connes’s index theorem *for the noncommutative torus* is a different pairing, using ordinary cyclic cocycles \(\tau,\psi_j,\psi\).


## Against the screen


The golden rotation is not a foliation of a manifold of codimension \(n\), has no frame bundle \(PM\), and has no hypoelliptic signature operator. Godbillon–Vey is not the slip coboundary. The CM pairing is a number in \(\mathbb Z\) or \(\mathbb R\) attached to a \(K\)-class of a crossed product; v1.3’s identities are functions on a circle.


One *could* view a suspension of \(R_\alpha\) as a Kronecker foliation of \(\mathbb T^2\) and apply the codimension-1 theorem. The output would be a multiple of Godbillon–Vey of that foliation, which for a linear Kronecker foliation vanishes or reduces to a volume pairing — not a 39-bin occupation, and not \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\).


The short sentence: the CM index theorem identifies the local index of the transverse hypoelliptic signature operator with the pairing of \(K(A_\Gamma)\) against Hopf-cyclic classes of \(\mathcal H_n\), i.e. against Gelfand–Fuchs classes of the foliation.
Hopf-cyclic cohomology is cyclic cohomology built on tensors of a Hopf algebra, twisted by a modular pair or a SAYD module.


## Data


A Hopf algebra \(H\) over \(k\supset\mathbb Q\), and a twist:


- **Modular pair in involution** \((\delta,\sigma)\): character \(\delta\), grouplike \(\sigma\), \(\delta(\sigma)=1\), \(\tilde S_\delta^2=\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma\) with \(\tilde S_\delta=(\delta\otimes S)\circ\Delta\); or
- **SAYD module** \(M\): left module and left comodule, anti-Yetter–Drinfeld braiding, stability \(m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\).


The line \(k_{\delta,\sigma}\) is the rank-one case. \(\mathcal H_1\) has no other finite-dimensional SAYD module.


## Cocyclic module


\(C^0=k\), \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\) (or \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\)). Faces and degeneracies are the Hopf-algebra simplicial maps. The cyclic operator is
\[
\tau_n(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)
=\tilde S_\delta(h_1)\cdot(h_2\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n\otimes\sigma)
\]
(diagonal adjoint action). Involution / stability \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\). That is the only extra axiom.


## \((b,B)\) and \(SBI\)


From \(\tau\):
\[
N=1+\tau+\cdots+\tau^n,\qquad
B=Ns(1-\tau).
\]
Then \(b^2=B^2=bB+Bb=0\). Total cohomology of the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex is
\[
\mathrm{HC}^\bullet_{(\delta,\sigma)}(H)\quad\text{or}\quad\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M).
\]
\(\mathrm{HP}\) inverts the periodicity map \(S\). The exact couple is
\[
\cdots\to\mathrm{HH}^n_{\mathrm{Hopf}}
\xrightarrow{I}\mathrm{HC}^n
\xrightarrow{S}\mathrm{HC}^{n+2}
\xrightarrow{B}\mathrm{HH}^{n+1}_{\mathrm{Hopf}}\to\cdots.
\]


## Characteristic map


An \(H\)-module algebra \(A\) with a \(\delta\)-invariant \(\sigma\)-trace \(\tau_A\) gives
\[
\chi(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)(a_0,\ldots,a_n)
=\tau_A\bigl(a_0\,h_1(a_1)\cdots h_n(a_n)\bigr),
\]
hence \(\chi^\ast:\mathrm{HC}(H)\to\mathrm{HC}(A)\).


## Founding computation


For the Connes–Moscovici algebra \(\mathcal H_n\) of affine transverse jets,
\[
\mathrm{HP}^\bullet(\mathcal H_n)\;\simeq\; H^\bullet_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mathfrak a_n).
\]
In codimension 1, \([\delta_1]\) is Godbillon–Vey and \([\Pi]\) is the transverse fundamental class / Chern character of the hypoelliptic signature operator. The 1998 transverse index theorem is the pairing of \(K(A_\Gamma)\) against \(\chi(\mathrm{HP}(\mathcal H_n))\).


## Distinctions


- Cochains are tensors *of elements of \(H\)*, not cochains on \(H\) as an algebra.
- Not ordinary \(\mathrm{HC}(A_\theta^\infty)\) (Connes–Nest: four de Rham classes, \(S\) kills Diophantine junk).
- Not Connes–Kreimer Birkhoff factorization of a character.
- Not Gelfand–Fuchs until \(H=\mathcal H_n\).


## Against the screen


The theory needs \(H\), a twist, and — to produce a number on a geometry — a module-algebra and a trace. v1.3 has none of these. Godbillon–Vey is not the slip coboundary. The CM index is not \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\). A Kronecker suspension of \(R_\alpha\) *is* a foliation to which the codimension-1 theorem applies; the output is a volume / GV pairing of that foliation, not a 39-bin identity.


The short sentence: form \(C^n=H^{\otimes n}\) with \(\tau\) built from \(\tilde S_\delta\), take \((b,B)\)-cohomology; that is Hopf-cyclic cohomology. For \(\mathcal H_n\) it computes transverse characteristic classes.
A SAYD module is a vector space with two compatible \(H\)-structures and one trace-type constraint. The properties below are the ones that matter for Hopf-cyclic cohomology.


## Defining properties


Let \(H\) be a Hopf algebra with invertible antipode. \(M\) is **SAYD** when it is:


1. a left \(H\)-module,
2. a left \(H\)-comodule,
3. **anti-Yetter–Drinfeld**:
   \[
   (h\cdot m)_{(-1)}\otimes(h\cdot m)_{(0)}
   =h_{(1)}m_{(-1)}S^{-1}(h_{(3)})\otimes h_{(2)}\cdot m_{(0)},
   \]
4. **stable**:
   \[
   m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m.
   \]


(1)–(3) make \(M\) AYD. (4) is independent of (3). Side conventions (left-right, …) are equivalent up to \(H^{\mathrm{op}}\) / \(H^{\mathrm{cop}}\).


## Formal properties


- **Rank one.** \(\dim M=1\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) a modular pair in involution: action \(=\delta\), coaction \(=\sigma\), AYD \(\Leftrightarrow\tilde S_\delta^2=\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma\), stability \(\Leftrightarrow\delta(\sigma)=1\).
- **YD vs AYD.** Replacing \(S^{-1}\) by \(S\) gives a Yetter–Drinfeld module, i.e. an object of the Drinfeld centre. AYD modules are modules over a twist of \(D(H)\). Stability is not a double-module axiom.
- **Tensor.** The tensor product of two SAYD modules is not automatically SAYD; the correct monoidal structure lives on YD modules. Coefficients are used one \(M\) at a time.
- **Dual / opposite.** Dualizing or passing to \(H^{\mathrm{op}}\) exchanges variants; stability is preserved when both structures dualize coherently.
- **Restriction to \(\mathcal H_1\).** The only finite-dimensional SAYD module is the modular line. There is no finite-dimensional enrichment of Connes–Moscovici coefficients.
- **Larger categories.** SAYD sits inside two strictly larger coefficient categories (Hassanzadeh–Kucerovsky–Rangipour); the inclusions are proper.


## Properties that make the cyclic theory work


On cochains \(H^{\otimes n}\otimes M\):


- AYD \(\Rightarrow\) faces and the cyclic operator \(\tau\) can be written (para-cocyclic identities).
- Stability \(\Rightarrow\) \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\).
- Both \(\Rightarrow\) \(N(1-\tau)=0\), \(B=Ns(1-\tau)\) satisfies \(B^2=0\), the \((b,B)\)-bicomplex exists, \(SBI\) is exact, \(\mathrm{HC}^\bullet(H,M)\) is defined.


Drop stability and \(B^2\) fails. Drop AYD and \(\tau\) is not even a cyclic operator.


## Properties used by the characteristic map


If \(A\) is an \(H\)-module algebra, an \(M\)-valued functional \(\tau_A\) that is invariant for \(\delta_M\) and cyclic up to the coaction of \(M\) produces
\[
\chi:\mathrm{HC}(H,M)\to\mathrm{HC}(A).
\]
Invariance of \(\tau_A\) is the module-algebra analogue of stability: the two structures on \(M\) cancel in the pairing, just as coaction-then-action cancels on \(M\) itself.


## Geometric sources and their properties


| Source | AYD | Stable? |
|---|---|---|
| Modular pair in involution | yes | yes, by \(\delta(\sigma)=1\) |
| Coinvariants of a Hopf–Galois extension with flipped Miyashita–Ulbrich action | yes | extra condition |
| Twist of a YD module | yes | extra condition |
| Lie–Hopf correspondence (Rangipour–Sutlu) | transfers | transfers |
| Schwarzian Hopf algebra | yes | a known 4-dimensional example |


## What SAYD modules do not have


- No canonical finite-dimensional basis (except rank one, and except special Hopf algebras).
- No automatic rigidity theorem for a general \(H\) (only for \(\mathcal H_1\) and some Lie–Hopf algebras).
- No product that stays inside SAYD without further hypotheses.
- No direct relation to Brjuno, \(\Upsilon(\alpha)\), Loewner drivers, or 39-bins.


## Against the screen


These properties are properties of a pair of \(H\)-structures. v1.3 has no \(H\) and no \(M\). Stability of the modular line of \(\mathcal H_1\) is \(\delta(1)=1\); that identity does not compute a slip coboundary.


The short sentence: SAYD means AYD plus \(m_{(-1)}\cdot m_{(0)}=m\); AYD writes \(\tau\), stability makes \(\tau^{n+1}=\mathrm{id}\), and together they give Hopf-cyclic cohomology with coefficients.
