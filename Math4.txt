Root grafting is two different operations that get collapsed in the S41 notes. One builds a new root. The other inserts an existing root onto vertices. Only the second is the pre-Lie product; only its antisymmetrization plus Guin–Oudom (or PBW) produces the associative Grossman–Larson product that is dual to Connes–Kreimer.


## 1. The two graftings


Let \(\mathcal T\) be the vector space with basis the undecorated nonplanar rooted trees. Write \(b\) for the one-vertex tree, \(\ell\) for the two-vertex chain, and \(c\) for the three-vertex cherry (two leaves on one root). Forests are commutative monomials in trees; the empty forest is \(1\).


**New-root grafting** \(B_+\). For a forest \(F=t_1\cdots t_k\),
\[
B_+(F)
\]
is the single tree obtained by adding one fresh root and attaching each root of \(F\) to it. In particular \(B_+(1)=b\), \(B_+(b)=\ell\), \(B_+(b^2)=c\), \(B_+(\ell)\) is the three-vertex ladder.


This is a linear isomorphism from forests of weight \(n\) onto trees of weight \(n+1\). It is **not** a product on trees.


**Vertex grafting** \(\to\). For trees \(t,s\) and a vertex \(v\) of \(s\), write \(t\to_v s\) for the tree obtained by attaching the root of \(t\) to \(v\) by a new edge. Sum over vertices:
\[
t\to s=\sum_{v\in V(s)} t\to_v s.
\]
This **is** a product on \(\mathcal T\). It is the free pre-Lie algebra on one generator \(b\).


Low-degree table:


| \(t\to s\) | \(s=b\) | \(s=\ell\) | \(s=c\) |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(t=b\) | \(\ell\) | \(c+\lambda\) | \(B_+(b\ell)+B_+(b^3)\)-type terms |
| \(t=\ell\) | \(\lambda\) | two distinct 4-vertex trees | … |


Here \(\lambda=B_+(\ell)\) is the ladder. Explicitly
\[
b\to\ell=\ell\text{ with a leaf on the root}+ \ell\text{ with a leaf on the top}=c+\lambda.
\]
The coefficient \(2\) appears only when the target vertices are symmetric, as in \(b\to c\).


## 2. Why vertex grafting is pre-Lie, not associative


A product \(\triangleright\) is left pre-Lie when the associator is symmetric in the first two slots:
\[
(x\triangleright y)\triangleright z-x\triangleright(y\triangleright z)
=(y\triangleright x)\triangleright z-y\triangleright(x\triangleright z).
\]
For \(\to\), both sides count the ways of attaching \(x\) and \(y\) to two (possibly equal) vertices of \(z\). The difference of attachment order on the **same** vertex cancels, so only the symmetric double-insertion remains. Hence \(\to\) is left pre-Lie.


It is not associative. Already
\[
(b\to b)\to b-b\to(b\to b)=\ell\to b-b\to\ell=\lambda-(c+\lambda)=-c\neq0.
\]
The Lie bracket
\[
[t,s]:=t\to s-s\to t
\]
is the usual commutator of the pre-Lie product. This is the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}}\) whose primitives generate the dual of Connes–Kreimer.


This is the same formal pattern as the vector-field product in the report, just with opposite handedness. There one fixed \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) (right-symmetric, commutator equal to **minus** the ordinary bracket). Grafting of trees is the left-handed analogue. Mixing the two conventions is exactly the S41 error recorded in §33.1.


## 3. Connes–Kreimer: forests and cuts, not grafting as product


\(H_{\mathrm{CK}}\) is the **commutative** polynomial algebra on rooted trees. The product is disjoint union of forests. The coproduct is by admissible cuts: an admissible cut \(C\) of a tree \(t\) is a (possibly empty) set of edges, at most one on each path from the root, such that
\[
\Delta t=t\otimes1+1\otimes t+\sum_{C\neq\emptyset} P^C(t)\otimes R^C(t),
\]
where \(R^C(t)\) is the connected component containing the root and \(P^C(t)\) is the remaining forest. Equivalent recursive form, which is what the report quotes:
\[
\Delta B_+(F)=B_+(F)\otimes1+(\mathrm{id}\otimes B_+)\Delta F.
\]
This identity is internal to \(H_{\mathrm{CK}}\). It does **not** say that \(B_+\) is primitive.


For the cherry \(c=B_+(b^2)\):
- empty cut: \(c\otimes1\)
- full “formal” cut of every edge adjacent to the root: \(b^2\otimes b\)
- cut exactly one of the two symmetric edges: \(b\otimes\ell\), twice


so
\[
\Delta c=c\otimes1+1\otimes c+2b\otimes\ell+b^2\otimes b,
\]
which is the formula in §33.1. It is not cocommutative. Cutting only the root-adjacent edges produces the child-forest term \(b^2\otimes b\), **not** a primitive \(c\otimes1\). The primitive part of that formula is the separate formal full-cut / empty-cut pair.


Counit kills every nonempty forest. The antipode exists because the algebra is graded connected; the recursion is the usual one on the reduced coproduct.


## 4. Grossman–Larson: the associative product built from grafting


Start from the same vector space of trees, but now form the symmetric algebra \(S(\mathcal T)\) and extend vertex grafting by the Guin–Oudom recipe: for forests \(F=t_1\cdots t_k\) and a tree \(s\),
\[
F\star s
=\sum_{\text{injections of the }t_i\text{ onto distinct vertices of }s}
\text{(graft each \(t_i\) there)},
\]
and more generally allow some factors of \(F\) to remain ungrafted (they stay as extra forest factors). Restricted to single trees this recovers
\[
t\star s=ts+t\to s.
\]
The extra commutative monomial \(ts\) is the “place both side by side” term. That is why \(\star\) is associative while \(\to\) is only pre-Lie: the forest factor absorbs the associator.


The coproduct on Grossman–Larson is the unshuffle coproduct that makes every single tree primitive after one identifies a tree whose root has a single child with a primitive generator (Panaite’s original identification missed a symmetry factor; Hoffman’s inner product
\[
\langle t,s\rangle=\delta_{t,s}\,|{\mathrm{Aut}}(t)|
\]
repairs it). Then
\[
H_{\mathrm{CK}}^\circ\;\cong\;H_{\mathrm{GL}}\;\cong\;U(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}}).
\]
So:


- Connes–Kreimer = commutative product, noncocommutative cut coproduct
- Grossman–Larson = noncommutative grafting product, cocommutative unshuffle coproduct
- grafting \(\to\) lives on the **primitive** tree space, not as the associative product of either Hopf algebra


Identifying \(H_{\mathrm{CK}}\) with \(U(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}})\) is the confusion S41 repeats.


## 5. Worked associators and a coassociativity check


On coefficients, the report’s right-symmetric product \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) has associator \(YZX''\), symmetric in \(Y,Z\). On trees the left grafting associator of \(b,b,b\) was \(-c\), as above.


Coassociativity of \(\Delta c\) is a three-line check. The only interesting extra terms come from cutting the cherry in two steps:


\[
(\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta c
=(\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id})(c\otimes1+1\otimes c+2b\otimes\ell+b^2\otimes b)
\]
and likewise \((\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta c\). Using \(\Delta b=b\otimes1+1\otimes b\) and
\[
\Delta\ell=\ell\otimes1+1\otimes\ell+b\otimes b,
\]
the mixed terms \(2b\otimes\ell\) produce \(2b\otimes b\otimes b\) from \(\Delta\ell\), while \(b^2\otimes b\) produces the same \(2b\otimes b\otimes b\) from \(\Delta(b^2)=b^2\otimes1+1\otimes b^2+2b\otimes b\). They match. That is the “six coassociativity fixtures” mentioned in §34, restricted to the three-vertex generator.


## 6. What this is **not**


The Hopf action actually used for the screen in §31.4 is much smaller:
\[
H=\mathbb C[P_1,P_2],\qquad
\Delta P_j=P_j\otimes1+1\otimes P_j,\qquad
P_j\cdot a=D_ja.
\]
Both generators are primitive. The characteristic map
\[
\chi(P_j)=\psi_j,\qquad
\chi(P_1\otimes P_2-P_2\otimes P_1)=\Omega
\]
produces the winding and volume cocycles of the rotation algebra. There is no tree, no cut, no grafting, and no identification with \(\mathcal H_1\) or Connes–Moscovici.


Natural growth (the Connes–Kreimer operator that sums the attachments of a single new leaf at every vertex) is exactly \(b\to(-\,)\). Combinations of trees under that operator span the commutative ladder / \(\delta_n\) subalgebra only after the correct linear combinations are taken. S41’s displayed \(\Delta\delta_3\) is not cocommutative, so it cannot be the bare-ladder deconcatenation Hopf algebra.


Character factorization (Birkhoff / BPHZ) needs a connected filtered Hopf algebra and a target splitting into two subalgebras. Connectedness does **not** mean every element is a product of primitives. Nothing in the 39-screen supplies a subtraction scheme or a regularized character.


## 7. Minimal dictionary


| Object | Operation | Role |
|---|---|---|
| \(B_+\) | new root under a forest | CK recursion; not a product |
| \(t\to_v s\) | attach root of \(t\) at vertex \(v\) of \(s\) | combinatorial insertion |
| \(t\to s=\sum_v t\to_v s\) | sum of insertions | free pre-Lie product |
| \([t,s]=t\to s-s\to t\) | commutator | \(\mathfrak g_{\mathrm{trees}}\) |
| \(t\star s=ts+t\to s\) | forest factor plus grafting | associative GL product |
| admissible cut | prune a forest off a trunk | CK coproduct |
| unshuffle of primitives | split a forest of primitives | GL coproduct |


The report’s correction is then one sentence: grafting defines a pre-Lie structure on trees; Connes–Kreimer is the graded dual of the enveloping algebra of that structure; the two products and the two coproducts must not be swapped.


A pre-Lie product is an associative product that has been allowed to fail, but only in a way that is invisible to the commutator. That single constraint is what makes vector fields, rooted-tree grafting, and the Guin–Oudom envelope all produce Lie algebras. The S41 notes mix the left and right versions of that constraint; the report’s §33.1 is the repair.


## 1. Two conventions, one opposite algebra


Write the associator of a bilinear product \(\triangleright\) as
\[
(x,y,z)_\triangleright=(x\triangleright y)\triangleright z-x\triangleright(y\triangleright z).
\]


- **Left pre-Lie** (left-symmetric, Vinberg, Gerstenhaber):
  \[
  (x,y,z)_\triangleright=(y,x,z)_\triangleright.
  \]
  The associator is symmetric in the first two arguments.
- **Right pre-Lie** (right-symmetric):
  \[
  (x,y,z)_\triangleright=(x,z,y)_\triangleright.
  \]
  The associator is symmetric in the last two arguments.


The opposite product \(x\triangleleft y:=y\triangleright x\) interchanges the two sides. Associative products are both. Literature uses “pre-Lie” for either side; Chapoton–Livernet and Guin–Oudom use the right-symmetric identity, Manchon’s surveys usually use the left-symmetric one. Nothing in the identities themselves prefers a side. What matters is not to change side mid-formula.


In both cases the commutator
\[
[x,y]:=x\triangleright y-y\triangleright x
\]
satisfies Jacobi. The proof is four associators cancelling in pairs. So every pre-Lie algebra has a **subadjacent Lie algebra**. The extra structure is a preferred way of writing each bracket as a difference of insertions.


## 2. The two model examples


### Vector fields, right-handed as in the report


On coefficient functions of one-dimensional vector fields the report takes
\[
X\triangleleft Y=YX'.
\]
Then
\[
(X\triangleleft Y)\triangleleft Z=ZYX''+ZY'X',\qquad
X\triangleleft(Y\triangleleft Z)=ZYX''+YZ'X',
\]
so
\[
(X,Y,Z)_\triangleleft=YZX''.
\]
That is symmetric in \(Y,Z\), hence **right** pre-Lie. The commutator is
\[
X\triangleleft Y-Y\triangleleft X=YX'-XY',
\]
which is **minus** the ordinary Lie bracket of vector fields \(XY'-YX'\). Choosing the opposite product \(X\triangleright Y=XY'\) restores the usual bracket and switches to left pre-Lie. S41 labelled the first identity left-symmetric and simultaneously claimed the usual bracket; those two sentences cannot both stand.


In several variables the same pattern is the flat torsion-free connection on \(\mathbb R^n\):
\[
(X^i\partial_i)\triangleleft(Y^j\partial_j)=Y^j(\partial_j X^i)\,\partial_i=\nabla_Y X
\]
or the opposite \(\nabla_X Y\), according to side. Torsion-freeness of \(\nabla\) is exactly the statement that the commutator of \(\triangleleft\) is the Lie bracket (up to the global sign of the convention). Flatness is what makes the associator symmetric.


Composition of the operators \(X\mapsto X\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\) **is** associative. The space of derivations is generally not closed under composition, so that associative product does not live on the derivations themselves. Noncommutation of two operators is not a failure of associativity. That is the second half of the §33.1 correction.


### Rooted trees, left-handed grafting


Let \(\mathcal T\) be spanned by undecorated nonplanar rooted trees. For trees \(t,s\) and a vertex \(v\) of \(s\), write \(t\to_v s\) for the tree obtained by attaching the root of \(t\) to \(v\). Then
\[
t\to s=\sum_{v\in V(s)}t\to_v s
\]
is **left** pre-Lie: both \((t\to s)\to u-t\to(s\to u)\) and \((s\to t)\to u-s\to(t\to u)\) count the ways of attaching \(t\) and \(s\) at (possibly equal) vertices of \(u\). Attachments at two distinct vertices of \(u\) are visibly symmetric in \(t,s\); the same-vertex terms cancel in the associator.


Low-degree check with \(b\) the one-vertex tree and \(\ell=b\to b\) the two-vertex chain:
\[
(b\to b)\to b-b\to(b\to b)=\ell\to b-b\to\ell=-c,
\]
where \(c\) is the cherry. The associator is nonzero, so grafting is not associative, but it equals the swapped associator \((b\to b)\to b-b\to(b\to b)\) after exchanging the two \(b\)’s in the first two slots.


Chapoton–Livernet: this is the free left pre-Lie algebra on one generator \(b\). The free object on a set \(X\) is \(X\)-decorated rooted trees with the same grafting. That is why every example is a quotient of a tree algebra.


## 3. From pre-Lie to an associative envelope


Guin–Oudom put an associative product on the symmetric algebra \(S(L)\) of any pre-Lie algebra \(L\), not just the free one. Extend the pre-Lie product to an action of \(S(L)\) on itself by the rules


- \(A\circ 1=A\),
- \(T\circ(BX)=(T\circ B)\circ X-T\circ(B\circ X)\) for \(T\in S(L)\), \(X\in L\),
- \((AB)\circ C=(A\circ C_{(1)})(B\circ C_{(2)})\),


and set
\[
A*B:=(A\circ B_{(1)})B_{(2)},
\]
using the unshuffle coproduct of \(S(L)\). Then \((S(L),*,\Delta)\) is a Hopf algebra isomorphic to \(U(L_{\mathrm{Lie}})\). On generators one recovers the mnemonic
\[
x*y=xy+x\circ y.
\]
The extra commutative monomial \(xy\) is what kills the associator.


For trees this \(*\) is the Grossman–Larson product: sum over all ways of grafting some of the trees of a forest onto vertices of another forest, leaving the unused trees as extra factors. Dualizing with Hoffman’s pairing \(\langle t,s\rangle=\delta_{t,s}\,|\mathrm{Aut}(t)|\) produces Connes–Kreimer. Hence:


| Structure | Product | Coproduct |
|---|---|---|
| pre-Lie trees \((\mathcal T,\to)\) | vertex grafting | none |
| \(L_{\mathrm{Lie}}\) | commutator of grafting | none |
| Grossman–Larson \(S(\mathcal T)\) | \(*\) (graft or drop) | unshuffle |
| Connes–Kreimer | disjoint union of forests | admissible cuts |


Grafting is the pre-Lie product on primitives. It is not the associative product of either Hopf algebra.


## 4. What the identity actually forbids


The left pre-Lie axiom is equivalent to
\[
L_{[x,y]}=[L_x,L_y]
\]
where \(L_x(y)=x\triangleright y\) is left multiplication: left multiplications form a Lie homomorphism \(L_{\mathrm{Lie}}\to\mathfrak{gl}(L)\). Right pre-Lie is the same statement for right multiplications.


So a pre-Lie structure is a Lie algebra together with a representation of itself on itself that is “close to multiplication.” That is why it appears in three places that look unrelated:


1. **Affine geometry.** A flat torsion-free connection on a manifold makes \(\mathfrak X(M)\) pre-Lie. The connection is the left multiplication.
2. **Deformation theory.** Gerstenhaber’s composition product on Hochschild cochains \(C^\bullet(A,A)\) is right pre-Lie. Its commutator is the Gerstenhaber bracket that controls deformations.
3. **Numerical analysis.** Elementary differentials of a vector field on \(\mathbb R^d\) are the image of the unique pre-Lie morphism
   \[
   (\mathcal T,\to)\longrightarrow\bigl(\mathfrak X(\mathbb R^d),\nabla\bigr)
   \]
   sending \(b\) to that field. B-series are the corresponding characters of the Grossman–Larson envelope. Butcher’s group is a group of those characters.


None of these identifications is available for an arbitrary bilinear product whose commutator happens to be a Lie bracket (Lie-admissible algebras). The extra symmetry of the associator is the whole point.


## 5. Variants that are easy to confuse with the raw structure


- **Novikov.** Right pre-Lie plus \(x\triangleleft(y\triangleleft z)=y\triangleleft(x\triangleleft z)\): left multiplications commute. The one-dimensional vector-field product \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is Novikov, not merely pre-Lie. Tree grafting is not Novikov.
- **NAP / nonassociative permutative.** \(x\triangleright(y\triangleright z)=y\triangleright(x\triangleright z)\) with no pre-Lie axiom. Root-grafting onto the root only (not onto every vertex) is NAP.
- **Post-Lie.** A Lie bracket together with a product whose associator is controlled by that bracket. Recovers pre-Lie when the Lie bracket vanishes. This is the algebraic setting for Lie–Butcher series on homogeneous spaces.
- **Brace / symmetric brace.** Guin–Oudom also show that a pre-Lie product is equivalent to a family of higher braces
  \[
  \{x;y_1,\ldots,y_n\}
  \]
  with \(\{x;y\}=x\circ y\). The higher braces are the multiple insertions that already appear in the recursive definition of \(*\).


The commutative derivation Hopf algebra of the screen
\[
H=\mathbb C[P_1,P_2],\qquad \Delta P_j=P_j\otimes1+1\otimes P_j
\]
is none of these. Both generators are primitive, the action on \(A_\alpha^\infty\) is by the commuting derivations \(D_j\), and the characteristic map produces \(\psi_j\) and \(\Omega\). There is no pre-Lie product on those two generators that encodes the 39-screen.

| primitive grafting is the associative product | grafting is pre-Lie on primitives; \(*\) is the associative extension |
| cutting root-adjacent edges gives the primitive term | it gives \(P^C\otimes R^C=\) child forest \(\otimes\) root |


Once the side of the product is fixed and the envelope is distinguished from the dual, the rest of §33.1 is ordinary bookkeeping: the three-vertex coproduct is not cocommutative, so it cannot be an enveloping-algebra coproduct, and the \(\delta_n\) subalgebra of Connes–Moscovici is not the bare ladder Hopf algebra.


A one-line test that avoids the naming mess: compute the associator of the product you wrote down, see which two arguments it is symmetric in, and compute the commutator on a pair of test elements. If those two answers match the sentences you are about to write, the convention is consistent. S41 fails that test; the report passes it.
A Novikov algebra is a pre-Lie algebra whose multiplications on one side commute. That extra axiom is exactly what the one-dimensional product in §33.1 satisfies, and exactly what tree grafting fails. The name is from hydrodynamics, not from the Landweber–Novikov cobordism operations.


## 1. The axiom, both sides


Keep the associator \((x,y,z)=(x\cdot y)\cdot z-x\cdot(y\cdot z)\).


A **left Novikov** algebra is left pre-Lie with commuting right multiplications:
\[
(x,y,z)=(y,x,z),\qquad (x\cdot y)\cdot z=(x\cdot z)\cdot y.
\]
Equivalently, writing \(L_x(y)=x\cdot y\) and \(R_x(y)=y\cdot x\),
\[
L_{[x,y]}=[L_x,L_y],\qquad [R_x,R_y]=0.
\]


A **right Novikov** algebra is right pre-Lie with commuting left multiplications:
\[
(x,y,z)=(x,z,y),\qquad x\cdot(y\cdot z)=y\cdot(x\cdot z),
\]
i.e.
\[
R_{[x,y]}=[R_x,R_y],\qquad [L_x,L_y]=0.
\]


Opposite product swaps the two sides. Several papers call “Novikov” the left version (Zelmanov, Bai–Meng, Xu); recent surveys often use the right version to match Chapoton–Livernet pre-Lie. The identities are the same after \(x\cdot_{\mathrm{op}} y=y\cdot x\). What cannot be mixed is the side of the extra commutativity with the side of the associator.


Both versions are Lie-admissible: \([x,y]=x\cdot y-y\cdot x\) is a Lie bracket. The extra axiom is a strong restriction on that bracket’s multiplications.


## 2. The prototype, and the report’s product


Let \(A\) be commutative associative and let \(D\) be a derivation of \(A\). Then
\[
a*b:=(Da)\,b
\]
is left Novikov, and
\[
a\triangleleft b:=a\,(Db)
\]
is right Novikov. Direct check for the right-handed product used in the report: on coefficient functions,
\[
X\triangleleft Y=YX'.
\]
Left multiplications:
\[
L_Y(X)=YX',\qquad L_Z L_Y(X)=YZX'',
\]
so \(L_Z L_Y=L_Y L_Z\). The associator was already computed as \(YZX''\), symmetric in \(Y,Z\). Hence \((\mathbb C^\infty,\triangleleft)\) is right Novikov, not merely right pre-Lie.


This is the Gelfand–Dorfman / Balinsky–Novikov prototype. Every Novikov algebra is not of this form — “special” Novikov algebras are those that embed into some \((A,D)\) — but every identity true in the prototype is a candidate identity for the variety, and the free Novikov algebra maps onto every such example.


In one variable the product is even stronger: it is Novikov and the underlying commutative algebra is the ordinary product of functions. In several variables
\[
(X^i\partial_i)\triangleleft(Y^j\partial_j)=Y^j(\partial_j X^i)\,\partial_i=\nabla_Y X
\]
on \(\mathbb R^n\) with the flat connection is only pre-Lie. Left multiplications no longer commute once mixed partials of distinct components can fail to cancel. Novikov is the one-dimensional remnant of that geometry.


## 3. What the extra axiom forbids


Tree grafting \(t\to s=\sum_v t\to_v s\) is free left pre-Lie on one generator. It is **not** Novikov. Already on \(\{b,\ell\}\):
\[
b\to(b\to b)=b\to\ell=c+\lambda,\qquad
b\to(b\to b)\ \text{versus}\ b\to(b\to b)
\]
is tautological, but the Novikov test is \(t\to(s\to u)=s\to(t\to u)\). Taking \(t=s=u=b\) both sides equal \(\ell\to b=\lambda\), too small. The first failure is
\[
b\to(\ell\to b)=\lambda\text{ with a leaf on each possible vertex of }\lambda
\]
versus
\[
\ell\to(b\to b)=\ell\to\ell,
\]
which are unequal linear combinations of the two distinct four-vertex trees (ladder with a side leaf at height 1 versus height 2, and the branched tree). So the free pre-Lie algebra on one generator is not Novikov, and neither is Grossman–Larson grafting.


Operator form of the obstruction: Novikov demands that all \(L_x\) commute, while free pre-Lie only demands that \(x\mapsto L_x\) be a Lie homomorphism. Tree insertions at different vertices do not commute.


## 4. Multiplication operators and the subadjacent Lie algebra


For a left Novikov product the right multiplications form a commutative family, so they can be simultaneously triangularized in the finite-dimensional case (over algebraically closed fields). That is the main classification device in low dimension: first choose a form for the commuting family \(\{R_x\}\), then impose left-symmetry.


Immediate consequences:


- \(R_x R_y=R_{x\cdot y}=R_{y\cdot x}\). Right multiplications see only the symmetrization of the product.
- The subadjacent Lie algebra has
  \[
  [L_x,L_y]=L_{[x,y]},\qquad [R_x,R_y]=0.
  \]
  So the left regular representation is a Lie homomorphism and the right regular representation is abelian.
- If the product is additionally commutative, Novikov collapses to commutative associativity: \((xy)z=(xz)y=x(yz)\). Commutative Novikov algebras are precisely commutative associative algebras. Balinsky–Novikov used that case to produce flat metrics of hydrodynamic type.


Idempotents are constrained. If \(e\cdot e=e\), left-symmetry at \((e,e,x)\) and the commuting-right axiom force \(e\) to act as a projection on a large piece of the algebra. This is why finite-dimensional simple Novikov algebras over \(\mathbb C\) cannot be interesting.


## 5. Simplicity and low-dimensional lists


Zelmanov (1987): every finite-dimensional simple Novikov algebra over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero is one-dimensional. There is no noncommutative finite-dimensional simple example in that setting. Infinite-dimensional simple examples exist (Filippov, Xu); finite-dimensional simple examples exist only in positive characteristic.


Low dimension over \(\mathbb C\):


- Dimension 1: \(e\cdot e=0\) or \(e\cdot e=e\).
- Dimension 2: seven isolated isomorphism types with nontrivial product, plus a one-parameter family \(N_6^\ell\) for \(\ell\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}\).
- Dimension 3 and the 4-dimensional nilpotent-Lie cases are classified (Bai–Meng, Burde–de Graaf).


Codimension growth of the polynomial identities is at most linear in dimension two. Identities separate the nonassociative two-dimensional types.


The one-dimensional function example \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is infinite-dimensional and far from simple: every ideal of functions closed under multiplication by derivatives of other functions is enormous.


## 6. Gelfand–Dorfman: the reason the axiom exists


A **Gelfand–Dorfman algebra** is a vector space with a Novikov product \(\circ\) and a Lie bracket \([,]\) satisfying
\[
[x\circ y,z]-[x\circ z,y]+[x,y]\circ z-[x,z]\circ y-x\circ[y,z]=0.
\]
The subadjacent bracket of \(\circ\) is allowed to differ from the extra Lie bracket. From structure constants of a GD-algebra one builds a first-order Hamiltonian operator
\[
H^{ij}=g^{ij}(u)\,\partial_x+\Gamma^{ij}_k(u)\,u^k_x
\]
on the loop space of the underlying vector space. Hydrodynamic Poisson brackets
\[
\{u(x),v(y)\}=[u,v](x)\,\delta(x-y)+\partial_x(u\circ v)(x)\,\delta(x-y)+(u\circ v+v\circ u)(x)\,\partial_x\delta(x-y)
\]
are the analytic form of the same compatibility. That is the origin of the definition: Balinsky–Novikov and Gelfand–Dorfman needed an algebraic identity that makes this bracket satisfy Jacobi.


Special GD-algebras embed into a Poisson algebra with a derivation. Not every GD-algebra is special; there are extra identities of degree five that hold in the special ones.


This Hamiltonian calculus is unrelated to the 39-screen. The report’s use of \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is only as a one-dimensional test product for the pre-Lie sign, not as a hydrodynamic operator.


## 7. Free objects, trees, and neighbouring varieties


The free Novikov algebra on a set \(X\) can be realized on a quotient of decorated rooted trees: one keeps only those insertion patterns compatible with commuting right (or left) multiplications. Dzhumadil’daev–Löfwall give an explicit basis. It is much thinner than the free pre-Lie algebra on the same set — the cherry-versus-ladder distinction that freely generates pre-Lie relations is largely killed.


Nearby varieties:


| Variety | Extra axiom beyond pre-Lie |
|---|---|
| Novikov | multiplications on one side commute |
| NAP | \(x\cdot(y\cdot z)=y\cdot(x\cdot z)\) with no pre-Lie axiom |
| assosymmetric | both left and right pre-Lie |
| commutative Novikov | associative and commutative |
| Novikov–Poisson | Novikov plus a commuting associative product with Leibniz rules |


Assosymmetric is strictly weaker than associative (Kleinfeld). Novikov is not assosymmetric in general: the prototype \(a*b=(Da)b\) has associator \((Da)(Db)c\), which is not symmetric in all three arguments unless \(D=0\).


Hom-Novikov and \(\delta\)-Novikov deform the axioms by a linear endomorphism. Anti-Novikov (\(\delta=-1\)) behaves differently: it does admit finite-dimensional noncommutative simples over \(\mathbb C\).


## 8. What this is not


The **Landweber–Novikov algebra** is the algebra of stable cobordism operations. It is a different object that happens to share a name. Formal vector fields on the line appear in both stories — as the prototype Novikov product, and as the Lie algebra whose cohomology relates to cobordism operations — but the algebraic structures are not the same.


Connes–Kreimer, Grossman–Larson, and the two-generator derivation Hopf algebra of §31.4 are not Novikov algebras. The last of these is commutative polynomial on two primitives; its only product is ordinary multiplication of \(P_1,P_2\), which is associative.


## 9. The one-line test against the report


For the product written in §33.1:


- associator of \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is \(YZX''\), symmetric in the last two slots → right pre-Lie;
- \(L_Y L_Z=L_Z L_Y\) → left multiplications commute → right Novikov;
- commutator \(YX'-XY'\) is minus the usual vector-field bracket.


Tree grafting fails the second bullet. Composition of differential operators is associative and therefore Novikov, but it does not preserve the space of derivations. Those three sentences are the entire interaction between Novikov algebras and the v1.4 correction register.
A Gelfand–Dorfman algebra is a Novikov product and a Lie bracket on the same space, locked together by one extra identity. That identity is the algebraic content of “this first-order differential operator is Hamiltonian.” It is not a bialgebra in the Hopf sense: there is no coproduct.


## 1. Definition


A **Gelfand–Dorfman algebra** (GD-algebra) is a vector space \(A\) with two bilinear operations \(\circ\) and \([\,{,}\,]\) such that


1. \((A,\circ)\) is Novikov,
2. \((A,[\,{,}\,])\) is Lie,
3. the compatibility identity holds:
\[
[x\circ y,z]-[x\circ z,y]+[x,y]\circ z-[x,z]\circ y-x\circ[y,z]=0.
\]


Some papers write an equivalent cyclic form
\[
[a,b\circ c]-[c,b\circ a]+[b,a]\circ c-[b,c]\circ a-b\circ[a,c]=0.
\]
The two are the same after renaming and skew-symmetry of the bracket. Older sources say “Gelfand–Dorfman bialgebra”; that word now collides with algebra-plus-coalgebra, so GD-algebra is the current name.


Handedness of the Novikov product must be fixed before the compatibility identity is copied. The form above matches the left-Novikov convention
\[
(x,y,z)=(y,x,z),\qquad (x\circ y)\circ z=(x\circ z)\circ y.
\]
Opposite product and opposite bracket give the right-handed twin.


A Novikov algebra with the **zero** Lie bracket is a GD-algebra: every term in the compatibility identity vanishes. A Novikov algebra with its **subadjacent** bracket
\[
[x,y]_-=x\circ y-y\circ x
\]
is also a GD-algebra. That is the original Gelfand–Dorfman observation: the extra identity is automatic for the commutator of a Novikov product. So every Novikov algebra is a GD-algebra in at least two ways, and those two ways coincide precisely when the product is commutative (hence associative).


## 2. The special construction: Poisson plus a derivation


Let \(P\) be a Poisson algebra with product \(xy\), bracket \(\{x,y\}\), and a derivation \(d\) of both structures. Set
\[
x\circ y = x\,d(y)\qquad\text{or}\qquad x\circ y=d(x)\,y
\]
according to side, and \([x,y]=\{x,y\}\). The result \(P^{(d)}\) is a GD-algebra.


A GD-algebra is **special** if it embeds into some \(P^{(d)}\). It is **exceptional** otherwise. Special GD-algebras form a variety: they are defined by the GD axioms plus further identities, so there is a free special object \(\mathrm{SGD}\langle X\rangle\). Every two-dimensional GD-algebra is special. Exceptional examples exist in dimension three.


Two independent identities of degree 4 already fail in the free GD-algebra and hold in every \(P^{(d)}\). They generate all special identities through degree 5; whether they generate the whole special variety is open. Those identities are the precise difference between “the axioms that make an operator Hamiltonian” and “the axioms that come from an ambient Poisson structure.”


The one-dimensional product from §33.1 sits here. On functions, \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is the Novikov half of \(C^\infty(\mathbb R)^{(d/dx)}\). If one takes the Poisson bracket to be zero, the Lie half is trivial and one has a special GD-algebra with only the Novikov product. A nonzero Poisson structure on the same functions (for instance a constant bivector in several variables) gives a genuine two-operation example.


## 3. Why the identity exists: Hamiltonian operators


In the formal calculus of variations one considers evolutionary vector fields on the jet space of maps \(x\mapsto u(x)\in A^*\), and first-order operators
\[
H^{ij}=g^{ij}(u)\,\partial_x+\Gamma^{ij}_k(u)\,u^k_x.
\]
Gelfand–Dorfman asked when \(H\) defines a Poisson bracket on local functionals. If the coefficients are linear in the coordinates of \(A\),
\[
g^{ij}(u)=\text{structure constants of a bracket},\qquad
\Gamma^{ij}_k(u)=\text{structure constants of a product},
\]
the Jacobi identity for that Poisson structure is exactly the GD axioms. Written on generating functions the same Poisson bracket is the hydrodynamic formula
\[
\{u(x),v(y)\}
=[u,v](x)\,\delta(x-y)
+\partial_x(u\circ v)(x)\,\delta(x-y)
+(u\circ v+v\circ u)(x)\,\partial_x\delta(x-y).
\]
The first term is the Lie structure, the last two are the Novikov structure, and compatibility of \(\delta\) and \(\partial_x\delta\) is the extra identity.


Commutative Novikov algebras give the Balinsky–Novikov case: \(g^{ij}\) is a constant metric (after a change of coordinates, under nondegeneracy) and the operator reduces to a first-order Dubrovin–Novikov operator of hydrodynamic type. Noncommutative Novikov algebras produce a broader class of linear Hamiltonian operators whose “metric” is no longer symmetric in the naive sense.


This is a statement about jet-space Poisson geometry. It does not supply a Hamiltonian structure on the 39-screen.


## 4. Quadratic Lie conformal algebras


A **Lie conformal algebra** is a \(\mathbb C[\partial]\)-module \(R\) with a \(\lambda\)-bracket
\[
[a_\lambda b]\in R[\lambda]
\]
satisfying sesquilinearity, skew-symmetry, and the conformal Jacobi identity. It is **quadratic** if \(R\) is free of finite rank over \(\mathbb C[\partial]\) and
\[
[a_\lambda b]=[a,b]+\lambda\,(a\circ b)+\lambda\,(b\circ a)
\]
plus possible \(\partial\)-terms of weight one, for a pair of bilinear operations on a finite-dimensional space of generators.


Xu’s correspondence: quadratic Lie conformal algebras (of the appropriate degree) are equivalent to finite-dimensional GD-algebras. The \(\lambda\)-bracket packages the Lie bracket as the \(\lambda^0\) coefficient and the Novikov product as the \(\lambda^1\) coefficient. Conformal Jacobi expands into Lie Jacobi, Novikov, and the GD compatibility identity. Super and Hom versions of the same dictionary exist.


For special GD-algebras the associated quadratic conformal algebra embeds into an associative conformal envelope with locality bound \(N=3\). That is the vertex-algebra shadow of “comes from a Poisson algebra with derivation.”


## 5. Built-in constructions


| Source | Novikov product | Lie bracket | Special? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novikov algebra \(N\) | \(\circ\) of \(N\) | \(0\) | yes (embed in \(N\) with \(d=0\), \(\{\,\}=0\)) |
| Novikov algebra \(N\) | \(\circ\) of \(N\) | \([x,y]_-=x\circ y-y\circ x\) | yes |
| Poisson algebra \(P\) with derivation \(d\) | \(x\,d(y)\) or \(d(x)\,y\) | \(\{x,y\}\) | yes, by definition |
| Novikov–Poisson algebra | the Novikov product | the Poisson commutator | yes |
| commutative GD | commutative Novikov \(=\) commutative associative | a Lie bracket compatible as above | these are transposed Poisson algebras |


The last line is the overlap with the transposed-Poisson literature: a commutative GD-algebra is precisely a commutative associative algebra with a Lie bracket satisfying
\[
[xy,z]=x[y,z]+[x,z]y
\]
up to the exact GD rearrangement, i.e. a transposed Poisson structure.


Extending structures, matched pairs, and Hom-GD algebras are the usual deformation-and-gluing calculus applied to this signature. They produce new examples; they do not change the meaning of the compatibility identity.


## 6. Free objects and operads


The variety of all GD-algebras is governed by a binary quadratic operad \(\mathtt{GD}\). The special subvariety is a quotient of that operad by the special identities. Bases of the free Novikov algebra (Dzhumadil’daev–Löfwall, and a later rewriting by Gubarev–Sartayev) are the raw material for a monomial basis of the free special GD-algebra: one takes Novikov monomials and inserts Lie brackets under rewriting rules coming from compatibility.


The Koszul dual / Manin product description of \(\mathtt{GD}\) identifies it with a product of the Novikov operad and the Lie operad with a mixed term. That is the operadic restatement of “two structures plus one compatibility.”


Weight filtrations coming from \(\mathrm{wt}(x)=-1\), \(\mathrm{wt}(uv)=\mathrm{wt}(u)+\mathrm{wt}(v)\), \(\mathrm{wt}(d(u))=\mathrm{wt}(u)+1\) give a concrete spanning set for the dual / “GD!” constructions used to list derived identities.


## 7. What this is not


- Not a Hopf algebra and not a coassociative bialgebra.
- Not Connes–Kreimer, not Grossman–Larson, not the two-generator derivation Hopf algebra of §31.4.
- Not the Gelfand–Naimark theorem, not Gelfand–Dikii (though Gelfand–Dikii Hamiltonian pairs are the analytic ancestors).
- Not automatically a vertex algebra. The conformal envelope of a special GD-algebra is a step toward one; locality and a vacuum still have to be supplied.


The pre-Lie / Novikov / GD tower, for the one-dimensional test product of the report, is therefore:


\[
X\triangleleft Y=YX'
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{right Novikov}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{GD with }[X,Y]=0\text{ or }[X,Y]=XY'-YX'.
\]


The first arrow is the extra commuting-multiplication axiom. The second arrow is free if one uses the commutator, and is the special embedding into functions if one uses the zero bracket. Neither arrow involves rooted-tree grafting, and neither produces a Hamiltonian operator on the 39-screen.
Novikov algebras are the algebraic residue of a linear Poisson bracket of hydrodynamic type. The name is Osborn’s, after Balinsky–Novikov; the axioms are older and come from Gelfand–Dorfman Hamiltonian operators. They are not the Landweber–Novikov cobordism operations.


## 1. Axioms


A bilinear product \(\circ\) is **Novikov** when it is pre-Lie on one side and the multiplications on the other side commute.


**Left Novikov** (the convention of Zelmanov, Xu, Bai–Meng):
\[
(x\circ y)\circ z-x\circ(y\circ z)=(y\circ x)\circ z-y\circ(x\circ z),
\]
\[
(x\circ y)\circ z=(x\circ z)\circ y.
\]
In operators: \(L_{[x,y]}=[L_x,L_y]\) and \([R_x,R_y]=0\).


**Right Novikov** (Dzhumadil’daev–Löfwall, and the side matching \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\)):
\[
(x\circ y)\circ z-x\circ(y\circ z)=(x\circ z)\circ y-x\circ(z\circ y),
\]
\[
x\circ(y\circ z)=y\circ(x\circ z).
\]
In operators: \(R_{[x,y]}=[R_x,R_y]\) and \([L_x,L_y]=0\).


The opposite product swaps the two sides. An associative product is Novikov on both sides. A commutative Novikov product is commutative associative: the extra axiom plus commutativity forces the associator to vanish.


The commutator \([x,y]=x\circ y-y\circ x\) is always a Lie bracket, so every Novikov algebra is Lie-admissible. That subadjacent Lie algebra is typically solvable or nilpotent in the finite-dimensional characteristic-zero case; it is not a free extra structure.


## 2. The generating example


If \(A\) is commutative associative and \(D\) is a derivation of \(A\), then
\[
a\circ b=(Da)\,b
\]
is left Novikov and
\[
a\triangleleft b=a\,(Db)
\]
is right Novikov. This is the Gelfand–Dorfman prototype. Every identity true in all such \(A^{(D)}\) is a candidate identity of the Novikov variety; not every Novikov algebra embeds in one (the embedding problem is the “special” question, now usually asked at the Gelfand–Dorfman level).


On functions of one variable the report’s test product
\[
X\triangleleft Y=YX'
\]
is exactly \(C^\infty(\mathbb R)^{(d/dx)}\) with the right-handed convention. Left multiplications commute because mixed partials of a single coordinate collapse. On \(\mathbb R^n\) the analogous connection product \(\nabla_Y X\) is only pre-Lie: Novikov is the one-dimensional remnant.


The same recipe with a constant \(\lambda\in A\) in place of a derivation,
\[
a\circ b=(\lambda a)\,b,
\]
still produces Novikov algebras (Filippov, Xu). Hom-Novikov algebras replace \(D\) by an endomorphism twisted against a twisting map \(\alpha\).


## 3. Where the axioms came from


Gelfand–Dorfman (1979) isolated the identities that make a first-order operator
\[
H^{ij}=g^{ij}(u)\,\partial_x+\Gamma^{ij}_k(u)\,u^k_x
\]
Hamiltonian when the coefficients are linear in the fields. Balinsky–Novikov (1985) treated the symmetric case
\[
\{u^i(x),u^j(y)\}=g^{ij}(u)\,\delta'(x-y)+b^{ij}_k(u)\,u^k_x\,\delta(x-y)
\]
and showed that, for \(g^{ij}\) linear, the algebraic content is a Novikov product (commutative, hence associative, when \(b^{ij}_k\) is symmetric). Nondegenerate commutative examples are Frobenius algebras; after a change of coordinates one recovers a constant Dubrovin–Novikov bracket. The noncommutative theory is the same linear Jacobi identity without the symmetry assumption on \(b^{ij}_k\).


That is the whole physical origin. A Novikov algebra is not itself a Poisson structure on a manifold; it is the coefficient algebra of one.


## 4. Free algebras and growth


The free right-symmetric algebra on a set \(X\) has a basis of rooted trees with a planar (or labelled) structure; Novikov is the quotient by the commuting-left (or commuting-right) axiom. Dzhumadil’daev–Löfwall give two realizations of a basis of the free Novikov algebra: certain classes of trees, and later Young diagrams. For one generator the dimension in degree \(n\) equals the number of unordered partitions of \(n-1\). For the variety as a whole the codimension sequence of the free algebra on countably many generators is
\[
\dim N_n=\binom{2n-2}{n-1},\qquad \operatorname{Exp}(\mathbf{Nov})=4,
\]
with generating function \(x(1-4x)^{-1/2}\). The Novikov operad is not Koszul.


Tree grafting of the Chapoton–Livernet pre-Lie algebra does **not** descend to Novikov: \(t\to(s\to u)\) and \(s\to(t\to u)\) differ as soon as one leaves the one-vertex generator. The free Novikov algebra is a proper quotient of the free pre-Lie algebra on the same set.


A linear basis can also be written as “r-elements” (iterated right multiplications of generators subject to a normal-ordering rule). Gröbner–Shirshov bases for the magmatic presentation recover the Dzhumadil’daev–Löfwall monomials as irreducibles.


## 5. Simplicity and low dimension


Zelmanov (1987): over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, every finite-dimensional simple Novikov algebra is one-dimensional. There is no noncommutative finite-dimensional simple example in that setting.


Filippov constructed simple infinite-dimensional examples, and simple finite-dimensional examples in positive characteristic. Xu classified the finite-dimensional simples in characteristic \(p>2\) and the infinite-dimensional simples in characteristic zero under local-finiteness hypotheses on an idempotent.


Over \(\mathbb C\):


- dimension 1: \(e\circ e=0\) or \(e\circ e=e\);
- dimension 2: seven isolated nontrivial types plus a one-parameter family \(N_6^\ell\), \(\ell\notin\{0,1\}\);
- dimension 3: classified (Bai–Meng);
- dimension 4: classified when the subadjacent Lie algebra is nilpotent (Burde–de Graaf).


Polynomial identities separate the nonassociative two-dimensional types; their PI-codimensions grow at most linearly.


The function example \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is infinite-dimensional and far from simple.


## 6. Novikov–Poisson algebras


Xu introduced a second product to study simples and modules. A **Novikov–Poisson** algebra carries a commutative associative product \(\cdot\) and a Novikov product \(\circ\) linked by
\[
(x\cdot y)\circ z=x\cdot(y\circ z),
\]
\[
(x\circ y)\cdot z-(y\circ x)\cdot z=x\circ(y\cdot z)-y\circ(x\cdot z).
\]
(The exact placement of \(\cdot\) versus \(\circ\) varies with handedness; the displayed form is one of the two standard ones.) Every commutative associative algebra with a derivation produces a Novikov–Poisson algebra by \(x\circ y=D(x)\cdot y\), and every unital Novikov–Poisson algebra arises this way. Tensor products of Novikov–Poisson algebras remain Novikov–Poisson, which is why Xu needed the extra product to have a tensor theory at all.


A Novikov algebra is simple (characteristic not 2) if and only if its associated commutative derivation algebra is differentially simple, under the hypotheses of Zhelyabin–Tikhov. Novikov–Poisson algebras embed into commutative conformal algebras with a derivation; the commutator GD-algebra they produce is therefore special.


## 7. Neighbours in the same neighbourhood


| Structure | Relation to Novikov |
|---|---|
| pre-Lie / left-symmetric | drop commuting multiplications |
| NAP | keep \(x\circ(y\circ z)=y\circ(x\circ z)\), drop pre-Lie |
| assosymmetric | both left and right pre-Lie; strictly contains associative |
| Gelfand–Dorfman | Novikov plus a (possibly different) Lie bracket and one compatibility identity |
| transposed Poisson | commutative GD, i.e. commutative Novikov plus compatible Lie |
| noncommutative Novikov | derived from associative algebras with derivation rather than commutative ones |


Gelfand–Dorfman is the natural two-operation extension: any Novikov algebra becomes a GD-algebra by taking either the zero bracket or the commutator bracket. Special GD-algebras are those that embed in a Poisson algebra with a derivation; that is the precise sense in which the prototype \(A^{(D)}\) generates a proper subvariety of all GD-algebras, and of all Novikov algebras only after one forgets the extra bracket.


## 8. What the name does not mean


The **Landweber–Novikov algebra** is the algebra of stable cobordism operations. Formal vector fields on the line appear in both stories — as the prototype Novikov product, and as the Lie algebra whose cohomology sees cobordism operations — but the structures are different.


Connes–Kreimer, Grossman–Larson, and the two-primitive derivation Hopf algebra of the 39-screen are not Novikov algebras. Rooted-tree grafting is free pre-Lie, not Novikov.


## 9. The one-dimensional test, restated


For \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) on coefficient functions:


- associator \(YZX''\) is symmetric in \(Y,Z\) → right pre-Lie;
- \(L_Y L_Z=L_Z L_Y\) → right Novikov;
- commutator \(YX'-XY'\) is minus the ordinary vector-field bracket;
- pairing with the zero Poisson bracket, or with a genuine Poisson structure and \(d/dx\), produces a special Gelfand–Dorfman algebra.


That is the entire footprint of Novikov algebras on the v1.4 correction register. The rest of the theory — free bases, Zelmanov simplicity, hydrodynamic operators, conformal envelopes — is independent of the screen and is not used there.
A Gelfand–Dorfman algebra puts a Novikov product and a Lie bracket on the same vector space and forces one compatibility identity between them. That identity is the algebraic form of “a first-order operator with linear coefficients is Hamiltonian.” There is no coproduct; the older name “Gelfand–Dorfman bialgebra” is only historical.


## 1. The three axioms


A vector space \(A\) with bilinear operations \(\circ\) and \([\,{,}\,]\) is a **Gelfand–Dorfman algebra** when


1. \((A,\circ)\) is Novikov,
2. \((A,[\,{,}\,])\) is Lie,
3. for all \(x,y,z\in A\),
\[
[x\circ y,z]-[x\circ z,y]+[x,y]\circ z-[x,z]\circ y-x\circ[y,z]=0.
\]


An equivalent cyclic writing is
\[
[a,b\circ c]-[c,b\circ a]+[b,a]\circ c-[b,c]\circ a-b\circ[a,c]=0.
\]
The displayed form matches the left-Novikov convention
\[
(x,y,z)=(y,x,z),\qquad (x\circ y)\circ z=(x\circ z)\circ y.
\]
Opposite product and opposite bracket give the right-handed twin; the two must not be mixed inside one identity.


The Lie bracket is independent data. It may be zero, it may be the commutator of \(\circ\), or it may be something else, provided (3) holds.


## 2. Every Novikov algebra is a GD-algebra in two ways


**Zero bracket.** If \([\,{,}\,]=0\), every term in (3) vanishes. A Novikov algebra is a GD-algebra with trivial Lie structure.


**Subadjacent bracket.** Gelfand–Dorfman observed that
\[
[x,y]_-:=x\circ y-y\circ x
\]
always satisfies (3) when \(\circ\) is Novikov. So the commutator of a Novikov product is a compatible Lie structure, with no extra check.


These two GD-structures coincide if and only if \(\circ\) is commutative, in which case Novikov collapses to commutative associativity and one is looking at a commutative associative algebra with a (possibly zero) Lie bracket. That commutative case is the overlap with transposed Poisson algebras.


## 3. The special construction


Let \(P\) be Poisson, with product \(xy\) and bracket \(\{x,y\}\), and let \(d\) be a derivation of both operations. Then
\[
x\circ y = d(x)\,y\qquad\text{or}\qquad x\circ y=x\,d(y),
\]
together with \([x,y]=\{x,y\}\), makes \(P^{(d)}\) a GD-algebra.


A GD-algebra is **special** if it embeds in some \(P^{(d)}\), and **exceptional** otherwise. Special GD-algebras form a variety: they are cut out by the three axioms plus further identities, so there is a free special object \(\mathrm{SGD}\langle X\rangle\). Every two-dimensional GD-algebra is special. Exceptional examples appear in dimension three.


Two independent identities of degree 4 hold in every \(P^{(d)}\) and fail in the free GD-algebra. They generate all special identities through degree 5. Whether they generate the whole special variety is open. Those identities are the exact gap between “the operator is Hamiltonian” and “the structure comes from an ambient Poisson algebra.”


The one-dimensional product \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is the Novikov half of \(C^\infty(\mathbb R)^{(d/dx)}\). With the zero Poisson bracket this is a special GD-algebra with trivial Lie structure; with a genuine Poisson structure on the same functions it is a two-operation special example.


## 4. Hamiltonian operators


On the jet space of maps \(x\mapsto u(x)\in A^*\) consider first-order operators
\[
H^{ij}=g^{ij}(u)\,\partial_x+\Gamma^{ij}_k(u)\,u^k_x.
\]
When the coefficients are linear in the coordinates of \(A\), Jacobi for the induced Poisson bracket on local functionals is precisely the GD axioms: structure constants of \([\,{,}\,]\) become \(g^{ij}\), structure constants of \(\circ\) become \(\Gamma^{ij}_k\). On generating functions the same Poisson structure is the hydrodynamic bracket
\[
\{u(x),v(y)\}
=[u,v](x)\,\delta(x-y)
+\partial_x(u\circ v)(x)\,\delta(x-y)
+(u\circ v+v\circ u)(x)\,\partial_x\delta(x-y).
\]
The \(\delta\) term is the Lie structure, the \(\delta\) and \(\delta'\) terms built from \(\circ\) are the Novikov structure, and compatibility of those distributions is identity (3).


If \(\circ\) is commutative, \(g^{ij}\) is a genuine metric (after nondegeneracy) and one recovers Balinsky–Novikov / Dubrovin–Novikov operators of hydrodynamic type. The noncommutative theory is the same Jacobi identity without that symmetry.


This is jet-space Poisson geometry. It does not put a Hamiltonian structure on the 39-screen.


## 5. Quadratic Lie conformal algebras


A Lie conformal algebra is a \(\mathbb C[\partial]\)-module with a \(\lambda\)-bracket \([a_\lambda b]\in R[\lambda]\) satisfying sesquilinearity, skew-symmetry, and conformal Jacobi. It is **quadratic** when \(R\) is free of finite rank over \(\mathbb C[\partial]\) and the \(\lambda\)-bracket on generators is linear in \(\lambda\):
\[
[a_\lambda b]=[a,b]+\lambda\,(a\circ b+b\circ a)
\]
up to the usual \(\partial\)-corrections.


Xu’s dictionary: finite-dimensional GD-algebras \(\leftrightarrow\) quadratic Lie conformal algebras of the matching degree. The constant term of the \(\lambda\)-bracket is the Lie structure, the coefficient of \(\lambda\) is the Novikov structure, and conformal Jacobi expands into Lie Jacobi plus Novikov plus (3). Super and Hom versions of the same correspondence exist.


For a special GD-algebra the associated quadratic conformal algebra embeds in an associative conformal envelope with locality bound \(N=3\). That is the vertex-algebra shadow of “comes from Poisson plus a derivation.” Novikov–Poisson algebras embed further into commutative conformal algebras with a derivation, so the GD-algebra they produce is automatically special.


## 6. A table of constructions


| Source | \(\circ\) | \([\,{,}\,]\) | Special? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novikov algebra \(N\) | product of \(N\) | \(0\) | yes |
| Novikov algebra \(N\) | product of \(N\) | commutator of \(\circ\) | yes |
| Poisson \(P\) with derivation \(d\) | \(d(x)y\) or \(x\,d(y)\) | \(\{x,y\}\) | yes, by definition |
| Novikov–Poisson | the Novikov product | Poisson commutator | yes |
| commutative GD | commutative associative | compatible Lie | transposed Poisson |


Extending structures and matched pairs produce more examples by gluing two GD-algebras along compatible actions. Hom-GD algebras twist both operations and the identity by an endomorphism. Neither construction changes what the compatibility identity means.


## 7. Free objects and the operad


The variety of all GD-algebras is governed by a binary quadratic operad \(\mathtt{GD}\), the Manin product of the Novikov and Lie operads with a mixed term. The special subvariety is a quotient of that operad.


A monomial basis of the free special GD-algebra is built from a basis of the free Novikov algebra (Dzhumadil’daev–Löfwall trees or Young diagrams, later rewritten by Gubarev–Sartayev) by inserting Lie brackets and reducing via (3). Weight
\[
\mathrm{wt}(x)=-1,\qquad
\mathrm{wt}(uv)=\mathrm{wt}(u)+\mathrm{wt}(v),\qquad
\mathrm{wt}(d(u))=\mathrm{wt}(u)+1
\]
on the free commutative differential algebra gives a spanning set for the dual “GD!” constructions used to list derived identities.


The free GD-algebra is strictly larger than the free special one: the degree-4 special identities are the first difference.


## 8. What this is not


- Not a Hopf algebra, not a coassociative bialgebra, not Connes–Kreimer, not Grossman–Larson.
- Not the two-primitive derivation Hopf algebra of §31.4, whose only product is ordinary commutative multiplication of \(P_1,P_2\).
- Not Gelfand–Naimark and not Gelfand–Dikii, though Gelfand–Dikii Hamiltonian pairs are the analytic ancestors of the operator \(H^{ij}\).
- Not automatically a vertex algebra: the conformal envelope of a special GD-algebra still needs locality conventions and a vacuum.


## 9. The tower that actually meets the report


On coefficient functions the test product of §33.1 sits in this tower and nowhere higher:


\[
X\triangleleft Y=YX'
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{right Novikov}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{GD with }[X,Y]=0\text{ or }[X,Y]=XY'-YX'.
\]


The first arrow is commuting left multiplications. The second is free if one takes the commutator, and is the special embedding into functions if one takes the zero bracket. Rooted-tree grafting does not survive the first arrow. Neither arrow produces a Hamiltonian operator, a conformal algebra, or an identity used by the 39-screen.
A Lie conformal algebra is a Lie algebra that has been allowed to depend polynomially on a formal spectral parameter, with \(\partial\) acting as a derivation of that parameter. It is the algebraic skeleton of the singular part of an operator product expansion, without the normally ordered product that would make a vertex algebra.


## 1. Axioms


A **Lie conformal algebra** over \(\mathbb C\) is a \(\mathbb C[\partial]\)-module \(R\) together with a \(\mathbb C\)-bilinear \(\lambda\)-bracket
\[
[\,\cdot\,_\lambda\,\cdot\,]:R\otimes R\to R[\lambda]
\]
satisfying three axioms.


**Sesquilinearity**
\[
[\partial a_\lambda b]=-\lambda[a_\lambda b],\qquad
[a_\lambda\partial b]=(\partial+\lambda)[a_\lambda b].
\]


**Skew-symmetry**
\[
[a_\lambda b]=-[b_{-\lambda-\partial}a].
\]
The right-hand side means: compute \([b_\mu a]\), then substitute \(\mu\mapsto-\lambda-\partial\), with \(\partial\) acting on the coefficients from the left.


**Jacobi**
\[
[a_\lambda[b_\mu c]]=[[a_\lambda b]_{\lambda+\mu}c]+[b_\mu[a_\lambda c]],
\]
as an equality in \(R[\lambda,\mu]\).


Drop \(\lambda\), \(\mu\), and \(\partial\), and these are the ordinary Lie axioms. The super version inserts signs \(p(a,b)=(-1)^{|a||b|}\) in skew-symmetry and Jacobi. Leibniz conformal algebras are the same structure without skew-symmetry.


Expanding
\[
[a_\lambda b]=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}\,a_{(n)}b
\]
translates the axioms into a family of \(n\)-products \(a_{(n)}b\in R\). Sesquilinearity becomes
\[
(\partial a)_{(n)}b=-n\,a_{(n-1)}b,\qquad
a_{(n)}\partial b=\partial(a_{(n)}b)+n\,a_{(n-1)}b,
\]
and Jacobi becomes a binomial convolution of those products. Only finitely many \(n\)-products of any pair are nonzero — that is the algebraic form of locality of fields.


## 2. What the structure is encoding


Start from a Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g\) of formal distributions: elements of \(\mathfrak g\) are Laurent series in \(z\) with coefficients in some coefficient Lie algebra, and the bracket of two series is a finite linear combination of \(\delta^{(k)}(z-w)\) times other series. The Fourier transform in the difference variable is the \(\lambda\)-bracket. Sesquilinearity is \(\partial_z\) acting on those series, skew-symmetry is skew-symmetry of the coefficient bracket after integration by parts, and Jacobi is Jacobi of \(\mathfrak g\).


Conversely, every Lie conformal algebra \(R\) produces a Lie algebra of coefficients, the **annihilation algebra**
\[
\operatorname{Lie}(R)=R[t,t^{-1}]/( \partial+\partial_t)R[t,t^{-1}],
\]
with the residue pairing against the \(\lambda\)-bracket. The extended annihilation algebra is \(\mathbb C\partial\ltimes\operatorname{Lie}(R)\). For torsion-free finite \(R\), this filtered Lie algebra determines \(R\). That is the bridge used in the classification.


## 3. Basic examples


**Current algebra.** For a Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g\),
\[
\operatorname{Cur}\mathfrak g=\mathbb C[\partial]\otimes\mathfrak g,\qquad
[a_\lambda b]=[a,b].
\]
No \(\lambda\) appears. The coefficient algebra is the loop algebra \(\mathfrak g[t,t^{-1}]\). A central extension
\[
[a_\lambda b]=[a,b]+\lambda\langle a,b\rangle K,\qquad [\partial K_\lambda-]=0
\]
is the affine Kac–Moody conformal algebra, provided \(\langle\,,\rangle\) is invariant and symmetric.


**Virasoro.** Rank-one free module \(\mathbb C[\partial]L\) with
\[
[L_\lambda L]=(\partial+2\lambda)L.
\]
This is the unique (up to scale) nontrivial \(\lambda\)-bracket on one generator that satisfies Jacobi. The coefficient algebra is the Witt algebra, or Virasoro after a central extension
\[
[L_\lambda L]=(\partial+2\lambda)L+\tfrac{\lambda^3}{12}c.
\]


**Semidirect products.** \(\operatorname{Vir}\ltimes\operatorname{Cur}\mathfrak g\) with \(L\) acting by \(\partial\) on currents is the conformal algebra of a Kac–Moody–Virasoro pair.


**General linear.** For a \(\mathbb C[\partial]\)-module \(M\), the associative conformal algebra \(\operatorname{Cend} M\) of conformal endomorphisms has \(\lambda\)-products of finite order. Its commutator \(\mathfrak{gc}\,M\) is the general Lie conformal algebra. Finite rank free \(M\) gives \(\mathfrak{gc}_N\).


**Quadratic algebras.** If \(R=\mathbb C[\partial]V\) is free on a finite-dimensional space \(V\) and
\[
[a_\lambda b]\in V+\lambda V+\partial V
\]
for \(a,b\in V\), the structure is called **quadratic**. Writing
\[
[a_\lambda b]=[b,a]+\partial(b\circ a)+\lambda(a\circ b+b\circ a)
\]
(or an equivalent rearrangement of \(\circ\)), the \(\lambda\)-bracket axioms hold if and only if \((V,\circ,[\,{,}\,])\) is a Gelfand–Dorfman algebra. That is Xu’s correspondence. Current algebras are the case \(\circ=0\); Virasoro is the one-dimensional Novikov algebra \(\mathbb C L\) with \(L\circ L=L\) and zero Lie bracket, up to normalization.


## 4. Finite simple algebras


A Lie conformal algebra is **finite** if it is finitely generated as a \(\mathbb C[\partial]\)-module. D’Andrea–Kac classified the finite simple ones: after passing to the torsion-free quotient,


- \(\operatorname{Cur}\mathfrak s\) for a simple finite-dimensional Lie algebra \(\mathfrak s\),
- the Virasoro conformal algebra.


Semisimple finite algebras are direct sums of these, with a possible Virasoro acting by derivations on a current summand. The proof goes through the annihilation algebra, Cartan’s theory of filtered linearly compact Lie algebras, and a conformal Killing form.


The super classification (Fattori–Kac) is longer: it adds the Neveu–Schwarz family \(K_N\), the conformal algebras \(W_N\), \(S_{N,a}\), \(\widetilde S_N\), and a short list of exceptions (\(CK_6\), \(K'_4\)).


Finiteness is essential. Infinite-rank simple examples exist in abundance (Block-type conformal algebras, various graded quadratic algebras attached to infinite-dimensional Novikov algebras).


## 5. Modules and representations


A module \(M\) over \(R\) is a \(\mathbb C[\partial]\)-module with a \(\lambda\)-action \(a_\lambda v\in M[[\lambda]]\) satisfying the same sesquilinearity and
\[
a_\lambda(b_\mu v)-b_\mu(a_\lambda v)=[a_\lambda b]_{\lambda+\mu}v.
\]
Finite modules over finite simple algebras are known in the same spirit as finite-dimensional modules over simple Lie algebras, with conformal analogues of Lie’s theorem and Cartan–Jacobson. Whether every finite Lie conformal algebra admits a finite faithful conformal representation is open in general; it is known for quadratic algebras built on *special* Gelfand–Dorfman algebras.


## 6. Vertex algebras, and what is missing


A vertex algebra is a Lie conformal algebra plus a vacuum \(|0\rangle\) and a normally ordered product \(:ab:\) making \(V\) a unital differential algebra, with the two operations linked by the Wick / noncommutative Wick formulae (or, equivalently, by the Borcherds identity). The \(\lambda\)-bracket is recovered from the OPE as the singular part
\[
[a_\lambda b]=\operatorname{sing}_{z=w}\,e^{\lambda(z-w)}a(z)b(w).
\]
So a Lie conformal algebra is precisely the singular OPE data. Reconstructing a vertex algebra requires a choice of regular part. Not every Lie conformal algebra is the singular skeleton of a vertex algebra in a given category of modules; the obstruction is a conformal cohomology class.


Associative conformal algebras stand to associative algebras as Lie conformal algebras stand to Lie algebras. The commutator of an associative conformal product is a \(\lambda\)-bracket. Universal associative envelopes exist, with a locality bound \(N\) specifying how many \(n\)-products may be nonzero. For quadratic algebras on special GD-algebras that bound can be taken to be \(N=3\).


## 7. Cohomology and central extensions


Conformal cohomology \(H^\bullet(R,M)\) is defined by a cochain complex whose differentials are the obvious \(\lambda\)-insertions. \(H^2(R,\mathbb C)\) classifies central extensions. For current algebras this recovers the invariant forms; for Virasoro it recovers the \(\lambda^3\) central charge. For a quadratic algebra the same groups can be rewritten in terms of bilinear forms and operators on the underlying GD-algebra.


## 8. The tower that meets the earlier notes


\[
\begin{array}{c}
\text{commutative algebra }+\,D\\
\downarrow\\
\text{Novikov}\\
\downarrow\\
\text{GD (zero or commutator bracket)}\\
\downarrow\\
\text{quadratic Lie conformal algebra }R=\mathbb C[\partial]V\\
\downarrow\\
\text{coefficient / annihilation Lie algebra}\\
\end{array}
\]


Virasoro sits at the bottom of this tower as the free rank-one quadratic algebra on the one-dimensional Novikov algebra. Current algebras sit on the other extreme, with vanishing Novikov product. Speciality of the GD-algebra is what lets the quadratic conformal algebra embed into an associative conformal envelope.


None of this is used by the 39-screen. The two-primitive derivation Hopf algebra of §31.4 is an ordinary commutative Hopf algebra of derivations of the rotation algebra, not a conformal algebra: there is no \(\lambda\)-bracket, no \(\partial\)-module structure beyond ordinary differentiation, and no locality. The product \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) is the Novikov seed that *can* be fed into this tower; the report stops at the pre-Lie sign of that seed.
A vertex operator algebra is a vertex algebra with a conformal vector: the singular OPE data of a Lie conformal algebra, plus a normally ordered product, a vacuum, and a Virasoro element that grades the space by energy. It is the algebraic form of a chiral algebra in two-dimensional conformal field theory.


## 1. Vertex algebras first


A **vertex algebra** is a vector space \(V\) with a vacuum \(\mathbf 1\in V\) and a state-field map
\[
Y:V\to(\mathrm{End}\,V)[[z,z^{-1}]],\qquad
a\mapsto Y(a,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}a_{(n)}z^{-n-1},
\]
satisfying three axioms.


**Vacuum.** \(Y(\mathbf 1,z)=\mathrm{id}_V\), and \(Y(a,z)\mathbf 1\in a+zV[[z]]\). In particular \(a_{(-1)}\mathbf 1=a\) and \(a_{(n)}\mathbf 1=0\) for \(n\ge0\).


**Translation.** There is an operator \(T\) (often written \(\partial\)) with \(T\mathbf 1=0\) and
\[
[T,Y(a,z)]=\partial_z Y(a,z),\qquad Y(Ta,z)=\partial_z Y(a,z).
\]


**Locality.** For each pair \(a,b\) some \(N\ge0\) gives
\[
(z-w)^N\bigl[Y(a,z),Y(b,w)\bigr]=0
\]
as an identity of formal series. Equivalently, the Borcherds identity holds for all integers \(k,\ell,m\):
\[
\sum_i\binom{k}{i}(a_{(m+i)}b)_{(k+\ell-i)}c
=\sum_i(-1)^i\binom{m}{i}\Bigl(
a_{(k+m-i)}(b_{(\ell+i)}c)
-(-1)^m b_{(\ell+m-i)}(a_{(k+i)}c)\Bigr).
\]
Truncation \(a_{(n)}b=0\) for \(n\gg0\) is part of the package: only finitely many non-negative modes of \(a\) can hit \(b\).


The modes \(a_{(n)}\) for \(n\ge0\) are the \(n\)-products of the underlying Lie conformal algebra. The new data are the negative modes, packaged as the normally ordered product
\[
:ab:\,=a_{(-1)}b.
\]
Wick’s theorem (or the noncommutative Wick formula) reconstructs the full OPE from the \(\lambda\)-bracket plus this regular part. A vertex algebra is therefore a Lie conformal algebra together with a unital differential product compatible with the \(\lambda\)-bracket. Not every Lie conformal algebra admits such a product; the obstruction lives in conformal cohomology.


Commutative vertex algebras are ordinary commutative differential algebras: \(Y(a,z)b=\bigl(e^{zT}a\bigr)b\), with no negative powers of \(z\).


## 2. What a VOA adds


A **vertex operator algebra** is a vertex algebra with a conformal vector \(\omega\in V_2\) whose modes
\[
Y(\omega,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}L_n z^{-n-2}
\]
satisfy the Virasoro relations
\[
[L_m,L_n]=(m-n)L_{m+n}+\frac{c}{12}(m^3-m)\delta_{m+n,0},
\]
and such that \(L_{-1}=T\), \(L_0\) is diagonalizable, and
\[
V=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z}V_n,\qquad L_0\big|_{V_n}=n\,\mathrm{id}.
\]
Standard extra hypotheses, not always part of the definition: \(\dim V_n<\infty\), \(V_n=0\) for \(n\ll0\), and often \(V_n=0\) for \(n<0\) with \(V_0=\mathbb C\mathbf 1\) (“CFT type”). The scalar \(c\) is the central charge. Frenkel–Lepowsky–Meurman added the Virasoro package to Borcherds’ vertex algebras precisely so that the moonshine module would be an object of this type.


Weight of a homogeneous vector \(a\in V_k\) and mode index are related by
\[
a_{(n)}:V_\ell\to V_{k+\ell-n-1}.
\]
In particular \(L_0\) is the conformal Hamiltonian, and \(L_{-1}\) is infinitesimal translation on the worldsheet.


A vertex operator *super*algebra allows half-integer weights and a parity, with signs in locality. Neveu–Schwarz and Ramond sectors appear as modules, not as a change of the vacuum axioms.


## 3. Reconstruction from Lie conformal data


Given a Lie conformal algebra \(R\) and a vacuum module \(M\) generated by a vector killed by all positive modes, one attempts to define
\[
Y(a,z)=\sum_n a_{(n)}z^{-n-1}
\]
on the induced module. This works, and produces a vertex algebra, when the action is local and the normally ordered products close on a subspace containing the vacuum. Standard sources:


- the vacuum module of Virasoro at central charge \(c\), quotiented by the maximal submodule, gives the **Virasoro VOA** \(M_c\) (or \(L(c,0)\) after irreducibility);
- the vacuum module of an affine Kac–Moody algebra at level \(k\) gives the **affine VOA** \(V_k(\mathfrak g)\);
- the Fock module of a Heisenberg algebra gives the **Heisenberg VOA**, and even lattices \(L\) give **lattice VOAs** \(V_L\) by adjoining vertex operators \(e^\alpha(z)\) for \(\alpha\in L\).


In each case the \(\lambda\)-bracket of generating fields is the Lie conformal structure; the VOA is the universal envelope that also knows how to multiply fields regularly.


## 4. Standard families


**Heisenberg / free bosons.** One generator \(h\) of weight \(1\) with \([h_\lambda h]=\lambda\). The VOA is \(\mathrm{Sym}(h_{(-n)}:n>0)\), central charge \(1\). Several free bosons give \(c=\mathrm{rank}\).


**Affine / WZW.** For \(\mathfrak g\) simple and \(k\in\mathbb C\),
\[
[a_\lambda b]=[a,b]+k\lambda\langle a,b\rangle.
\]
Integrable positive-integer levels produce rational VOAs whose irreducible modules are the integrable highest-weight modules of that level.


**Virasoro.** Generated by \(\omega\) alone. Minimal models \(L(c_{p,q},0)\) at
\[
c=1-\frac{6(p-q)^2}{pq}
\]
are the rational family.


**Lattice.** For an even lattice \(L\), \(V_L=M_{\mathfrak h}\otimes\mathbb C[L]\) with \(\mathfrak h=L\otimes\mathbb C\). Unimodular even \(L\) gives a holomorphic VOA (unique irreducible module, itself). The \(E_8\) lattice and the Leech lattice \(\Lambda_{24}\) are the rank-\(8\) and rank-\(24\) cases.


**Moonshine module.** \(V^\natural\) is the VOA of central charge \(24\) obtained from the Leech lattice by a \(\mathbb Z/2\) orbifold that removes the weight-\(1\) space. Its automorphism group is the Monster, and
\[
\mathrm{tr}_{V^\natural}q^{L_0-c/24}=J(q)=q^{-1}+196884q+\cdots
\]
is the modular invariant function with vanishing constant term. That is the algebraic content of monstrous moonshine. Borcherds proved Conway–Norton’s genus-zero claims by constructing a Monster Lie algebra from \(V^\natural\) by a vertex-algebra analogue of the no-ghost theorem.


## 5. Modules, Zhu’s algebra, modularity


A weak module is a space \(M\) with a map \(Y_M:V\to(\mathrm{End}\,M)[[z,z^{-1}]]\) satisfying the same Borcherds identity and vacuum axiom. Admissible and ordinary modules impose grading and \(L_0\)-bounded-below conditions.


Zhu’s associative algebra \(A(V)=V/O(V)\) has the property that ordinary irreducible \(V\)-modules are in bijection with irreducible \(A(V)\)-modules, via the top \(L_0\)-space. For affine and Virasoro VOAs this recovers the expected highest-weight classification.


A VOA is **rational** if every admissible module is a direct sum of irreducibles, and **\(C_2\)-cofinite** if \(V/\mathrm{span}\{a_{(-2)}b\}\) is finite-dimensional. Zhu’s theorem: if \(V\) is \(C_2\)-cofinite and weakly rational, the characters
\[
\chi_M(\tau)=\mathrm{tr}_M q^{L_0-c/24},\qquad q=e^{2\pi i\tau},
\]
span a representation of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)\). The \(S\)-matrix of that representation, for sufficiently nice VOAs, is the Verlinde matrix of the fusion ring. This is the precise sense in which a rational VOA produces a modular tensor category of representations and, on a pair of opposite VOAs, the chiral data of a rational CFT.


Orbifolds, simple-current extensions, and regular vertex tensor categories are the calculus that builds new VOAs from old ones. Huang–Lepowsky–Zhang tensor products make the module category of a nice VOA braided.


## 6. Poisson and classical limits


The associated graded of the filtration by conformal weight, or the quasiclassical limit \(a_{(n)}\mapsto0\) for \(n\ge1\) after rescaling, produces a **vertex Poisson algebra**: a commutative vertex algebra with a compatible Lie conformal bracket. For affine VOAs this recovers the Lie–Poisson structure on \(\mathfrak g[[z]]\); for Virasoro it recovers the Gelfand–Dikii / Virasoro–Poisson structure on densities. Gelfand–Dorfman algebras re-enter here as the coefficient algebras of *quadratic* vertex Poisson structures — the same correspondence as for quadratic Lie conformal algebras, now with a commutative product in the regular part.


## 7. What this is not, relative to the report


A VOA is not a Hopf algebra of rooted trees, not a Novikov algebra, and not the two-primitive derivation Hopf algebra of §31.4. The rotation algebra \(A_\alpha^\infty\) is a Fréchet algebra of Fourier series with a pair of commuting derivations; it has a trace and cyclic cocycles, but it has no state-field correspondence, no locality in \(z\), and no Virasoro element whose \(L_0\) grades a CFT-type decomposition.


The one-dimensional product \(X\triangleleft Y=YX'\) can be fed into the Novikov → GD → quadratic Lie conformal algebra tower, and that conformal algebra is the singular skeleton of the Heisenberg or Virasoro vertex algebra according to the choice of bracket. The v1.4 correction register never takes that step. Nothing in the 39-screen is a vertex operator, a character \(\chi_M(\tau)\), or a moonshine module.


The useful dictionary, if one ever wanted to import the language, is one sentence: Lie conformal algebras are the singular OPEs; vertex algebras add the regular product and the vacuum; vertex operator algebras add a Virasoro vector that grades energy. The screen supplies none of the three.
Zhu’s algebra \(A(V)\) is the associative algebra of zero modes of a vertex operator algebra, taken modulo the relations forced by positive modes on a lowest-energy space. Irreducible positive-energy \(V\)-modules correspond to irreducible \(A(V)\)-modules via that lowest-energy space.


## 1. The two bilinear operations


Let \(V=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z}V_n\) be a vertex algebra graded by \(L_0\) (or by any conformal weight compatible with \(T=L_{-1}\)). For homogeneous \(u\in V_k\) and arbitrary \(v\in V\) set
\[
u*v=\operatorname{Res}_z\Bigl(Y(u,z)v\,\frac{(1+z)^k}{z}\Bigr)
=\sum_{i\ge0}\binom{k}{i}u_{(i-1)}v,
\]
\[
u\circ v=\operatorname{Res}_z\Bigl(Y(u,z)v\,\frac{(1+z)^k}{z^2}\Bigr)
=\sum_{i\ge0}\binom{k}{i}u_{(i-2)}v.
\]
Extend linearly in \(u\). The subspace
\[
O(V)=\operatorname{span}\{u\circ v:u,v\in V\}
\]
is a two-sided ideal for \(*\). Zhu’s algebra is the quotient
\[
A(V)=V/O(V),
\]
with product induced by \(*\). The image of the vacuum is the unit. Associativity of \(*\) on the quotient is a formal residue identity; van Ekeren–Heluani give a short proof using elliptic functions.


Two useful congruences modulo \(O(V)\):
\[
u*v-v*u\equiv\operatorname{Res}_z\bigl((1+z)^{k-1}Y(u,z)v\bigr)
=\sum_{i\ge0}\binom{k-1}{i}u_{(i)}v,
\]
so the commutator in \(A(V)\) is the image of the zero mode of \(Y(u,z)\). In particular the image of \(\omega\) is central and acts as the scalar \(h(h-1)/2+c/24\) issues aside — on a lowest-energy space of weight \(h\) one has \(o(\omega)=h\), where
\[
o(u)=u_{(k-1)}\qquad(u\in V_k)
\]
is the zero mode.


## 2. Why these formulae


On a positive-energy module \(M=\bigoplus_{n\ge h}M_n\) with \(M_h\neq0\), every field of weight \(k\) satisfies
\[
u_{(n)}M_h=0\qquad\text{for all }n>k-1.
\]
The only mode that can act on \(M_h\) is therefore \(o(u)=u_{(k-1)}\). The factor \((1+z)^k/z\) in \(*\) is the change of coordinate \(z\mapsto e^{2\pi i\tau}-1\) that turns the cylinder into a neighbourhood of the puncture at \(q=0\): Zhu’s product is the composition of zero modes after that change of coordinate. The extra \(1/z\) that defines \(\circ\) generates the relations among those zero modes coming from derivatives and from modes that already annihilate \(M_h\).


Thus \(M_h\) becomes an \(A(V)\)-module by \([u]\cdot m=o(u)m\). This is the only input the construction uses.


## 3. The correspondence


Let \(\Omega(M)=\{m\in M:u_{(n)}m=0\text{ whenever }u\in V_k,\,n>k-1\}\) be the space of singular vectors in the weak sense. For an ordinary (positive-energy, \(L_0\)-diagonalizable) module, \(\Omega(M)=M_h\).


**Zhu’s theorem.** 


- If \(M\) is an ordinary \(V\)-module then \(\Omega(M)\) is an \(A(V)\)-module.
- Every irreducible \(A(V)\)-module \(U\) is \(\Omega(M)\) for a unique (up to isomorphism) irreducible ordinary \(V\)-module \(M\), obtained by inducing \(U\) along the modes and quotienting by the maximal submodule in positive degree.
- Equivalence classes of irreducible ordinary \(V\)-modules \(\leftrightarrow\) equivalence classes of irreducible \(A(V)\)-modules.


For a rational VOA, \(A(V)\) is finite-dimensional semisimple,
\[
A(V)\;\cong\;\bigoplus_M\operatorname{End}(M_h),
\]
the sum running over irreducibles. That is the associative-algebra shadow of semisimplicity of \(\operatorname{Rep}(V)\).


## 4. Standard computations


**Heisenberg / free boson.** \(A(M_{\mathfrak h})\cong\mathbb C[h]\), a polynomial algebra in the zero mode. Irreducible ordinary modules are Fock spaces labelled by the momentum \(\alpha\in\mathfrak h^*\).


**Universal affine VOA.** For \(k\neq-h^\vee\),
\[
A(V^k(\mathfrak g))\;\cong\;U(\mathfrak g).
\]
The isomorphism sends the image of \(xt^{-1}\mathbf 1\) to \(x\in\mathfrak g\). Ordinary irreducible \(V^k(\mathfrak g)\)-modules therefore have top spaces that are arbitrary irreducible \(\mathfrak g\)-modules; smoothness of the affine action is automatic on the induced module. Passing to the simple quotient \(L_k(\mathfrak g)\) quotients \(U(\mathfrak g)\) further by an ideal \(I_k\), and \(L(\lambda)\) is an \(L_k(\mathfrak g)\)-module if and only if \(I_k\) annihilates \(L_{\mathfrak g}(\bar\lambda)\). At integrable positive-integer level this recovers the finitely many integrable highest weights.


**Universal Virasoro.** \(A(\mathrm{Vir}_c)\cong\mathbb C[x]\), with \(x\) the image of \(\omega\). Quotienting by the maximal ideal that produces \(L(c,0)\) imposes a polynomial relation; for minimal models \(A(L(c_{p,q},0))\) is a finite-dimensional semisimple algebra whose irreducibles are the Kac table weights.


**Lattice.** For \(V_L\) the algebra \(A(V_L)\) is a quotient of a smash product of the group algebra of \(L\) by a Clifford / Weyl piece coming from the Heisenberg zero modes. Rank-one even lattices give explicit presentations such as \(\mathbb C[H]/(H(H-1)\cdots(H-2k+1)-\text{correction})\).


## 5. The \(C_2\)-algebra is a different quotient


Zhu also introduced
\[
C_2(V)=\operatorname{span}\{u_{(-2)}v\},\qquad R_V=V/C_2(V).
\]
The product \(u_{(-1)}v\) descends to a commutative Poisson structure on \(R_V\), with bracket from \(u_{(0)}v\). \(C_2\)-cofiniteness means \(\dim R_V<\infty\).


There is a surjective Poisson map \(R_V\to\mathrm{gr}\,A(V)\), where the associated graded of \(A(V)\) is taken with respect to the filtration inherited from conformal weight. The map is not always an isomorphism: Gaberdiel–Gannon observed that \(E_8\) affine VOAs (and some others) have \(\dim R_V>\dim A(V)\). So \(C_2\)-cofiniteness is a finiteness condition used in modular-invariance proofs; it is not a substitute for \(A(V)\) in the classification of modules. A suggested interpretation is that \(R_V\) sees twisted modules that \(A(V)\) does not.


## 6. Higher-level Zhu algebras


Dong–Li–Mason defined \(A_n(V)\) by replacing \((1+z)^k/z\) with \((1+z)^{k+n}/z^{n+1}\) and enlarging the ideal. Then \(A_0(V)=A(V)\), there are projections \(A_{n+1}(V)\twoheadrightarrow A_n(V)\), and \(A_n(V)\)-modules correspond to admissible \(V\)-modules whose first nonvanishing piece can sit in degree \(\le n\). These algebras are the correct tool for logarithmic and indecomposable modules: already for the Heisenberg VOA, \(A_2(V)\) is noncommutative, and Virasoro \(A_1\) induces logarithmic modules that \(A(V)\) cannot see.


Twisted Zhu algebras \(A_g(V)\) do the same job for \(g\)-twisted modules, \(g\in\mathrm{Aut}(V)\).


## 7. Role in modular invariance


Zhu’s original application was not only classification. The change of coordinate \(z\mapsto e^{2\pi i\tau}-1\) that defines \(*\) is the same change that turns a trace
\[
\chi_M(\tau,u)=\mathrm{tr}_M o(u)\,q^{L_0-c/24}
\]
into an elliptic one-point function on the torus. \(C_2\)-cofiniteness supplies a finite spanning set of such functions; rationality plus \(C_2\)-cofiniteness makes them modular of weight equal to the conformal weight of \(u\). The zero-mode algebra is what lets one reduce an arbitrary insertion to an insertion of an element of \(A(V)\) on the top space, which is why modularity of characters is a theorem about \(A(V)\) as much as about \(V\).


## 8. What this is not


Zhu’s algebra is an ordinary unital associative algebra. It is not a vertex algebra, not a Lie conformal algebra, not a Novikov or Gelfand–Dorfman algebra, and not a Hopf algebra of trees.


The rotation algebra \(A_\alpha^\infty\) of the 39-screen already *is* an associative algebra (in fact a Fréchet completion of a twisted group algebra). Forming \(A(V)\) for a VOA is a reduction *to* an associative algebra; it is not a construction that starts from \(A_\alpha^\infty\). There is no conformal vector, no state-field map, and no lowest-energy space in the screen whose zero modes would reproduce occupation of bins. The trace \(\mathsf T\) of §30 is a cyclic-cohomology trace on Fourier modes, not a Zhu one-point function \(\mathrm{tr}\,o(u)q^{L_0-c/24}\).


The only accurate sentence connecting the two is negative: Zhu’s construction explains how a chiral algebra forgets all but its zero modes on ground states; the screen never had those modes to forget.
For the Virasoro vertex algebra the story of Zhu’s algebra is completely explicit: the universal object has \(A\cong\mathbb C[x]\), and every relation that appears after quotienting by a singular vector is a polynomial in that one generator. Irreducible ordinary modules are therefore labelled by the roots of that polynomial.


## 1. The universal Virasoro VOA


Fix a central charge \(c\in\mathbb C\). The Virasoro Lie algebra acts on the one-dimensional module \(\mathbb C_c\) by \(L_n\mapsto0\) for \(n\ge-1\) and \(C\mapsto c\). The induced module
\[
M_c=\mathrm{Vir}_c=U(\mathrm{Vir})\otimes_{U(\mathrm{Vir}_{\ge-1}\oplus\mathbb C C)}\mathbb C_c
\]
is the **universal Virasoro vertex algebra**. As a vector space it has PBW basis
\[
L_{-n_1}\cdots L_{-n_k}\mathbf 1,\qquad n_1\ge\cdots\ge n_k\ge2,
\]
conformal vector \(\omega=L_{-2}\mathbf 1\), and \(T=L_{-1}\). It is generated by a single field \(L(z)=Y(\omega,z)\).


On this algebra the Zhu operations collapse. For any \(v\) and \(n\ge1\),
\[
L_{-n}v\equiv\bigl((-1)^n\bigl((n-1)(L_{-2}+L_{-1})+L_0\bigr)\bigr)v\pmod{O(M_c)}.
\]
Combined with \([(L_{-2}+L_{-1})u]=[\omega]*[u]\), every monomial in negative modes reduces to a polynomial in \([\omega]\). The result is Frenkel–Zhu’s isomorphism
\[
A(M_c)\;\cong\;\mathbb C[x],\qquad x=[\omega].
\]
The generator acts on a lowest-energy space of weight \(h\) as multiplication by \(h\), because \(o(\omega)=L_0\). So irreducible \(A(M_c)\)-modules are one-dimensional, labelled by \(h\in\mathbb C\), and Zhu’s correspondence recovers the ordinary irreducible \(M_c\)-modules: the simple quotients \(L(c,h)\) of the Verma modules \(M(c,h)\). Every complex weight is allowed; there is no polynomial relation yet.


## 2. Passing to a quotient: how a singular vector becomes a polynomial


Let \(I\subset M_c\) be a graded vertex ideal and \(V=M_c/I\). Right exactness of \(A(-)\) gives
\[
A(V)\;\cong\;A(M_c)\big/A(I)\;\cong\;\mathbb C[x]\big/(f),
\]
where \(f\) is the image of a generating set of \(I\) under the projection \(M_c\to A(M_c)\). For Virasoro the ideals of interest are generated by singular vectors, and Feigin–Fuchs supply those vectors explicitly.


A vector \(v\in M(c,h)\) of level \(N\) is singular when \(L_n v=0\) for all \(n>0\). Such a vector exists at level \(rs\) precisely when
\[
h=h_{r,s}(c)=\frac{(rt-s)^2-(t-1)^2}{4t},
\]
where \(c=13-6(t+1/t)\). Its image in \(A(M_c)\) is a scalar multiple of
\[
x-h_{r,s}(c)\qquad\text{or a product of such linear factors,}
\]
according to whether one is quotienting a Verma module or the vacuum module \(M_c=M(c,0)\). Wang’s computation makes this effective for the vacuum: the first nontrivial singular vector of \(M(c,0)\) projects to a monic polynomial \(G(x)\in\mathbb C[x]\), and
\[
A(L(c,0))\;\cong\;\mathbb C[x]/(G(x)).
\]


## 3. Minimal models


The vacuum module \(M(c,0)\) is reducible if and only if
\[
c=c_{p,q}=1-\frac{6(p-q)^2}{pq}
\]
for coprime integers \(p,q\ge2\). The simple quotient \(L(c_{p,q},0)\) is the **\((p,q)\)-minimal model**. It is rational and \(C_2\)-cofinite.


Wang’s theorem:
\[
A\bigl(L(c_{p,q},0)\bigr)\;\cong\;\mathbb C[x]\Big/\prod_{\substack{1\le r\le p-1\\1\le s\le q-1\\(r,s)\le(p-r,q-s)}}(x-h_{r,s}),
\]
the product running over distinct Kac-table weights (identify \((r,s)\) with \((p-r,q-s)\)). The degree is
\[
\deg G_{p,q}=\frac{(p-1)(q-1)}{2},
\]
which is exactly the number of inequivalent irreducible ordinary modules. Each linear factor \(x-h_{r,s}\) cuts out the one-dimensional top space of \(L(c_{p,q},h_{r,s})\). Semisimplicity of \(A\) is the associative-algebra form of rationality.


**The \((2,5)\) model**, \(c=-22/5\), is small enough to see by hand. The Kac table has two weights, \(0\) and \(-1/5\). The singular vector of \(M_c\) at level \(2\) reduces, after the Zhu rewriting \(L_{-n}\equiv(-1)^n\bigl((n-1)[\omega]+n\cdot(\text{shift})\bigr)\), to the relation
\[
[\omega]*\bigl([\omega]+\tfrac15\bigr)=0
\]
in \(A(M_c)\). Hence
\[
A\bigl(L(-22/5,0)\bigr)\;\cong\;\mathbb C[x]\big/x\bigl(x+\tfrac15\bigr),
\]
and the two irreducibles are the vacuum and \(L(-22/5,-1/5)\). That is the Lee–Yang model.


**The Ising model** \((3,4)\), \(c=1/2\), has
\[
G_{3,4}(x)=x\Bigl(x-\frac1{16}\Bigr)\Bigl(x-\frac12\Bigr),
\]
matching \(h=0,1/16,1/2\).


Because \(A\) is commutative of dimension equal to the number of irreducibles, each irreducible ordinary module has one-dimensional top space. That is special to Virasoro (and to \(W\)-algebras whose Zhu algebra is the centre of an enveloping algebra): there is only one field of weight \(2\), so only one zero mode survives on ground states.


## 4. What the polynomial does not see


Zhu’s algebra classifies *ordinary irreducible* modules: \(L_0\) diagonalizable and bounded below, top space an irreducible \(A(V)\)-module. It does not classify:


- Verma modules that have not been reduced (those are modules for \(M_c\), not for \(L(c,0)\));
- logarithmic modules, on which \(L_0\) has a Jordan block;
- relaxed modules whose spectrum of \(L_0\) is not bounded below.


For those one needs higher-level Zhu algebras \(A_n(M_c)\) or \(A_n(L(c,0))\). Already \(A_1\) of the universal Virasoro algebra induces indecomposable modules that are not highest-weight; Barron–Vander Werf–Yang construct a family of logarithmic Virasoro modules this way. For the triplet algebra \(\mathcal W(p)\), an extension of \(L(c_{p,1},0)\), Zhu’s algebra itself is non-semisimple and its indecomposable modules induce the well-known rank-two logarithmic \(\mathcal W(p)\)-modules. The minimal model \(L(c_{p,q},0)\) is rational, so \(A_n\) for \(n\ge1\) does not produce new irreducibles; it only rearranges extensions that split.


## 5. \(C_2\)-algebra of Virasoro


For \(M_c\) one has \(R_{M_c}\cong\mathbb C[x]\), the same polynomial ring, and the Poisson map \(R\to\mathrm{gr}\,A\) is an isomorphism: the universal Virasoro algebra is classically free. For minimal models \(\mathrm{Vir}_{2,q}\) the same holds. The equality \(\dim R_V=\dim A(V)\) is the typical situation for Virasoro and fails for examples such as \(L_1(E_8)\), not here.


## 6. The correspondence, specialised


| Object | Zhu algebra | Irreducible modules |
|---|---|---|
| \(M_c\) | \(\mathbb C[x]\) | \(L(c,h)\) for all \(h\in\mathbb C\) |
| \(L(c,0)\) at generic \(c\) | \(\mathbb C[x]/(x)\) | only the vacuum |
| \(L(c_{p,q},0)\) | \(\mathbb C[x]/G_{p,q}(x)\) | \(L(c_{p,q},h_{r,s})\), Kac table |
| \(\mathcal W(p)\) | non-semisimple, finite-dimensional | ordinary plus logarithmic |


In every Virasoro case the generator is the image of \(\omega\), and evaluating that generator on a top space is evaluating \(L_0\). There is no other generator and no noncommutative residue.


That is the whole construction for this family: one free generator coming from the unique weight-two field, cut down by the Feigin–Fuchs singular vector of the vacuum module, with roots equal to the admissible conformal weights.
The Zhu algebra of an affine \(W\)-algebra is not a new associative algebra invented for the vertex operators: it is the finite \(W\)-algebra of the same pair \((\mathfrak g,f)\). That identification is the whole structural fact. Everything else — principal series, minimal models, logarithmic triplets — is a specialisation of it.


## 1. The two \(W\)-algebras


Let \(\mathfrak g\) be simple finite-dimensional and \(f\in\mathfrak g\) nilpotent. Choose an \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\)-triple \(\{e,h,f\}\) and a good grading.


The **affine \(W\)-algebra** \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\) is the vertex algebra obtained by quantized Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction
\[
W^k(\mathfrak g,f)=H^0_f\bigl(V^k(\mathfrak g)\bigr),
\]
the BRST cohomology of the universal affine vertex algebra at level \(k\) with respect to the nilpotent \(f\). It is generated by fields whose weights are the exponents of the Slodowy grading; for principal \(f\) those weights are the exponents of \(\mathfrak g\) plus one (so \(2,3,\dots,\mathrm{rank}+1\) in type \(A\)).


The **finite \(W\)-algebra** \(U(\mathfrak g,f)\) (Premet, Gan–Ginzburg) is the associative algebra obtained by the analogous Hamiltonian reduction of \(U(\mathfrak g)\): Whittaker coinvariants, or equivalently a quantization of the Poisson algebra of functions on the Slodowy slice
\[
\mathcal S_f=f+\mathfrak g^e.
\]
When \(f=0\) one recovers \(U(\mathfrak g)\). When \(f\) is principal one recovers the centre \(Z(\mathfrak g)\).


De Sole–Kac: the \(H\)-twisted Zhu algebra of the affine object is the finite object,
\[
A\bigl(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\bigr)\;\cong\;U(\mathfrak g,f).
\]
The isomorphism is independent of \(k\) as an abstract algebra; \(k\) reappears when one quotients to the simple vertex algebra \(W_k(\mathfrak g,f)\). The associated graded of both sides is \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_f]\). That is why Zhu’s construction, for this family, is Hamiltonian reduction rather than a mode-by-mode computation.


## 2. Principal nilpotent: the centre


If \(f\) is principal, \(U(\mathfrak g,f)\cong Z(\mathfrak g)\), so
\[
A\bigl(W^k(\mathfrak g)\bigr)\;\cong\;Z(\mathfrak g)\;\cong\;\mathbb C[p_1,\dots,p_r],
\]
a polynomial ring on \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathfrak g)\) generators, the images of the fundamental invariants. Irreducible ordinary modules of the *universal* principal \(W\)-algebra are therefore labelled by central characters of \(\mathfrak g\), i.e. by Weyl orbits of weights, exactly as highest-weight theory of \(\mathfrak g\) would suggest. Arakawa’s early computation records this as \(A(W_\kappa(\bar{\mathfrak g}))\cong Z(\bar{\mathfrak g})\).


Special cases:


- \(\mathfrak g=\mathfrak{sl}_2\), principal \(f\): \(W^k(\mathfrak{sl}_2)\cong M_{c(k)}\), the universal Virasoro algebra, and \(Z(\mathfrak{sl}_2)=\mathbb C[x]\). This is the isomorphism \(A(M_c)\cong\mathbb C[x]\) of the previous note.
- \(\mathfrak g=\mathfrak{sl}_3\): \(W^k(\mathfrak{sl}_3)\) is the universal Zamolodchikov \(\mathcal W_3\)-algebra, generated by \(\omega\) and a weight-three field \(W\). Then
  \[
  A(W^k(\mathfrak{sl}_3))\;\cong\;Z(\mathfrak{sl}_3)\;\cong\;\mathbb C[c_2,c_3],
  \]
  polynomials in the Casimirs. On a top space the two generators act as the values of those Casimirs, equivalently as a pair \((h,w)\) subject to no relation yet.


The Miura map realises \(W^k(\mathfrak g)\) inside a Heisenberg vertex algebra of rank \(r\), and on Zhu algebras it becomes the Harish-Chandra homomorphism \(Z(\mathfrak g)\hookrightarrow\mathbb C[\mathfrak h^*]^W\).


## 3. Simple quotients and minimal series


Let \(W_k(\mathfrak g,f)\) be the unique simple quotient of \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\). Right exactness gives
\[
A\bigl(W_k(\mathfrak g,f)\bigr)\;\cong\;U(\mathfrak g,f)\big/I_k,
\]
where \(I_k\) is the image of the maximal vertex ideal. For principal \(f\) and \(k\) a **minimal-series** (nondegenerate admissible) level,
\[
k+h^\vee=\frac{p}{q}\quad(p,q\text{ coprime, }q\ge h^\vee),
\]
Frenkel–Kac–Wakimoto predicted rationality. Arakawa proved it: \(W_k(\mathfrak g)\) is rational and \(C_2\)-cofinite, and the key algebraic step is that taking Zhu algebras commutes with Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction. Consequently \(A(W_k(\mathfrak g))\) is finite-dimensional semisimple, of dimension equal to the number of admissible weights in the reduced Kac table, and
\[
A\bigl(W_k(\mathfrak g)\bigr)\;\cong\;\bigoplus_{\lambda} \operatorname{End}(\mathbb C)\;\cong\;\mathbb C^{\#\{\text{miniseries weights}\}},
\]
since top spaces remain one-dimensional (the finite \(W\)-algebra is commutative). The linear factors of the ideal \(I_k\subset Z(\mathfrak g)\) are the evaluations of the Harish-Chandra isomorphism on those admissible central characters.


For Virasoro this is Wang’s polynomial \(G_{p,q}(x)\). For \(\mathcal W_3\) at a minimal-series level the ideal in \(\mathbb C[c_2,c_3]\) is generated by the vanishing of all but the finitely many admissible \((h,w)\) pairs.


At admissible levels with \(f\) in the orbit \(\mathbb O_q\) determined by the denominator, \(A(W_k(\mathfrak g,f))\) is likewise semisimple. The associated variety of the simple affine algebra is then a nilpotent orbit closure, and the associated variety of the \(W\)-algebra is the corresponding nilpotent Slodowy slice; when that slice is a point, the Zhu algebra is artinian of maximal length.


## 4. Non-principal \(f\)


For general nilpotent \(f\), \(U(\mathfrak g,f)\) is noncommutative. Its finite-dimensional irreducibles classify Whittaker-type modules for \(\mathfrak g\) with fixed infinitesimal character, and by Zhu they classify ordinary irreducible \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\)-modules. Losev’s Skryabin equivalence
\[
\{\text{Whittaker }\mathfrak g\text{-modules of type }f\}
\;\longleftrightarrow\;
\{\text{modules for }U(\mathfrak g,f)\}
\]
is the Lie-theoretic original of Zhu’s correspondence in this setting.


Minimal nilpotent \(f\) produces the Bershadsky–Polyakov algebra (for \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\)) and its higher-rank analogues. The Zhu algebra is then a finite \(W\)-algebra of a much smaller slice, typically with a nontrivial \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) or \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\times\mathfrak t\) action, and top spaces of ordinary modules can have dimension greater than one.


## 5. Logarithmic \(W\)-algebras that are not DS reductions


The name “\(W\)-algebra” is also used for lattice orbifolds that extend a Virasoro minimal model: the triplet \(\mathcal W(p)\), the \(c=0\) algebra \(\mathcal W_{2,3}\), the singlet \(\overline{M(p)}\), and the \(N=1\) super-triplet \(\mathcal{SW}(m)\). These are not \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\) for a finite-dimensional \(\mathfrak g\). Their Zhu algebras must be computed internally.


Adamović–Milas: \(\mathcal W(p)\) admits a horizontal \(\mathbb Z\)-grading, and
\[
A(\mathcal W(p))
\]
is a finite-dimensional non-semisimple algebra whose semisimple quotient is \(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p-1}\mathbb C\) (ordinary modules \(L(c_{p,1},h_{i,1})\)) and whose radical produces the two-dimensional top spaces of the projective logarithmic modules \(\mathcal P^\pm\). In this family \(\dim A(\mathcal W(p))=\dim R_{\mathcal W(p)}\), so the Poisson map from the \(C_2\)-algebra is an isomorphism of vector spaces. For \(\mathcal W_{2,3}\) the centre of \(A\) is \(\mathbb C[x]/\langle f_{2,3}(x)\rangle\), twenty-dimensional, and the algebra admits a module on which \(L_0\) has a Jordan block of size three.


These computations use constant-term identities and the horizontal grading; they do not factor through a finite \(W\)-algebra of a simple Lie algebra.

| Vertex algebra | Zhu algebra | Commutative? |
|---|---|---|

| \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\) | \(U(\mathfrak g,f)\) | iff \(f\) principal |

| \(M_c=W^k(\mathfrak{sl}_2)\) | \(\mathbb C[x]\) | yes |
| \(L(c_{p,q},0)\) | \(\mathbb C[x]/G_{p,q}(x)\) | yes, semisimple |

| \(\mathcal W(p)\) triplet | non-semisimple, finite-dimensional | no |
| \(\mathcal W_{2,3}\) | non-semisimple, centre \(\mathbb C[x]/\langle f_{2,3}\rangle\) | no |


## 7. What the identification buys


Once \(A(W^k(\mathfrak g,f))\cong U(\mathfrak g,f)\) is known, every theorem about finite \(W\)-algebras becomes a theorem about ordinary modules of the affine \(W\)-algebra: finite-dimensionality of irreducibles for integral central characters, the category \(\mathcal O\) of Whittaker modules, associated varieties equal to Slodowy slices, and collapsing levels (where \(W_k(\mathfrak g,f)\) becomes an affine vertex algebra of smaller rank) as the slice degenerating.


What it does not buy is a description of logarithmic modules. Those require either a non-semisimple finite \(W\)-algebra (rare for simple \(\mathfrak g\)) or an extension such as \(\mathcal W(p)\), whose Zhu algebra is computed by hand.


Relative to the 39-screen there is again no map: a \(W\)-algebra is a vertex algebra of currents with a prescribed nilpotent reduction, and Zhu’s algebra of that object is a finite Hamiltonian reduction of \(U(\mathfrak g)\). Neither structure is present among the rotation generators \(U,V\) or the primitives \(P_1,P_2\).
The De Sole–Kac isomorphism is the statement that Zhu’s functor, applied to an affine \(W\)-algebra, returns the finite \(W\)-algebra of the same pair. It is not a computation of generators and relations. It is the assertion that two Hamiltonian reductions — one vertex-algebraic, one associative — commute with the passage from fields to zero modes.


## 1. The statement


Let \(\mathfrak g\) be simple finite-dimensional, \(f\in\mathfrak g\) nilpotent, and \(k\in\mathbb C\). Write \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\) for the affine \(W\)-algebra (quantized Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction of \(V^k(\mathfrak g)\)) and \(U(\mathfrak g,f)\) for the finite \(W\)-algebra (Whittaker reduction of \(U(\mathfrak g)\)). Let \(H\) be the Hamiltonian coming from the good grading, so \(H\) acts on a generator of conformal weight \(\Delta\) by multiplication by \(\Delta\).


**Theorem (De Sole–Kac, 2006).** There is a canonical isomorphism of unital associative algebras
\[
\operatorname{Zhu}_H\bigl(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\bigr)\;\cong\;U(\mathfrak g,f).
\]
The isomorphism does not depend on the level \(k\) as an abstract algebra. Level dependence appears only after one quotients \(W^k\) to its simple quotient \(W_k\).


The left-hand side is the **\(H\)-twisted Zhu algebra**, not the original \(\mathbb Z\)-graded Zhu algebra of Zhu 1996. For a vertex algebra that is graded by a Hamiltonian \(H\) with not necessarily integral eigenvalues (good gradings of \(W\)-algebras are typically \(\frac12\mathbb Z\)-graded), the product and the ideal \(O(V)\) are written with \(e^{2\pi i H}\) in place of the integer weight:
\[
u*v=\operatorname{Res}_z Y(u,z)v\,\frac{(1+z)^{\Delta_u}}{z},
\]
and \(O_H(V)\) is spanned by the analogous residues with \(z^{-2}\), now taken only among eigenvectors whose weights differ by an integer. When all conformal weights are integral and \(H=L_0\), this recovers \(A(V)\). For a general good grading it is the correct algebra controlling ordinary positive-energy modules.


## 2. Why the theorem is not tautological


Both sides are Hamiltonian reductions of enveloping objects attached to \(\mathfrak g\):


\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
V^k(\mathfrak g) & \xrightarrow{\;H^0_{\mathrm{DS},f}\;} & W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\\
\big\downarrow{\scriptstyle \operatorname{Zhu}_H} && \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle \operatorname{Zhu}_H}\\
U(\mathfrak g) & \xrightarrow{\;H^0_{\chi,f}\;} & U(\mathfrak g,f).
\end{array}
\]


The left vertical arrow is Frenkel–Zhu: \(A(V^k(\mathfrak g))\cong U(\mathfrak g)\). The bottom arrow is Premet / Gan–Ginzburg. The top arrow is Feigin–Frenkel / Kac–Roan–Wakimoto. The theorem says the square commutes. That is a theorem because Zhu’s functor is not exact, BRST cohomology does not automatically pass to a quotient by \(O(V)\), and the conformal grading on the BRST complex is not the same as the Kazhdan filtration on \(U(\mathfrak g)\). One has to check that the differential of the BRST complex preserves the Zhu ideal and that the resulting cohomology is the Whittaker reduction.


## 3. The route through non-linear Lie conformal algebras


De Sole–Kac do not compute residues on generating fields of \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\). They lift the problem one categorical level.


A **non-linear Lie conformal algebra** is a \(\mathbb C[\partial]\)-module \(R\) with a \(\lambda\)-bracket valued in the free vertex algebra on \(R\), subject to sesquilinearity, skew-symmetry and Jacobi up to terms of lower filtration. Its universal enveloping vertex algebra \(V(R)\) is freely generated by \(R\) (PBW). Affine vertex algebras and affine \(W\)-algebras are of this type.


To such an \(R\) they attach an **\(H\)-twisted Zhu non-linear Lie algebra** \(\operatorname{Zhu}_H R\): the same underlying space, with ordinary (non-conformal) bracket obtained by extracting the constant term of the \(\lambda\)-bracket after the same \((1+z)^\Delta\) twist. The universal enveloping algebra of that non-linear Lie algebra is then isomorphic to \(\operatorname{Zhu}_H V(R)\):
\[
U(\operatorname{Zhu}_H R)\;\cong\;\operatorname{Zhu}_H V(R).
\]
This is the general mechanism, and it is why the theorem is insensitive to the particular form of the \(W\)-relations: once \(W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\) is known to be the universal envelope of a non-linear Lie conformal algebra \(R_W\), it is enough to identify \(\operatorname{Zhu}_H R_W\) with the non-linear Lie algebra whose envelope is \(U(\mathfrak g,f)\).


That identification is the content of the BRST comparison in their §4–5: the same complex of charged fermions and currents, reduced once as a vertex algebra and once as an associative algebra, yields isomorphic Zhu non-linear Lie algebras.


## 4. Classical shadow


The same square in the quasiclassical limit is a theorem about Poisson (vertex) algebras. The classical affine \(W\)-algebra \(\mathcal W(\mathfrak g,f)\) is a Poisson vertex algebra of functions on the current space, and its Zhu Poisson algebra (take \(z\to0\) in the family \(\operatorname{Zhu}_z\)) is the Poisson algebra of functions on the Slodowy slice. De Sole–Kac–Valeri later wrote the Poisson brackets on both sides explicitly and obtained
\[
\operatorname{Zhu}_z\bigl(\mathcal W(\mathfrak g,f)\bigr)\;\cong\;\mathcal W^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathfrak g,f)
\]
as Poisson algebras, for every value of the deformation parameter \(z\). The quantum isomorphism is the deformation of this Poisson isomorphism.


## 5. Specialisations


**Principal nilpotent.** \(U(\mathfrak g,f_{\mathrm{prin}})\cong Z(\mathfrak g)\), so
\[
\operatorname{Zhu}_H W^k(\mathfrak g)\;\cong\;Z(\mathfrak g).
\]
This is Arakawa’s earlier computation for principal \(W\)-algebras, recovered as the commutative case of De Sole–Kac. For \(\mathfrak g=\mathfrak{sl}_2\) one has \(W^k(\mathfrak{sl}_2)\cong M_{c(k)}\) and \(Z(\mathfrak{sl}_2)=\mathbb C[x]\), which is \(A(M_c)\cong\mathbb C[x]\).


**Zero nilpotent.** \(W^k(\mathfrak g,0)=V^k(\mathfrak g)\) and \(U(\mathfrak g,0)=U(\mathfrak g)\), so the theorem specialises to Frenkel–Zhu.


**Minimal nilpotent.** The finite \(W\)-algebra is noncommutative of a much smaller slice; ordinary modules of the Bershadsky–Polyakov algebra and its cousins are modules for that finite \(W\)-algebra.


The isomorphism does not see the simple quotient. For \(W_k(\mathfrak g,f)=W^k(\mathfrak g,f)/J_{\max}\) one has only a surjection
\[
U(\mathfrak g,f)\twoheadrightarrow A(W_k(\mathfrak g,f)),
\]
and the kernel is the image of \(J_{\max}\). At minimal-series levels that kernel is such that the quotient is finite-dimensional semisimple (Arakawa); the De Sole–Kac isomorphism is the reason one can compute that kernel on the finite-\(W\) side.


## 6. What the isomorphism does for representations


Zhu’s correspondence plus De Sole–Kac gives a bijection
\[
\bigl\{\text{ordinary irreducible }W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\text{-modules}\bigr\}
\;\longleftrightarrow\;
\bigl\{\text{finite-dimensional irreducible }U(\mathfrak g,f)\text{-modules}\bigr\}.
\]
Skryabin’s equivalence further identifies the right-hand side with Whittaker \(\mathfrak g\)-modules of type \(f\) and fixed infinitesimal character. Highest-weight modules of a principal \(W\)-algebra are therefore ordinary modules labelled by central characters of \(\mathfrak g\), which is why the Kac table of a minimal-series \(W\)-algebra is a set of Weyl orbits.


The isomorphism does *not* classify logarithmic modules, twisted modules for automorphisms other than \(\theta_H\), or modules that are not positive-energy. Those require the more general \((g,H)\)-twisted Zhu algebras of later work (Genra–Song–Suh, and the 2024 extension of De Sole–Kac to \((g,H)\)-twists), which identify
\[
\operatorname{Zhu}_{g,H}W^k(\mathfrak g,f)\;\cong\;U(\mathfrak g^{g\circ\theta_H^{-1}},f)
\]
and thereby reduce twisted ordinary modules to finite \(W\)-algebras of fixed-point Lie algebras.


## 7. What it is not


It is not a presentation of \(A(W^k(\mathfrak g,f))\) by generators and relations in the mode algebra of \(W\). It does not compute the Zhu algebra of the triplet \(\mathcal W(p)\) or of \(\mathcal W_{2,3}\), which are not Drinfeld–Sokolov reductions of a finite-dimensional \(\mathfrak g\). It is not a statement about the \(C_2\)-algebra: the associated graded of \(U(\mathfrak g,f)\) is \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_f]\), while \(R_{W^k}\) is a Poisson vertex-algebra reduction whose spectrum is the same slice, but the two filtrations need not coincide after passing to simple quotients.


Relative to the 39-screen there is nothing to transport. The isomorphism compares two reductions of enveloping objects built from a simple Lie algebra and a nilpotent. The rotation algebra is neither.
Skryabin equivalence is the statement that modules for a finite \(W\)-algebra are the same thing as Whittaker modules for \(\mathfrak g\). Combined with De Sole–Kac, it is the reason ordinary modules of an affine \(W\)-algebra are Whittaker modules for \(\mathfrak g\) whose annihilator is supported on the corresponding nilpotent orbit.


## 1. The two categories


Fix a nilpotent \(e\in\mathfrak g\), an \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\)-triple \(\{e,h,f\}\), and a good grading. Let \(\mathfrak m\subset\mathfrak g\) be a nilpotent subalgebra on which \(\chi=(e,\,\cdot\,)\) is a nondegenerate character, and write \(\mathfrak m_\chi=\{x-\chi(x):x\in\mathfrak m\}\).


A \(\mathfrak g\)-module \(E\) is a **Whittaker module** of type \(\chi\) if every \(x-\chi(x)\), \(x\in\mathfrak m\), acts locally nilpotently. Write \(\mathrm{Wh}(\mathfrak g,\chi)\) for the category of such modules (usually restricted to finitely generated ones). The space of Whittaker vectors
\[
\mathrm{Wh}(E)=\{v\in E:(x-\chi(x))v=0\text{ for all }x\in\mathfrak m\}
\]
is nonzero whenever \(E\) is.


The finite \(W\)-algebra is the endomorphism ring
\[
U(\mathfrak g,e)=\mathrm{End}_{U(\mathfrak g)}\bigl(U(\mathfrak g)/U(\mathfrak g)\mathfrak m_\chi\bigr)^{\mathrm{op}},
\]
equivalently Premet’s quantum Hamiltonian reduction, equivalently Gan–Ginzburg’s quantization of \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\). Write \(Q_\chi=U(\mathfrak g)/U(\mathfrak g)\mathfrak m_\chi\), a \((U(\mathfrak g),U(\mathfrak g,e))\)-bimodule.


## 2. The equivalence


**Theorem (Skryabin, appendix to Premet 2002).** The functors
\[
\begin{align*}
U(\mathfrak g,e)\text{-Mod}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Wh}(\mathfrak g,\chi),
&N&\longmapsto Q_\chi\otimes_{U(\mathfrak g,e)}N,\\
\mathrm{Wh}(\mathfrak g,\chi)&\longrightarrow U(\mathfrak g,e)\text{-Mod},
&E&\longmapsto\mathrm{Wh}(E)
\end{align*}
\]
are quasi-inverse equivalences of abelian categories.


Gan–Ginzburg gave a different proof as part of their Slodowy-slice quantization. Goodwin extended the statement from Dynkin gradings to arbitrary good gradings. The modular analogue (characteristic \(p\), \(p\) large or in a suitable restricted setting) is due to Topley and Goodwin–Topley: \(Q\) is free of rank \(p^{d_\chi}\) over the modular finite \(W\)-algebra, and the equivalence becomes a Morita equivalence with a matrix ring of that size.


The essential formal point is that \(Q_\chi\) is a projective generator of the Whittaker category, with endomorphism ring \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\). Once that is known, the equivalence is Morita theory.


## 3. What the functors do to annihilators


If \(I\subset U(\mathfrak g)\) is a two-sided ideal, the equivalence restricts to
\[
\mathrm{Wh}(\mathfrak g,\chi)^I\;\simeq\;\bigl(U(\mathfrak g,e)/I^\dagger\bigr)\text{-Mod},
\]
where \(I^\dagger\) is Losev’s image of \(I\) in the finite \(W\)-algebra. In the other direction, a finitely generated \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\)-module \(N\) produces a Whittaker module whose annihilator \(J=\mathrm{Ann}_{U(\mathfrak g)}(Q_\chi\otimes N)\) satisfies \(\mathcal V(U(\mathfrak g)/J)=\overline{G\cdot e}\). Every primitive ideal of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) whose associated variety is the closure of the orbit of \(e\) arises this way (Premet’s conjecture, proved by Losev): the fibre of
\[
\{\text{primitive finite-codimension ideals of }U(\mathfrak g,e)\}
\longrightarrow
\{\text{primitive ideals of }U(\mathfrak g)\text{ supported on }\overline{G\cdot e}\}
\]
is a single orbit under the component group \(C(e)=Z_G(e,h,f)/Z_G(e,h,f)^\circ\). That is the classification of finite-dimensional irreducible \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\)-modules, up to that finite group.


One-dimensional representations of \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\) give the Losev–Premet ideals, the best-understood primitives on the orbit closure.


## 4. Category \(\mathcal O\) refinement


Brundan–Goodwin–Kleshchev defined a highest-weight category \(\mathcal O\) for \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\), using a Levi of the good grading as Cartan. They conjectured that, when \(e\) is principal in a Levi, this \(\mathcal O\) is equivalent to a category of generalized Whittaker \(\mathfrak g\)-modules (McDowell, Miličić–Soergel, Backelin). Losev proved the conjecture, and in fact a more general equivalence
\[
\mathcal O\bigl(U(\mathfrak g,e)\bigr)\;\simeq\;\{\text{generalized Whittaker }\mathfrak g\text{-modules of type }e\},
\]
by comparing completions of the two algebras. For principal \(e\) the finite \(W\)-algebra is \(Z(\mathfrak g)\), \(\mathcal O\) is semisimple of dimension one over each central character, and one recovers that Whittaker modules of principal type with a fixed infinitesimal character are multiples of a single Whittaker Verma module.


## 5. Passage to affine \(W\)-algebras


De Sole–Kac identifies \(\operatorname{Zhu}_H W^k(\mathfrak g,e)\) with \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\). Zhu’s correspondence then says that ordinary irreducible \(W^k(\mathfrak g,e)\)-modules are ordinary irreducible \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\)-modules. Skryabin translates the latter into Whittaker \(\mathfrak g\)-modules. The composite dictionary is
\[
\bigl\{\text{ordinary irred. }W^k(\mathfrak g,e)\text{-modules}\bigr\}
\;\longleftrightarrow\;
\bigl\{\text{finite-dim. irred. }U(\mathfrak g,e)\text{-modules}\bigr\}
\;\longleftrightarrow\;
\bigl\{\text{irred. Whittaker }\mathfrak g\text{-modules of type }e
\text{ with finite-dim. Whittaker space}\bigr\}.
\]
For principal \(e\) the Whittaker space is one-dimensional and the parameter is a central character, which is why minimal-series \(W\)-modules are labelled by a reduced set of Weyl orbits.


This dictionary is for *ordinary* positive-energy modules of the *universal* affine \(W\)-algebra. Simple quotients \(W_k(\mathfrak g,e)\) correspond to quotients of \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\) by Losev’s \(I^\dagger\), and logarithmic or non-positive-energy modules are outside the scope of both Zhu and Skryabin.


## 6. Affine Skryabin


Raskin proved an affine analogue, necessarily derived. Let \(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\) be the DG category of modules for the affine Kac–Moody algebra at level \(\kappa\), and let \(\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod})\) be the Whittaker category (operators \(x-\psi(x)\) locally nilpotent on cohomology). There is a canonical t-exact equivalence
\[
\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod})\;\simeq\;W_\kappa\text{-mod}
\]
whose composite with the Drinfeld–Sokolov functor is the forgetful functor to vector spaces. Unlike the finite statement, one cannot stay in underived abelian categories: the loop-group Whittaker model is a derived invariant. Combined with Feigin–Frenkel duality this yields a categorical duality
\[
\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod})\;\simeq\;\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\check{\mathfrak g}}_{\check\kappa}\text{-mod}),
\]
and at critical level an identification with quasi-coherent sheaves on the space of opers. That is Skryabin’s theorem rewritten as a statement in local geometric Langlands.


## 7. Variants


| Setting | Equivalence |
|---|---|
| Finite, char. 0, any good grading | Skryabin / Gan–Ginzburg / Goodwin |
| Finite, category \(\mathcal O\) | Losev, proving BGK |
| Finite, char. \(p\) | Goodwin–Topley, Morita of rank \(p^{d_\chi}\) |
| Finite, Lie superalgebras | Skryabin-type theorems for principal / periplectic / \(q(n)\) |
| Affine, derived | Raskin |
| Quantum groups / \(q\)-\(W\) | equivariant modules \(\simeq\) \(q\)-\(W\)-modules |


In every case the pattern is the same: the reduction algebra is the endomorphism ring of a projective generator of the Whittaker category.


## 8. What it does not say


Skryabin does not classify all \(U(\mathfrak g)\)-modules, only Whittaker ones. It does not describe the abelian category of *all* modules over an affine \(W\)-algebra — only the ordinary ones, via Zhu, or the derived Whittaker category, via Raskin. It does not compute Zhu algebras of lattice-orbifold \(W\)-algebras such as \(\mathcal W(p)\). And it has no content for the 39-screen: there is no nilpotent in a simple Lie algebra, no Whittaker character, and no finite \(W\)-algebra whose modules could be occupation measures of a substitution.
Gan–Ginzburg’s method is Hamiltonian reduction of \(\mathfrak g^*\), first classically and then with \(U(\mathfrak g)\) in place of \(\mathbb C[\mathfrak g^*]\). The output is the Poisson algebra of the Slodowy slice and a filtered quantization of it, which is the finite \(W\)-algebra. The paper is short because, once the slice is written as a reduction, the quantization is the same formulae with enveloping algebras.


## 1. The slice


Let \(e\in\mathfrak g\) be nilpotent, completed to an \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\)-triple \(\{e,h,f\}\). The **Slodowy slice** is the affine space
\[
\mathcal S_e=e+\mathfrak g^f
\]
(equivalently \(\chi+\mathfrak g_e^*\) after identifying \(\mathfrak g\simeq\mathfrak g^*\) by a nondegenerate invariant form, with \(\chi=(e,\,\cdot\,)\)). It is transverse to adjoint orbits: at every point \(x\in\mathcal S_e\),
\[
T_x(G\cdot x)+T_x(\mathcal S_e)=\mathfrak g.
\]
It is strictly transverse to the orbit of \(e\). The \(\mathbb C^\times\)-action \(t\cdot(e+x)=t^2e+t^{2-\mathrm{ad}h}x\) contracts \(\mathcal S_e\) onto \(e\).


Because coadjoint orbits are the symplectic leaves of the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau Poisson structure on \(\mathfrak g^*\), transversality implies that \(\mathcal S_e\) is a Poisson transversal: each intersection \(\mathcal S_e\cap\mathcal O\) is symplectic, and these intersections are the symplectic leaves of a unique Poisson structure on \(\mathcal S_e\). The nilpotent Slodowy slice \(\mathcal S_e\cap\mathcal N\) is a conic symplectic singularity.


## 2. The slice as a Hamiltonian reduction


Let \(\mathfrak m\subset\mathfrak g\) be a nilpotent subalgebra complementary to \(\mathfrak g^e\) in a good grading, with \(\chi|_{\mathfrak m}\) nondegenerate, and let \(M=\exp(\mathfrak m)\). The moment map for the coadjoint \(M\)-action is restriction \(\mu:\mathfrak g^*\to\mathfrak m^*\). Gan–Ginzburg prove
\[
\mathcal S_e\;\cong\;\mu^{-1}(\chi)/M
\]
as Poisson varieties, and more precisely that
\[
\chi+\mathfrak m^\perp\;\cong\;M\times\mathcal S_e
\]
\(M\)-equivariantly, so the scheme-theoretic intersection of the nilpotent cone with \(\chi+\mathfrak m^\perp\) is a reduced complete intersection. In Poisson-algebra language,
\[
\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\;\cong\;\bigl(S(\mathfrak g)/I_\chi\bigr)^{\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak m},
\]
where \(I_\chi\) is the ideal generated by \(x-\chi(x)\) for \(x\in\mathfrak m\). That invariant ring is the **classical finite \(W\)-algebra**. The construction is independent of the choice of isotropic (Lagrangian) subspace used to present \(\mathfrak m\), up to canonical isomorphism.


This is ordinary Marsden–Weinstein reduction of \(\mathfrak g^*\) at the value \(\chi\), not a new Poisson structure invented for the slice. The slice is what you get when you cut \(\mathfrak g^*\) by the Whittaker moment map and quotient by the unipotent group.


## 3. Quantization: replace \(S(\mathfrak g)\) by \(U(\mathfrak g)\)


The same formulae with the enveloping algebra in place of the symmetric algebra produce an associative algebra. Let
\[
Q_\chi=U(\mathfrak g)/U(\mathfrak g)\mathfrak m_\chi,\qquad
\mathfrak m_\chi=\{x-\chi(x):x\in\mathfrak m\}.
\]
The **finite \(W\)-algebra** in the Gan–Ginzburg presentation is
\[
U(\mathfrak g,e)=\bigl(U(\mathfrak g)/U(\mathfrak g)\mathfrak m_\chi\bigr)^{\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak m}
=\mathrm{End}_{U(\mathfrak g)}(Q_\chi)^{\mathrm{op}}.
\]
This is Premet’s algebra, written as a quantum Hamiltonian reduction. To see that it quantizes the slice one uses the **Kazhdan filtration**: combine the PBW filtration of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) with the \(\mathrm{ad}\,h\)-grading by
\[
K_iU(\mathfrak g)=\sum_{k+2j\le i}U(\mathfrak g)_k^{(j)},
\]
so that \(e\) has degree \(2\). The filtration descends to \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\), is positive, and
\[
\mathrm{gr}^K U(\mathfrak g,e)\;\cong\;\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]
\]
as Poisson algebras. That is the definition of a filtered quantization of the slice.


Gan–Ginzburg’s contribution relative to Premet is that this comparison is a five-page argument in characteristic zero, using the geometric isomorphism \(\chi+\mathfrak m^\perp\cong M\times\mathcal S_e\) and a filtered version of the same reduction, rather than reduction to positive characteristic. They also prove independence of the Lagrangian and, in the same paper, Skryabin’s equivalence: \(Q_\chi\) is a projective generator of the Whittaker category with endomorphism ring \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\).


## 4. Why the method works


Hamiltonian reduction and quantization commute for this pair \((G,M)\) because:


- \(M\) is unipotent, so there are no issues of GIT versus stacky reduction;
- the moment map is flat (complete intersection);
- the Kazhdan filtration is \(M\)-stable and the associated graded of the quantum moment map is the classical moment map;
- the vanishing of higher Hamiltonian cohomology that identifies invariants with the reduction survives at the associated-graded level.


The same formal pattern — take a Poisson transversal that is a unipotent reduction, replace \(S(\mathfrak g)\) by \(U(\mathfrak g)\), keep the Kazhdan filtration — produces a quantization whenever those four points hold. Slodowy slices are the case that arises from nilpotent orbits in simple Lie algebras.


## 5. What the quantization does not uniquely determine


Losev and Namikawa showed that a conic symplectic singularity has a universal filtered quantization and a universal Poisson deformation. The finite \(W\)-algebra is *a* filtered quantization of \(\mathcal S_e\cap\mathcal N\), and is the universal one in many cases (Lehn–Namikawa–Sorger, Topley–et al.), but not always. Subregular slices in non-simply-laced types are the interesting exceptions: the \(W\)-algebra is universal among *equivariant* quantizations with respect to Dynkin automorphisms coming from unfolding, which is a noncommutative analogue of Slodowy’s theorem on simultaneous resolutions. So “Gan–Ginzburg quantization” means “this particular quantization, obtained by reducing \(U(\mathfrak g)\)”, not “the unique quantization of the slice”.


## 6. Bimodules and later uses of the same picture


Once \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\) is a quantization of \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\), Harish-Chandra \(U(\mathfrak g)\)-bimodules can be reduced to Harish-Chandra \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\)-bimodules (Ginzburg). Translation functors on the \(W\)-side become noncommutative resolutions of the slice. Losev’s maps \(I\mapsto I^\dagger\) between ideals of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) and of \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\) are the ideal-theoretic shadow of the same reduction. Reduction by stages for two nested nilpotents is the statement that the Gan–Ginzburg construction can be performed in two steps, matching a pair of finite \(W\)-algebras.


## 7. Affine and classical counterparts


The classical affine \(W\)-algebra is the same reduction performed on the Poisson vertex algebra of currents, i.e. Hamiltonian reduction of \(\mathfrak g((z))^*\) at a residue character. De Sole–Kac–Valeri write the Poisson vertex brackets explicitly and identify the Zhu Poisson algebra of that object with \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\). The quantum affine \(W\)-algebra is BRST reduction of \(V^k(\mathfrak g)\); De Sole–Kac identify its \(H\)-twisted Zhu algebra with \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\). So the Gan–Ginzburg picture is the finite, associative, characteristic-zero corner of a square whose other three corners are classical finite, classical affine, and quantum affine \(W\)-algebras, all obtained by reducing the same pair \((\mathfrak g,e)\) in the appropriate category.


## 8. Dictionary


| Classical | Quantum |
|---|---|
| \(\mathbb C[\mathfrak g^*]\), KKS Poisson | \(U(\mathfrak g)\), PBW |
| moment map \(\mu:\mathfrak g^*\to\mathfrak m^*\) | quantum moment map \(U(\mathfrak m)\to U(\mathfrak g)\) |
| \(\mu^{-1}(\chi)/M\) | \((U(\mathfrak g)/U(\mathfrak g)\mathfrak m_\chi)^{\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak m}\) |
| \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\) | \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\) with Kazhdan filtration |
| symplectic leaves \(\mathcal S_e\cap\mathcal O\) | primitive ideals supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) |
| functions on Whittaker vectors | \(\mathrm{Wh}(E)\) as \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\)-module |


The method is this dictionary, applied once. Everything that later sits on the finite \(W\)-algebra — Skryabin, Losev’s primitive-ideal fibres, the Zhu algebra of an affine \(W\)-algebra — is representation theory of the algebra the dictionary produces, not a different quantization procedure.
Losev’s theory is a dictionary between two-sided ideals of a finite \(W\)-algebra and two-sided ideals of \(U(\mathfrak g)\). Primitive ideals of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) are thereby organised by nilpotent orbits, and finite-dimensional irreducible \(W\)-modules are organised by component-group orbits on those primitives.


## 1. The two maps


Let \(W=U(\mathfrak g,e)\) and \(U=U(\mathfrak g)\). Losev constructed a pair of maps on two-sided ideals
\[
\begin{align*}
\bullet^\dagger&:\operatorname{Id}(W)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Id}(U),\\
\bullet_\dagger&:\operatorname{Id}(U)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Id}(W),
\end{align*}
\]
compatible with Skryabin’s bimodule \(Q_\chi\). The ascending map is
\[
I^\dagger=\operatorname{Ann}_U\bigl(Q_\chi\otimes_W(W/I)\bigr),
\]
the annihilator of the Whittaker module attached to \(W/I\). The descending map sends \(J\) to the kernel of the action of \(W\) on the Whittaker vectors of a faithful module with annihilator \(J\), or equivalently to the reduction of \(J\) along the quantum moment map.


Basic properties: \(I^\dagger\) is primitive if \(I\) is; if \(I\) has finite codimension then
\[
\mathcal V(U/I^\dagger)=\overline{\mathcal O},\qquad\mathcal O=G\cdot e.
\]
For any \(W\)-module \(N\),
\[
\operatorname{Ann}_W(N)^\dagger=\operatorname{Ann}_U\bigl(Q_\chi\otimes_W N\bigr).
\]
The two maps are not inverse on all ideals, but they become so on the finite-codimension / orbit-supported locus after passing to \(C(e)\)-invariants, as below.


## 2. Associated varieties


The associated variety of a primitive ideal \(J\subset U\) is a nilpotent orbit closure \(\overline{\mathcal O}_J\subset\mathfrak g^*\). Duflo’s theorem parametrizes primitives by annihilators of simple highest-weight modules; Joseph’s work describes which orbits arise. The finite \(W\)-algebra of \(e\) is responsible for the primitives with \(\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O}\). Ginzburg showed that associated varieties of primitives in \(W\) itself are irreducible; in type \(A\) the intersections \(\mathcal S_e\cap\mathcal O'\) that appear are irreducible as well.


Thus the set
\[
\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)=\{J\in\operatorname{Pr}(U):\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O}\}
\]
is the natural target of \(\bullet^\dagger\) restricted to finite-codimension primitives of \(W\).


## 3. The component group


Let \(Q=Z_G(e,h,f)\) and \(C(e)=Q/Q^\circ=A(e)\), the component group of the centralizer. The group \(Q\) acts on \(W\) by filtered automorphisms (changing the Lagrangian presentation of the reduction, or equivalently acting on the slice). The identity component acts trivially on the set of finite-codimension ideals, so \(C(e)\) acts on \(\operatorname{Id}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\) and on \(\operatorname{Irr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\). Modules in the same \(C(e)\)-orbit are indistinguishable as \(W\)-modules up to outer automorphism: they become isomorphic after twisting by an automorphism of \(W\).


## 4. Premet’s conjecture, as Losev proved it


Premet conjectured that every primitive \(J\) with \(\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O}\) is \(I^\dagger\) for some finite-codimension primitive \(I\subset W\), and asked for uniqueness. Losev proved the sharpened form:


- \(I\in\operatorname{Id}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\) equals \(J_\dagger\) for some \(J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)\) if and only if \(I\) is \(C(e)\)-invariant, in which case \(I=(I^\dagger)_\dagger\).
- For each \(J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)\), the fibre
  \[
  \{I\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W):I^\dagger=J\}
  \]
  is a single \(C(e)\)-orbit.


Consequently
\[
\operatorname{Irr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\big/C(e)\;\simeq\;\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U).
\]
That is an almost complete classification of finite-dimensional irreducible \(W\)-modules: they are parametrized by primitives supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\), up to the finite group \(C(e)\). Existence of at least one finite-dimensional irreducible (equivalently, of a one-dimensional representation after passing to a suitable cover or to classical types) was part of the same circle; Losev established existence for classical \(\mathfrak g\) already in the 2007 quantized-Hamiltonian-actions paper.


The proof uses Harish-Chandra \(U\)-bimodules and their reductions to \(W\)-bimodules, not a direct attack on generators of \(W\). A HC bimodule \(\mathcal B\) reduces to a \(W\)-bimodule \(\mathcal B_\dagger\); taking annihilators recovers \(\bullet^\dagger\). Completeness of the fibres is a statement about the action of \(C(e)\) on the reduced bimodules.


## 5. Identifying the orbits: Losev–Ostrik


The remaining datum is the stabilizer of the \(C(e)\)-orbit over a given \(J\). For integral central character, Losev–Ostrik compute it. If \(\sigma\) is the left cell attached to \(J\) (in the two-sided cell corresponding to \(\mathcal O\)), the stabilizer is Lusztig’s subgroup \(H_\sigma\subset A(e)\), and the orbit is \(\overline A/H_\sigma\). The ingredients are Joseph cells, Springer representations, and a monoidal structure on a category of HC bimodules. So for integral infinitesimal character one has a complete parametrization
\[
\operatorname{Irr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)_\lambda\;\simeq\;\bigsqcup_{J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)_\lambda} A(e)/H_{\sigma(J)}.
\]
Non-integral characters are not given by the same formula; the component-group action can be smaller or larger.


## 6. Type \(A\)


In type \(A\) the component group of a nilpotent is trivial (\(A(e)=1\)). Hence \(\bullet^\dagger:\operatorname{Pr}(W)\to\operatorname{Pr}(U)\) is injective, and in fact a bijection onto \(\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)\). Combined with Joseph’s classification of primitives in \(U(\mathfrak{sl}_n)\), this classifies all primitive ideals of \(W\) in type \(A\), not only the finite-codimension ones. Surjectivity onto the orbit-supported primitives holds more generally; injectivity is the type-\(A\) accident.


## 7. One-dimensional representations and Losev–Premet ideals


A one-dimensional representation of \(W\) is a character \(W\to\mathbb C\). Its annihilator is a maximal ideal of codimension one; the image under \(\bullet^\dagger\) is a primitive ideal of \(U\) supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\), called a **Losev–Premet ideal**. These are the primitives that arise from quantizing the structure sheaf of the slice rather than a higher-rank bundle. Their classification is a separate, still active problem (Topley and others): it amounts to describing the scheme of one-dimensional representations of \(W\), i.e. the spectrum of the abelianization \(W/[W,W]\), which is a finite scheme supported at points of the Slodowy slice corresponding to compatible central characters.


For principal \(e\), \(W\cong Z(\mathfrak g)\) and every irreducible is one-dimensional, labelled by central characters; the Losev–Premet ideals are exactly the maximal ideals of \(Z(\mathfrak g)\) pulled back to \(U(\mathfrak g)\), i.e. the usual infinitesimal characters.


## 8. How this sits on the earlier theorems


Skryabin identifies \(W\text{-Mod}\) with Whittaker \(\mathfrak g\)-modules. Losev’s maps are the ideal-theoretic shadow of that identification: annihilators travel with the equivalence. Gan–Ginzburg supplies the filtration that makes associated varieties of those annihilators into subvarieties of the slice. De Sole–Kac identifies \(W\) with \(\operatorname{Zhu}_H W^k(\mathfrak g,e)\), so finite-dimensional irreducible \(W\)-modules are precisely the top spaces of ordinary irreducible modules for the *universal* affine \(W\)-algebra. After quotienting to the simple affine \(W\)-algebra one keeps only those \(I\) that contain the image of the maximal vertex ideal; at minimal-series levels that leaves a finite semisimple algebra, and Losev’s fibres collapse to one point per admissible central character.


The theory does not classify logarithmic modules of affine \(W\)-algebras, primitives of lattice-orbifold algebras such as \(\mathcal W(p)\), or modules that are infinite-dimensional over \(W\). It is a theorem about finite-dimensional representation theory of a filtered quantization of a Slodowy slice, and about how that theory embeds into the primitive spectrum of \(U(\mathfrak g)\).
Losev–Ostrik identify the \(C(e)\)-orbit of finite-dimensional irreducible \(W\)-modules over a given primitive ideal with a Lusztig homogeneous space \(\overline A/H_\sigma\). The group and the subgroup are read off from the two-sided cell of the orbit and the left cell of the primitive, not from a presentation of the \(W\)-algebra.


## 1. Cells that label primitives


Fix an integral infinitesimal character \(\lambda\) and write \(W\) for the Weyl group (of the integral Weyl group if one prefers that language). Joseph’s Goldie-rank polynomials partition the primitives \(\operatorname{Pr}_\lambda(U)\) into **left cells** and **two-sided cells**:


- two-sided cells of \(W\) are in bijection with Lusztig families in \(\operatorname{Irr}(W)\), and, via Springer, with special nilpotent orbits;
- left cells inside a two-sided cell \(\mathbf c\) label the primitives supported on the corresponding special orbit \(\mathcal O\), at that infinitesimal character;
- the number of left cells in \(\mathbf c\) equals \(\dim\sigma_0(\mathbf c)\), the dimension of the special (Goldie-rank) representation of the family.


If \(J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)_\lambda\), write \(\sigma=\sigma(J)\) for its left cell and \(\mathbf c\) for the two-sided cell of \(\mathcal O\). That is the combinatorial input. The associated variety of every primitive in \(\mathbf c\) is \(\overline{\mathcal O}\); different left cells in the same \(\mathbf c\) are different primitives on the same orbit closure.


## 2. Lusztig’s quotient and the subgroups \(H_\sigma\)


Springer cohomology \(\operatorname{Spr}(\mathcal O)=H^{\mathrm{top}}(\mathcal B_e)\) is a \(W\times A(e)\)-module. Restrict to the isotypic component for the family of \(\mathbf c\). Lusztig’s **canonical quotient** \(\overline A=\overline A_{\mathbf c}\) is the smallest quotient of \(A(e)\) that acts faithfully on
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_W\bigl([\mathbf c],\operatorname{Spr}(\mathcal O)\bigr).
\]
Equivalently, \(\overline A\) is \(A(e)\) modulo the kernel of its action on \(\operatorname{Spr}(\mathcal O)_{\mathbf c}\). In classical types \(\overline A\) is a quotient of a product of copies of \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\) read from the partition of \(\mathcal O\); it equals \(A(e)\) when all relevant parts have even length in the usual numbering.


To each left cell \(\sigma\subset\mathbf c\) Lusztig assigns a subgroup \(H_\sigma\subset\overline A\), well-defined up to conjugacy, characterized by
\[
\mathbb Q(\overline A/H_\sigma)\;\cong\;\operatorname{Hom}_W\bigl([\sigma],\operatorname{Spr}(\mathcal O)\bigr)
\]
as \(\overline A\)-modules. Equivalently, in Lusztig’s parametrization of \(\operatorname{Irr}(W)_{\mathbf c}\) by pairs \((x_U,V_U)\),
\[
\operatorname{Spr}(\mathcal O)_{\mathbf c}\;=\;\bigoplus_{\substack{U\in\operatorname{Irr}(W)_{\mathbf c}\\x_U=1}}U\otimes V_U,
\]
and \(H_\sigma\) is the stabilizer that cuts out the constructible module \([\sigma]\). In types \(B\) and \(C\) the subgroups are given by Temperley–Lieb patterns on the symbols of the cell: one connects paired parts and takes the \(\mathbb F_2\)-span of the resulting circles. The number of simple objects in a double cell \(\sigma_1 J\sigma_2\) equals the number of \(\overline A\)-orbits on \(\overline A/H_{\sigma_1}\times\overline A/H_{\sigma_2}\).


## 3. The theorem


**Theorem (Losev–Ostrik).** Let \(J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)\) have integral central character, and let \(\sigma\) be the corresponding left cell. The \(A(e)\)-orbit of finite-dimensional irreducible \(W\)-modules with \(I^\dagger=J\) is isomorphic to
\[
\overline A\big/H_\sigma
\]
as an \(A(e)\)-set (the action factors through \(\overline A\)). In particular the stabilizer of any point of the orbit is a conjugate of \(H_\sigma\).


Thus
\[
\operatorname{Irr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)_\lambda
\;\simeq\;
\bigsqcup_{J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)_\lambda}
\overline A\big/H_{\sigma(J)}.
\]
When \(H_\sigma=\overline A\) the fibre is a single module. When \(H_\sigma=1\) the fibre is a free \(\overline A\)-orbit of size \(|\overline A|\). Proposition 6.9 of their paper produces, inside a non-exceptional cell, left cells realizing \(H=\overline A\), \(H=1\), and all codimension-one subgroups; so all of these orbit types occur.


Exceptional two-sided cells — three cells in types \(E_7\) and \(E_8\) — need a separate check that \(\overline A\) as defined from Springer still coincides with Lusztig’s quotient and that \(H^\lambda_\sigma=H_\sigma\). For non-exceptional cells this identification is part of the theorem.


## 4. Type \(A\), and why the group often vanishes


In type \(A\), \(A(e)=1\) for every nilpotent, so \(\overline A=1\) and every fibre is a singleton. That is why \(\bullet^\dagger\) is injective on primitives in type \(A\): there is nothing for the component group to permute. In types \(B,C,D\) the group \(\overline A\) is a \(2\)-group whose rank is read from the partition, and the Temperley–Lieb recipe for \(H_\sigma\) is explicit. For principal \(e\), \(A(e)=1\) in every type, \(W\cong Z(\mathfrak g)\), and each fibre is a single character, matching the one-dimensionality of irreducibles of the centre.


## 5. Why cells appear at all


The argument is not a computation inside \(W\). It compares three monoidal categories that act on the same set of objects.


- Harish-Chandra \(U\)-bimodules with integral infinitesimal character, reduced to \(W\)-bimodules, form a multi-fusion category whose simple objects are indexed by primitives on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\).
- Lusztig’s asymptotic Hecke algebra of the two-sided cell \(\mathbf c\), categorified, is a multi-fusion category whose simples are the left cells of \(\mathbf c\), with fusion controlled by \(\overline A\) and the subgroups \(H_\sigma\).
- Springer theory supplies a \(W\times\overline A\)-module that identifies the two Grothendieck rings.


Once the reduced HC category is identified with Lusztig’s cell category, the \(A(e)\)-orbit of irreducibles over \(J\) is forced to be the \(\overline A\)-set attached to \(\sigma(J)\). The embedding
\[
\mathbb Q(Y_\sigma)\hookrightarrow\overline A/H_\sigma
\]
coming from the Springer fibre is the concrete comparison of the two actions.


## 6. What the parametrization does not say


- It requires integral central character. For non-integral \(\lambda\) the component-group action on \(\operatorname{Irr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)_\lambda\) is not identified with a Lusztig homogeneous space by this theorem.
- It classifies *finite-dimensional* irreducibles of the *finite* \(W\)-algebra, not all primitives of \(W\) (except in type \(A\)) and not logarithmic or infinite-dimensional modules.
- It does not compute characters or explicit matrices; it computes the set of isomorphism classes as a \(C(e)\)-set.
- Exceptional cells in \(E_7,E_8\) are excluded from the clean identification \(H^\lambda_\sigma=H_\sigma\) in the main argument and must be treated separately.
- Affine \(W\)-algebras inherit the parametrization only on ordinary top spaces of the universal algebra, via De Sole–Kac. After passing to a simple affine quotient at a non-minimal-series level, some of these modules disappear.


The content of the theorem is therefore a matching of two already-existing combinatorial theories — Joseph cells of primitives and Lusztig cells of the Weyl group — through the Springer representation of the orbit that supports those primitives. The finite \(W\)-algebra is the object whose finite-dimensional representation theory realises that matching.
Joseph’s Goldie-rank polynomials record how the size of a primitive quotient \(U(\mathfrak g)/J\) varies with infinitesimal character. They are the numerical shadow of left cells, and the \(W\)-module they span is Lusztig’s special representation of the corresponding two-sided cell.


## 1. Goldie rank of a primitive quotient


Let \(J\subset U(\mathfrak g)\) be primitive. The quotient \(U(\mathfrak g)/J\) is a prime Noetherian ring. Its **Goldie rank** \(\operatorname{Grk}(U/J)\) is the number of simple factors in a maximal direct sum of uniform left ideals of \(U/J\), equivalently the matrix size of the simple artinian ring of fractions of \(U/J\). For \(J=\operatorname{Ann} L(\mu)\) it measures how large the “endomorphism ring at infinity” of \(L(\mu)\) is.


Duflo’s map \(w\mapsto J(w\lambda)\) is surjective onto \(\operatorname{Pr}_\lambda(U)\) for regular \(\lambda\). Goldie rank is therefore a function of a Weyl-group element and a weight.


## 2. The polynomials


Joseph’s discovery: after translating \(J\) through a Zariski-dense set of compatible infinitesimal characters (the Jantzen–Barbasch–Jantzen translation principle), the function
\[
\mu\;\longmapsto\;\operatorname{Grk}\bigl(U(\mathfrak g)/J_\mu\bigr)
\]
extends to a polynomial on \(\mathfrak h^*\). For each \(w\in W\) there is a unique \(p_w\in\mathbb C[\mathfrak h^*]\) such that
\[
\operatorname{Grk}\bigl(U/J(w\delta)\bigr)=p_w(\delta)
\]
for all anti-dominant regular \(\delta\) compatible with \(w\). These are the **Goldie-rank polynomials**. They depend only on the **left cell** of \(w\): if \(w\sim_L w'\) then \(J(w\delta)=J(w'\delta)\) for regular anti-dominant \(\delta\), so \(p_w=p_{w'}\). One therefore writes \(p_\sigma\) for a left cell \(\sigma\). On a Duflo involution \(d_\sigma\) of the cell one has \(\operatorname{Grk}(U/J(d_\sigma\lambda))=p_\sigma(\lambda)\) for dominant compatible \(\lambda\).


A companion family \(q_w\) interpolates Goldie ranks of all \(J(w\lambda)\), not only those of Duflo involutions. The ratio
\[
z_w=\frac{q_w}{p_w}
\]
is a positive integer, **Joseph’s scale factor**, independent of \(\lambda\). It measures the gap between an arbitrary element of the cell and the Duflo involution.


## 3. Formulae


**In terms of Verma multiplicities (Joseph II).** If \(w\) is of minimal length in its left cell and \(\lambda\) is the shape of the Robinson–Schensted tableau \(Q(w)\) (type \(A\)),
\[
p_w=\sum_{z\in D^\lambda}[L(w):M(z)]\,z^{-1}(h_\lambda),
\]
where \(h_\lambda\) is the product of \(\frac{x_i-x_j}{j-i}\) over transpositions in the parabolic \(W^\lambda\), and \(D^\lambda\) is the set of maximal-length coset representatives. In general type the same shape of formula holds with Kazhdan–Lusztig multiplicities in place of the type-\(A\) ones; the unknown constants that remain are the scale factors.


**In type \(A\), explicitly.** If \(Q(w)\) is a column-separated tableau \(A\),
\[
p_w=\prod_{(i,j)}\frac{x_i-x_j}{d(i,j)},
\]
the product running over pairs of entries with \(i\) strictly above \(j\) in the same column and \(d(i,j)\) the number of rows between them. Every Goldie-rank polynomial in type \(A\) takes the value \(1\) at a specific integral weight attached to a “row-constant” tableau of the same shape. That is Mœglin’s theorem in combinatorial form.


**As lowest-degree harmonics.** The \(W\)-translates of \(p_\sigma\) span an irreducible representation \(\sigma_0(\sigma)\subset S(\mathfrak h^*)\). This representation occurs in degree \(b(\sigma_0)\), strictly lower than any other constituent of the two-sided cell module, and the lowest-degree copy is unique. The representation \(\sigma_0(\mathbf c)\) of a two-sided cell \(\mathbf c\) is Lusztig’s **special representation** of the family, equivalently Joseph’s **Goldie-rank representation**. Every special representation arises this way. Barbasch–Vogan identify the two notions.


## 4. What the polynomials know about cells and orbits


Joseph’s theorems, in one list:


1. \(\operatorname{Ann} L(w)=\operatorname{Ann} L(w')\) if and only if \(w\sim_L w'\).
2. \(\mathcal V(L(w))=\mathcal V(L(w'))\) if \(w\sim_R w'\).
3. The left-cell module \(\mathbb Z\sigma\) contains \(\sigma_0(\sigma)\) with multiplicity one, in lowest degree.
4. \(\sigma_0(\sigma)=\sigma_0(\sigma')\) if and only if \(\sigma,\sigma'\) lie in the same two-sided cell.
5. Two-sided cells \(\leftrightarrow\) special representations \(\sigma_0(\mathbf c)\).
6. Number of left cells in \(\mathbf c\) equals \(\dim\sigma_0(\mathbf c)\).
7. \(|\operatorname{Pr}_\lambda(U)|=\sum_{\mathbf c}\dim\sigma_0(\mathbf c)\).


The Springer representation attached to \(\sigma_0(\mathbf c)\) is the special orbit \(\mathcal O(\mathbf c)\), and \(\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O(\mathbf c)}\) for every primitive \(J\) in the family. Associated varieties are therefore constant on two-sided cells and refine on right cells; Goldie-rank polynomials are constant on left cells and their \(W\)-span sees the whole family.


## 5. Relation to finite \(W\)-modules


Losev’s later work makes the polynomials talk to the finite \(W\)-algebra of \(\mathcal O\). For \(J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)\) and \(V\) a finite-dimensional irreducible \(W\)-module with \(I^\dagger=J\),
\[
\operatorname{Grk}(J)\;\le\;\frac{\dim V}{d_V},
\]
where \(d_V\) is the index of an equivariant Azumaya algebra on a homogeneous space attached to \(V\) (Losev–Panin). For integral infinitesimal character Losev conjectured, and proved in classical types,
\[
\operatorname{Grk}(J)=\dim V
\]
whenever \(V\) is chosen so that a certain one-dimensional \(W\)-module exists (a strengthening of existence of Losev–Premet characters). Under that conjecture the Goldie-rank polynomial *is* the dimension of the corresponding \(W\)-irreducible, as a function of \(\lambda\), and Joseph’s scale factors become ratios of those dimensions. That completes Joseph’s 1980s programme of computing Goldie ranks from Kazhdan–Lusztig data, at integral character and in classical type.


The ratio \(\operatorname{pr}_w(\lambda)=\dim V_w(\lambda)/p_w(\lambda)\) is independent of \(\lambda\) and depends only on the left cell; it is the remaining combinatorial factor once Goldie rank and \(W\)-dimension are known separately.


## 6. What they are not


Goldie-rank polynomials are not characters of Verma modules, not Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials, and not Hilbert polynomials of associated varieties (those are proportional to \(p_w^2\), by a theorem of Joseph recorded in Jantzen). They do not by themselves determine the \(C(e)\)-orbit of Losev–Ostrik: that orbit is \(\overline A/H_\sigma\), a group-theoretic object attached to the same left cell \(\sigma\), while \(p_\sigma\) is a single polynomial spanning the special line in the cell module.


They are polynomials on \(\mathfrak h^*\) whose value at a compatible weight is a positive integer, whose \(W\)-orbit spans a special representation, and whose left-cell constancy is the reason primitive ideals at varying infinitesimal character stay in a single combinatorial family. That is the entire content of the construction.
Losev’s connection is a third construction of the finite \(W\)-algebra — as \(G\)-invariants in a Fedosov quantization of an equivariant Slodowy slice — together with the maps that construction produces between ideals, bimodules, and numerical invariants of \(W\) and of \(U(\mathfrak g)\). It is how the slice becomes a machine for studying primitive ideals, not merely a quantization of \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\).


## 1. The equivariant-slice definition


Start with the ordinary Slodowy slice \(\mathcal S_e=e+\mathfrak g^f\). Form the **equivariant Slodowy slice**
\[
\widetilde{\mathcal S}=G\times^{Q^\circ}\mathcal S_e,
\]
an affine symplectic \(G\)-variety (Hamiltonian, with moment map the natural projection to \(\mathfrak g^*\)). Fedosov quantization produces a star-product algebra \(\mathbb A_\hbar(\widetilde{\mathcal S})\) of which \(G\) acts by automorphisms. Losev’s algebra is the invariant ring
\[
\widetilde W=\mathbb A_\hbar(\widetilde{\mathcal S})^G,
\]
specialised at \(\hbar=1\). This is isomorphic to Premet’s / Gan–Ginzburg’s \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\), but the presentation makes three things formal:


- \(G\) acts on a quantization, so one can form invariants, Hamiltonian reductions, and completions in the language of quantized symplectic actions;
- the Kazhdan \(\mathbb C^\times\) is built into the Fedosov data, so filtrations and associated graded identifications \(\mathrm{gr}\,W\cong\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\) are automatic;
- a reductive group larger than the unipotent \(M\) is present from the start, so the component group \(C(e)=Q/Q^\circ\) acts on \(W\) by filtered automorphisms.


The **decomposition theorem** in this language is a slice theorem for the quantized action: formally completing \(W\) at the maximal ideal corresponding to \(e\) recovers a Weyl algebra times a completion of \(U(\mathfrak g)\), which is why finite-dimensional representation theory of \(W\) can see the formal neighbourhood of the orbit.


## 2. What the definition immediately gives


From the invariant-ring presentation Losev obtained, before the later classification papers:


- a relation between **prime ideals** of \(W\) and of \(U(\mathfrak g)\), the ancestor of the maps \(\bullet^\dagger\), \(\bullet_\dagger\);
- existence of a **one-dimensional representation** of \(W\) when \(\mathfrak g\) is classical (the character needed later to identify Goldie ranks with dimensions);
- **separation of points** of \(W\) by finite-dimensional representations: the map \(W\to\prod_V\mathrm{End}(V)\) over finite-dimensional irreducibles is injective.


Those three facts are not visible from the Whittaker-reduction definition alone. They are theorems about invariants of a quantized Hamiltonian \(G\)-variety.


## 3. The ideal connection


The maps
\[
I\mapsto I^\dagger\subset U(\mathfrak g),\qquad
J\mapsto J_\dagger\subset W
\]
are the reduction and induction of annihilators along the \((U,W)\)-bimodule coming from the quantized slice. Their formal properties — primitives go to primitives, finite-codimension ideals go to ideals supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\), \((I^\dagger)_\dagger=I\) after passing to \(C(e)\)-invariants — are Morita calculus for that bimodule, plus the fact that \(Q^\circ\) acts trivially on finite-codimension ideals.


Premet’s conjecture is the statement that this calculus is bijective on the finite-dimensional locus up to \(C(e)\). Losev proved it by transporting Harish-Chandra \(U\)-bimodules across the same quantized slice: a HC bimodule \(\mathcal B\) reduces to a \(W\)-bimodule \(\mathcal B_\dagger\), and annihilators travel with the reduction. That is the technical heart of the 2011 Duke paper. The component group appears because it is the residual automorphism group of the quantized slice.


## 4. The numerical connection


Once irreducibles of \(W\) are known to fibre over primitives of \(U\) with associated variety \(\overline{\mathcal O}\), two integers are attached to the same \(J\in\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathcal O}(U)\):


- Joseph’s Goldie rank \(\operatorname{Grk}(U/J)\),
- \(\dim V\) for \(V\in\operatorname{Irr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\) with \(I^\dagger=J\).


Losev’s Inventiones theorem: at integral infinitesimal character, and assuming a one-dimensional \(W\)-module with a compatibility condition that holds in classical types,
\[
\operatorname{Grk}(J)=\dim V.
\]
Joseph’s scale factors \(z_w\) become ratios of these dimensions, and the Goldie-rank polynomials become the dimension polynomials of the corresponding \(W\)-irreducibles. That is the completion of Joseph’s 1980s programme, with the finite \(W\)-algebra supplying the missing integer. A parallel Kazhdan–Lusztig-type formula computes characters in the Brundan–Goodwin–Kleshchev category \(\mathcal O\) for \(W\).


Losev–Panin later refined the inequality without the conjecture:
\[
\operatorname{Grk}(J)\le\frac{\dim V}{d_V},
\]
with \(d_V\) the index of an equivariant Azumaya algebra; equality is the vanishing of that index.


## 5. The combinatorial connection


Losev–Ostrik identify the \(C(e)\)-orbit over \(J\) with Lusztig’s homogeneous space \(\overline A/H_{\sigma(J)}\). The input is Joseph’s left cell of \(J\); the output is a set of \(W\)-modules. The proof compares three monoidal categories — reduced HC bimodules, Lusztig’s asymptotic Hecke algebra of the two-sided cell, and Springer theory of \(\mathcal O\) — all of which act on the same set of primitives. The quantized-slice construction is what produces the HC-to-\(W\) functor that makes the comparison possible.


## 6. The affine connection, in one line


De Sole–Kac says \(\operatorname{Zhu}_H W^k(\mathfrak g,e)\cong U(\mathfrak g,e)\). Losev’s theory is therefore the representation theory of the Zhu algebra of the universal affine \(W\)-algebra. Ordinary top spaces of affine \(W\)-modules are finite-dimensional \(W\)-modules; their annihilators in \(U(\mathfrak g)\) are the primitives supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\); their dimensions, in classical type at integral character, are Goldie ranks. Losev does not construct affine \(W\)-algebras; he supplies the finite-dimensional fibre of the Zhu correspondence.


## 7. What is distinctive


Premet defined \(W\) by Whittaker reduction and conjectured the classification. Gan–Ginzburg identified \(W\) with a quantization of \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\) and proved Skryabin. De Sole–Kac identified \(W\) with a Zhu algebra. Losev’s contribution is the *transport*: a quantized \(G\)-variety whose invariants are \(W\), through which ideals, HC bimodules, one-dimensional representations, Goldie ranks, and cell combinatorics of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) can be moved onto \(W\) and back. The connection is that transport, not a new presentation of generators and relations.


The 39-screen still does not appear. There is no equivariant Slodowy slice for a rotation algebra, no Fedosov star product whose \(G\)-invariants recover \(A_\alpha^\infty\), and no primitive spectrum of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) whose Goldie ranks could be occupation numbers of a substitution.
Losev’s bimodule reduction is an exact tensor functor from Harish-Chandra \(U(\mathfrak g)\)-bimodules supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) to finite-dimensional \(Q\)-equivariant \(W\)-bimodules. Annihilators, multiplicities, and the monoidal structure travel with it; that is why primitive ideals and cells of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) become representation theory of \(W\).


## 1. The two categories


A **Harish-Chandra \(U\)-bimodule** is a finitely generated \(U\)-\(U\)-bimodule on which the adjoint \(\mathfrak g\)-action is locally finite. Write \(\mathrm{HC}(U)\) for the category of all such, and \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)\) for the full subcategory of objects whose associated variety (support of \(\mathrm{gr}\,M\) in \(\mathfrak g^*\times\mathfrak g^*\)) lies in the diagonal copy of \(\overline{\mathcal O}\). Tensor product over \(U\) makes \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)\) monoidal; the unit is \(U/J\) for \(J\) the ideal of \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) in the appropriate sense, and simple objects at a fixed infinitesimal character are closely related to primitive quotients supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\).


On the \(W\)-side, a **Harish-Chandra \(W\)-bimodule** is a finitely generated \(W\)-\(W\)-bimodule on which the adjoint action of the image of \(\mathfrak q=\mathrm{Lie}\,Q\) is locally finite. Write \(\mathrm{HC}^Q_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\) for those that are finite-dimensional as vector spaces (equivalently: associated variety a point of the slice) and carry a compatible \(Q\)-action. Finite-dimensional \(W\)-bimodules are automatically HC after passing to a finite cover of \(Q\).


Ginzburg defined HC bimodules over the quantized slice by a characteristic-variety condition (support on the diagonal in \(T^*\mathcal S_e\)) plus a regular-singularities condition, and constructed a Whittaker functor \(\mathrm{HC}(U)\to\mathrm{HC}(W)\). Losev upgrades that functor to a \(Q\)-equivariant pair of adjoint functors with a precise list of compatibilities.


## 2. The functors


Losev constructs
\[
\begin{align*}
\bullet_\dagger&:\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)\longrightarrow\mathrm{HC}^Q_{\mathrm{fin}}(W),\\
\bullet^\dagger&:\mathrm{HC}^Q_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\longrightarrow\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U),
\end{align*}
\]
by reducing (resp. inducing) along the quantized equivariant slice, or equivalently along the Whittaker bimodule \(Q_\chi\) together with a completion at \(e\). On ideals they restrict to the maps already named \(\bullet^\dagger\), \(\bullet_\dagger\): for \(J\in\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal O}(U)\),
\[
(U/J)_\dagger=W/J_\dagger.
\]
The same formulae work for all of \(\mathrm{HC}(U)\), not only the orbit-supported subcategory; restriction to \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}\) is what lands in finite-dimensional \(W\)-bimodules.


## 3. Formal properties


**Theorem (Losev, 2011).** 


1. \(\bullet_\dagger\) is exact and left adjoint to \(\bullet^\dagger\).
2. For \(M\in\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)\), \(\dim M_\dagger=\mathrm{mult}_{\mathcal O}(M)\), the multiplicity of \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) in the associated variety of \(M\). The kernel and cokernel of the unit \(M\to(M_\dagger)^\dagger\) lie in \(\mathrm{HC}_{\partial\mathcal O}(U)\), i.e. are supported on the boundary of the orbit closure.
3. \(\bullet_\dagger\) is a tensor functor: \((M\otimes_U N)_\dagger\cong M_\dagger\otimes_W N_\dagger\).
4. Left and right annihilators are preserved:
   \[
   \mathrm{LAnn}(M)_\dagger=\mathrm{LAnn}(M_\dagger),\qquad
   \mathrm{RAnn}(M)_\dagger=\mathrm{RAnn}(M_\dagger).
   \]
5. \(\bullet_\dagger\) is fully faithful onto a full subcategory of \(\mathrm{HC}^Q_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\) closed under subquotients.


The functor also intertwines \(\mathrm{Tor}\) and \(\mathrm{Ext}\) (the later restriction-functor papers record this in the language of completions at a point of a symplectic singularity). Associated varieties shrink by the rule
\[
\mathcal V(M_\dagger)\times\mathcal L_e^\wedge=\mathcal V(M)^\wedge_e:
\]
reduction cuts the formal neighbourhood of the orbit down to a finite scheme on the slice.


## 4. Why this proves Premet’s conjecture


A finite-dimensional irreducible \(W\)-module \(V\) is a simple object of \(\mathrm{HC}^Q_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\) after inducing the \(Q\)-action. Its annihilator \(I=\mathrm{Ann}_W(V)\) is a finite-codimension primitive. Property (4) sends \(I\) to a primitive \(I^\dagger\subset U\) supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\). Property (2) says that every HC bimodule supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) — in particular every primitive quotient \(U/J\) with \(\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O}\) — reduces to a nonzero finite-dimensional \(W\)-bimodule, so every such \(J\) is \(I^\dagger\) for some finite-codimension \(I\). Property (5) plus the \(Q\)-action identify the fibre with a \(C(e)\)-orbit: two reductions that become isomorphic after an automorphism of \(W\) coming from \(Q\) are the same point of the orbit space.


The monoidal structure is what later feeds Losev–Ostrik. The category \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)_\lambda\) of integral infinitesimal character is a multi-fusion category whose simples are indexed by primitives on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\). Its image under \(\bullet_\dagger\) is a full monoidal subcategory of finite-dimensional \(W\)-bimodules, which at the Grothendieck-ring level is \(\mathrm{Coh}_{\overline A}(Y\times Y)\) for \(Y=\operatorname{Irr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)_\lambda\). Matching that with Lusztig’s cell category produces \(\overline A/H_\sigma\).


## 5. Relation to Ginzburg’s functor


Ginzburg’s Whittaker functor is the same reduction without the \(Q\)-equivariance and without the adjoint pair written on the whole of \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)\). He used it to prove existence — every primitive supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\) comes from some finite-dimensional \(W\)-module — and to construct translation functors on the quantized slice, i.e. noncommutative resolutions. Losev’s contribution is the adjoint, the tensor property, the multiplicity formula \(\dim M_\dagger=\mathrm{mult}_{\mathcal O}(M)\), and the statement that the image is closed under subquotients. Those four points turn existence into a classification up to \(C(e)\).


## 6. What the functor is not


It is not Skryabin’s equivalence. Skryabin identifies *left* \(W\)-modules with Whittaker *left* \(U\)-modules. Bimodule reduction identifies *bimodules* with *bimodules*, and on the \(U\)-side the objects are HC, not Whittaker. A simple HC bimodule \(U/J\) reduces to a finite-dimensional \(W\)-bimodule that may be a matrix algebra over a product of the irreducibles in the \(C(e)\)-orbit of \(J\), not a single irreducible.


It is not exact on all of \(\mathrm{HC}(U)\), only after restricting to \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}\); objects supported on larger orbit closures reduce to objects that need not be finite-dimensional. It does not see logarithmic or non-HC phenomena. And it has no content for a rotation algebra: there is no pair \((U(\mathfrak g),W)\) and no diagonal orbit closure along which a bimodule could be reduced.


The functor is the precise meaning of the sentence “Losev transports Harish-Chandra bimodules across the quantized slice.” Everything in the primitive-ideal and cell stories that moves from \(U(\mathfrak g)\) to \(W\) moves because of the five properties above.
Ginzburg’s Whittaker functor is the first map that sends Harish-Chandra \(U(\mathfrak g)\)-bimodules to Harish-Chandra bimodules over the quantized Slodowy slice. It is a bimodule-level Whittaker reduction, not Skryabin’s equivalence on left modules, and it is the source of Losev’s later adjoint pair.


## 1. The algebras


Write \(U_c=U(\mathfrak g)/(Z-\chi_c)\) for a central character, and \(A_c=U(\mathfrak g,e)_c\) for Premet’s filtered quantization of \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e]\) at the same character (Ginzburg’s notation in the 2009 paper). Both are filtered; associated graded objects are \(\mathbb C[\mathcal N]\) and \(\mathbb C[\mathcal S_e\cap\mathcal N]\) respectively. The Kazhdan \(\mathbb C^\times\) acts on both.


A **weak HC \(A_c\)-bimodule** is a finitely generated \(A_c\)-\(A_c\)-bimodule whose characteristic variety — support of the associated graded in \(T^*\mathcal S_e\simeq\mathcal S_e\times\mathcal S_e\), after identifying via the symplectic form — lies on the diagonal. A **genuine HC \(A_c\)-bimodule** is a weak one that is regular-singular in Ginzburg’s microlocal sense: after lifting to the Springer resolution of the nilpotent slice, the corresponding \(\mathcal D\)-module has regular singularities. Ordinary HC \(U\)-bimodules are the special case \(e=0\). Finite-dimensional \(A_c\)-modules are automatically genuine HC bimodules over themselves.


## 2. The functor


Let \(\mathfrak m\) be the Whittaker nilpotent and \(\chi=(e,\,\cdot\,)\). For an HC \(U\)-bimodule \(M\), form the space of simultaneous Whittaker vectors for the left and right \(\mathfrak m\)-actions,
\[
\mathrm{Wh}(M)=\{m\in M:(x-\chi(x))m=m(x-\chi(x))=0\text{ for all }x\in\mathfrak m\},
\]
or, equivalently, the Hamiltonian reduction
\[
\mathrm{Wh}(M)=\bigl(M\big/M\mathfrak m_\chi+\mathfrak m_\chi M\bigr)^{\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak m}.
\]
This carries commuting left and right \(A_c\)-actions, because \(A_c\) is itself that reduction of \(U\). Ginzburg writes \(\mathrm{Wh}_{\mathfrak m}^{\mathfrak m}\) for the resulting functor
\[
\mathrm{Wh}:\mathrm{HC}(U)\;\longrightarrow\;\{\text{weak HC }A_c\text{-bimodules}\}.
\]
On the unit object one recovers \(\mathrm{Wh}(U)=A_c\). On a primitive quotient \(U/J\) with \(\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O}\) the output is finite-dimensional.


The construction is the same formulae that produce \(A_c\) from \(U\), applied to a bimodule instead of to the algebra. Geometrically it is restriction of a quantized sheaf on \(\mathfrak g^*\) to the slice, followed by taking \(M\)-invariants.


## 3. Properties Ginzburg records


- **Exactness.** \(\mathrm{Wh}\) is exact. The complete-intersection property of \(\chi+\mathfrak m^\perp\) and unipotence of \(M\) kill higher Hamiltonian cohomology on HC bimodules.
- **Faithfulness on orbit-supported quotients.** If \(M\) is supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\), \(\mathrm{Wh}(M)=0\) implies \(M=0\) after killing a submodule supported on \(\partial\mathcal O\). In particular \(\mathrm{Wh}(U/J)\ne0\) whenever \(\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O}\).
- **Monoidal.** \(\mathrm{Wh}(M\otimes_U N)\cong\mathrm{Wh}(M)\otimes_{A_c}\mathrm{Wh}(N)\) on the subcategory of objects supported on \(\overline{\mathcal O}\).
- **Finite-dimensionality.** If the associated variety of \(M\) is contained in \(\overline{\mathcal O}\), then \(\mathrm{Wh}(M)\) is finite-dimensional as a vector space, hence a genuine HC \(A_c\)-bimodule.
- **Adjoint.** \(\mathrm{Wh}\) admits a right adjoint, induction along the same Whittaker bimodule, which produces HC \(U\)-bimodules from HC \(A_c\)-bimodules.


These are the properties Losev later sharpened to an adjoint pair of tensor functors on \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)\) with the multiplicity formula \(\dim M_\dagger=\mathrm{mult}_{\mathcal O}(M)\) and image closed under subquotients. Ginzburg has exactness, monoidality, and non-vanishing; he does not write the multiplicity or the subquotient-closure.


## 4. What he uses it for


**Existence half of Premet’s conjecture.** Apply \(\mathrm{Wh}\) to \(U/J\) for \(J\) primitive with \(\mathcal V(U/J)=\overline{\mathcal O}\). The output is a nonzero finite-dimensional \(A_c\)-bimodule. A simple quotient \(N\) of that bimodule (as a left \(A_c\)-module) is a finite-dimensional irreducible \(A_c\)-module whose induced Whittaker \(U\)-module has annihilator \(J\). That is existence: every such primitive comes from some finite-dimensional \(W\)-module. Uniqueness up to \(C(e)\) is Losev’s.


**Translation functors and noncommutative resolutions.** Because \(\mathrm{Wh}\) is monoidal and exact, translation functors on HC \(U\)-bimodules — tensoring by finite-dimensional \(U\)-modules, or wall-crossing in the Beilinson–Ginzburg sense — descend to exact endofunctors of HC \(A_c\)-bimodules. Those endofunctors are projective functors on the quantized slice. Iterating them produces a noncommutative resolution of \(\mathcal S_e\cap\mathcal N\): a homologically smooth \(A_c\)-algebra whose associated graded is a resolution of the nilpotent slice. This is the slice analogue of Beilinson–Bernstein localisation plus translation, written entirely on the finite-\(W\) side.


**Joseph’s ideal.** For the minimal orbit the functor relates the Joseph ideal of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) to the unique completely prime primitive of the minimal finite \(W\)-algebra, recovering a theorem of Premet by reduction rather than by generators.


## 5. Distinction from neighbouring functors


| Functor | Input | Output | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skryabin | left \(W\)-modules | Whittaker left \(U\)-modules | modules |
| Ginzburg \(\mathrm{Wh}\) | HC \(U\)-bimodules | HC \(A_c\)-bimodules | bimodules |
| Losev \(\bullet_\dagger\) | \(\mathrm{HC}_{\mathcal O}(U)\) | \(\mathrm{HC}^Q_{\mathrm{fin}}(W)\) | bimodules, \(Q\)-equivariant |
| De Sole–Kac Zhu | affine \(W\)-vertex algebra | \(A_c\) as an algebra | algebras |


Skryabin identifies the abelian category of left modules. Ginzburg reduces *adjoint-finite bimodules*, which is the correct input if one wants annihilators and tensor structure. Losev’s functor is Ginzburg’s, upgraded by the \(Q\)-action coming from the equivariant slice and by the formal properties needed to classify fibres. Zhu’s construction produces the algebra \(A_c\) itself, not a functor on its bimodules.


A Kostant–Whittaker reduction of HC bimodules also appears in Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg’s description of the loop-rotation equivariant Satake category; that is the principal-nilpotent case of the same idea, with the Kostant slice in place of a general Slodowy slice.


## 6. What it does not do


Ginzburg’s functor does not compute \(C(e)\)-orbits, does not identify Goldie ranks with dimensions, and does not produce Lusztig’s \(\overline A/H_\sigma\). It does not apply to non-HC bimodules or to logarithmic modules of affine \(W\)-algebras. It is a reduction functor on a category of quantized sheaves supported along an orbit closure, and its content is exactness plus monoidality plus non-vanishing on that support. Losev’s classification is what one obtains once those three facts are supplemented by the component-group action and the cell combinatorics of the source category.
Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg identify the \(G(\mathcal O)\)-equivariant derived category of the affine Grassmannian with \(G^\vee\)-equivariant coherent sheaves on the dual Lie algebra, and the loop-rotation equivariant upgrade with Harish-Chandra bimodules. Global cohomology becomes Kostant–Whittaker reduction — the principal case of Ginzburg’s Whittaker functor.


## 1. Geometric Satake, then derived


Geometric Satake (Lusztig, Ginzburg, Mirković–Vilonen) is an equivalence of tensor abelian categories
\[
\operatorname{Perv}_{G(\mathcal O)}(\operatorname{Gr}_G)\;\simeq\;\operatorname{Rep}(\check G).
\]
Convolution on the affine Grassmannian \(\operatorname{Gr}_G=G((t))/G[[t]]\) matches tensor product of representations of the Langlands dual group. The derived upgrade, following a suggestion of Drinfeld and using older work of Ginzburg, replaces perverse sheaves by the \(G(\mathcal O)\)-equivariant derived category and representations by equivariant coherent sheaves on the Lie algebra:
\[
D_{G(\mathcal O)}(\operatorname{Gr}_G)\;\simeq\;D^G_{\mathrm{perf}}\bigl(\operatorname{Sym}(\check{\mathfrak g})[-2]\bigr),
\]
perfect complexes of \(G^\vee\)-equivariant \(\operatorname{Sym}(\check{\mathfrak g})\)-modules, with \(\operatorname{Sym}(\check{\mathfrak g})\) placed in cohomological degree \(2\). The extra grading records cohomological degree on the sheaf side and the homological grading on \(\operatorname{Gr}\). This is not Tannakian reconstruction; it is a formality-plus-Ginzburg computation of equivariant cohomology of spherical sheaves.


## 2. Global cohomology is restriction to the Kostant slice


Under that equivalence the functor of global \(G(\mathcal O)\)-equivariant cohomology
\[
H^\bullet_{G(\mathcal O)}(\operatorname{Gr},-):D_{G(\mathcal O)}(\operatorname{Gr})\;\longrightarrow\;\operatorname{Mod}\bigl(H^\bullet_{G(\mathcal O)}(\mathrm{pt})\bigr)
\]
corresponds to restriction of coherent sheaves from \(\check{\mathfrak g}\) to the **Kostant slice**
\[
\mathcal S_{\mathrm{Kost}}=\check f+\check{\mathfrak g}^{\check e}\subset\check{\mathfrak g},
\]
the principal Slodowy slice. The identification
\[
H^\bullet_{G(\mathcal O)}(\mathrm{pt})\;\cong\;\mathbb C[\check{\mathfrak t}^*]^W\;\cong\;\mathbb C[\mathcal S_{\mathrm{Kost}}]
\]
is the Chevalley theorem plus Kostant’s cross-section: regular functions on the slice are the invariant polynomials. So the most naive numerical invariant of a spherical sheaf — its equivariant cohomology — is, on the dual side, the restriction of a \(G^\vee\)-equivariant sheaf to the principal slice. That is the geometric meaning of Kostant’s theorem that \(\mathbb C[\mathfrak g]^G\to\mathbb C[\mathcal S_{\mathrm{Kost}}]\) is an isomorphism.


## 3. Loop rotation: sheaves become bimodules


Loop rotation \(\mathbb G_m\circlearrowright\operatorname{Gr}\) (scaling \(t\)) upgrades the left-hand side to
\[
D_{G(\mathcal O)\rtimes\mathbb G_m}(\operatorname{Gr}).
\]
On the dual side the extra \(\mathbb G_m\) is the Kazhdan grading that quantizes \(\operatorname{Sym}(\check{\mathfrak g})\) to \(U(\check{\mathfrak g})\). Equivariant coherent sheaves become **Harish-Chandra \(\check G\)-bimodules**:
\[
D_{G(\mathcal O)\rtimes\mathbb G_m}(\operatorname{Gr})\;\simeq\;D\bigl(\mathrm{HC}(U(\check{\mathfrak g}))\bigr)
\]
in a Koszul-dual, graded sense (Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg, Theorem 1). Turning off loop rotation recovers the coherent-sheaf statement by passing to associated graded. The two actions of \(H^\bullet_{G(\mathcal O)}(\mathrm{pt})\) on loop-rotation equivariant homology agree after forgetting \(\mathbb G_m\), which is the commutativity that lets the centre of \(U(\check{\mathfrak g})\) act from both sides.


## 4. Global cohomology becomes quantum Kostant–Whittaker reduction


With loop rotation present, global cohomology is no longer restriction of a sheaf to a slice. It is the **quantum Kostant–Whittaker reduction** of an HC bimodule:
\[
\kappa(M)=\bigl(M\big/M\,\check{\mathfrak n}_\psi+\check{\mathfrak n}_\psi M\bigr)^{\check N},
\]
the same formulae as Ginzburg’s \(\mathrm{Wh}\) for the *principal* nilpotent of \(\check{\mathfrak g}\). The output is a module over
\[
Z\bigl(U(\check{\mathfrak g})\bigr)\;\cong\;U(\check{\mathfrak g},\check e_{\mathrm{prin}}),
\]
the principal finite \(W\)-algebra, i.e. the centre. Equivariant cohomology of a spherical sheaf is therefore a Whittaker reduction of the HC bimodule that the sheaf becomes after quantization.


Twisting by a finite-dimensional \(\check G\)-representation on the Whittaker category corresponds to convolution with the corresponding spherical sheaf. That is why the global cohomology modules of the Satake sheaves are the bimodules that implement translation on Whittaker modules.


## 5. The Toda lattice


The convolution algebra of loop-rotation equivariant homology of \(\operatorname{Gr}\) is, by an explicit calculation of BF, the tensor square of the centre of \(U(\check{\mathfrak g})\) with a deformation parameter from \(\mathbb G_m\). That algebra is the **quantized completed Toda lattice**. This proves a conjecture of Ginzburg–Kapranov–Vasserot: 
\[
H_\bullet^{G(\mathcal O)\rtimes\mathbb G_m}(\operatorname{Gr})\;\cong\;\text{quantized completed Toda}.
\]
Classically the Toda lattice is the Hamiltonian reduction of \(T^*G\) at a principal character; quantum it is the principal Whittaker reduction of \(U(\mathfrak g)\otimes U(\mathfrak g)^{\mathrm{op}}\). Satake says that homology of the Grassmannian computes the same thing.


## 6. Place in the \(W\)-algebra thread


The principal finite \(W\)-algebra is \(Z(\mathfrak g)\). Ginzburg’s Whittaker functor for general \(e\) reduces HC bimodules to HC bimodules over \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\). Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg is the case \(e\) principal, with an additional geometric source for the HC bimodules: they are the loop-rotation equivariant sheaves on \(\operatorname{Gr}\). The dictionary is


| Satake side | Dual Lie-algebra side |
|---|---|
| \(D_{G(\mathcal O)}(\operatorname{Gr})\) | \(\operatorname{Coh}^{G^\vee}(\check{\mathfrak g})\) |
| global cohomology | restriction to Kostant slice |
| \(D_{G(\mathcal O)\rtimes\mathbb G_m}(\operatorname{Gr})\) | derived HC bimodules for \(\check{\mathfrak g}\) |
| global cohomology with loop rotation | quantum Kostant–Whittaker reduction |
| convolution homology | quantized Toda / \(Z(U(\check{\mathfrak g}))^{\otimes 2}\) |


Raskin’s affine Skryabin is the parallel statement with the affine Grassmannian replaced by the affine flag variety and the principal finite \(W\)-algebra replaced by an affine \(W\)-algebra. Geometric Satake is the spherical, derived, dual-group form of the same reduction.


## 7. What it is not


It is not geometric Satake itself — Satake is the abelian tensor equivalence with \(\operatorname{Rep}(\check G)\). It is not a construction of non-principal finite \(W\)-algebras; the slice that appears is only Kostant’s. It does not classify primitives of \(U(\mathfrak g)\) or finite-dimensional modules of a general \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\). It is a theorem that the most canonical functor on the Satake category — take cohomology — is, after Langlands duality and quantization, the principal Whittaker reduction of an HC bimodule. That is the precise sense in which “Satake sees the principal \(W\)-algebra”.
Affine Skryabin is Raskin’s theorem that the derived category of modules over an affine \(W\)-algebra is the Whittaker category of affine Kac–Moody modules. Unlike the finite statement, the equivalence is derived: the abelian categories do not match, and the proof is geometric.


## 1. The two sides


Let \(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\) be the affine Kac–Moody algebra at level \(\kappa\), and let \(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\) be the DG category of discrete modules (or, equivalently, of \(\mathcal D\)-modules on the loop group with a suitable monodromy). The **Whittaker category**
\[
\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\bigr)
\]
is the category of Whittaker coinvariants (or, equivalently after Beraldo-type identification, invariants) for \(N(K)=N((t))\) with a nondegenerate character \(\psi\). Concretely, objects are complexes on which \(x-\psi(x)\) acts locally nilpotently on cohomology for every \(x\in\mathfrak n((t))\).


The **affine \(W\)-algebra** \(W_\kappa=W^\kappa(\mathfrak g)\) is the quantum Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction of the vacuum module \(V^\kappa(\mathfrak g)\), a topological associative algebra quantizing functions on the loop space of the Kostant slice. Its module category \(W_\kappa\text{-mod}\) is not the naïve discrete modules of a topological algebra: Raskin defines it as the ind-completion of the subcategory of the bounded-below derived category generated by a projective system of **generalized vacuum modules** \(W^n_\kappa\), the affine analogues of \(\mathrm{ind}^{U(\mathfrak g)}_{U(\mathfrak m_\chi)}\mathbb C_\chi\). Those \(W^n_\kappa\) are themselves Drinfeld–Sokolov reductions of induced modules from compact open subgroups \(I_n\subset G((t))\).


## 2. The theorem


**Theorem (Raskin, Affine Skryabin).** There is a canonical equivalence of DG categories
\[
\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\bigr)\;\simeq\;W_\kappa\text{-mod}
\]
such that the composition
\[
\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\bigr)\;\simeq\;W_\kappa\text{-mod}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathrm{Vect}
\]
is the Drinfeld–Sokolov functor \(\Psi\). The first arrow is the canonical map from a category to its Whittaker coinvariants.


The equivalence is t-exact once \(\mathrm{Whit}\) is given the t-structure Raskin constructs from the adolescent Whittaker categories \(\mathrm{Whit}^{\le n}\) (invariants for an increasing sequence of compact open subgroups approximating \(N(K)\)). That t-structure is the reason one can speak of an abelian heart at all; without it the Whittaker category is only derived.


## 3. Why derived categories are essential


Finite Skryabin is an equivalence of abelian categories because \(Q_\chi\) is a projective generator of the Whittaker category and \(\mathrm{End}(Q_\chi)^{\mathrm{op}}=U(\mathfrak g,e)\). In the affine setting:


- \(N(K)\) is not unipotent in the finite-dimensional sense; Whittaker invariants and coinvariants differ by a dualizing shift and must be identified by a theorem (Beraldo for \(\mathrm{GL}_n\), Raskin in general);
- there is no single projective generator; one needs a whole system \(W^n_\kappa\);
- the Drinfeld–Sokolov functor \(\Psi\) is not exact on the abelian category of all \(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\)-modules (Raskin’s theorem clarifies exactly how inexact it is);
- the correct module category of \(W_\kappa\) is already an ind-completion of a derived category.


The finite statement is the associated-graded, compact-open-of-level-zero shadow of the affine one.


## 4. Geometric input


The proof does not compute modes of \(W\)-fields. It uses \(\mathcal D\)-modules on the loop group and the affine Grassmannian.


- Whittaker models are rewritten as invariants for a family of compact open subgroups of \(G((t))\), a geometric form of Rodier’s compact approximation. This produces an infinite family of affine Bezrukavnikov–Braverman–Mirković equivalences.
- The generalized vacua \(W^n_\kappa\) are \(\Psi\) applied to modules induced from those compact opens. A spectral sequence whose \(E_1\) is a structure sheaf on a finite-level slice \(f+\mathfrak b[[t]]/N(\mathcal O)\) shows they are concentrated in a single cohomological degree and generate.
- The t-structure on \(\mathrm{Whit}\) is transported from the t-structures on the compact-open invariant categories.


That is why affine Skryabin is a theorem in local geometric Langlands rather than a calculation with vertex operators.


## 5. Categorical Feigin–Frenkel


Ordinary Feigin–Frenkel duality is an isomorphism of topological algebras \(W_\kappa(\mathfrak g)\cong W_{\check\kappa}(\check{\mathfrak g})\). Combined with affine Skryabin it becomes an equivalence of Whittaker categories
\[
\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\bigr)\;\simeq\;\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\check{\mathfrak g}}_{\check\kappa}\text{-mod}\bigr).
\]
At critical level the right-hand side (or the left) is quasi-coherent sheaves on the indscheme of opers for the dual group on the punctured disc:
\[
\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}}\text{-mod}\bigr)\;\simeq\;\mathrm{QCoh}\bigl(\mathrm{Op}_{\check G}(\mathring D)\bigr).
\]
The adolescent filtration \(\mathrm{Whit}^{\le n}\) matches opers with poles of order at most \(n\). This is the categorical form of the statement that the centre of the critical-level Kac–Moody algebra is functions on opers.


## 6. Place among the neighbouring equivalences


| Statement | Categories | Derived? |
|---|---|---|
| Finite Skryabin | Whittaker \(\mathfrak g\)-modules \(\simeq\) \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\)-modules | no |
| Ginzburg \(\mathrm{Wh}\) | HC \(U\)-bimodules \(\to\) HC \(W\)-bimodules | no |
| Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg | \(D_{G(\mathcal O)\rtimes\mathbb G_m}(\mathrm{Gr})\simeq D(\mathrm{HC}(U(\check{\mathfrak g})))\) | yes |
| Affine Skryabin | \(\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod})\simeq W_\kappa\text{-mod}\) | yes |
| Categorical FF | \(\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa)\simeq\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\check{\mathfrak g}}_{\check\kappa})\) | yes |


Finite Skryabin is the reduction of a finite-dimensional unipotent; affine Skryabin is the reduction of a loop-group unipotent. Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg is the spherical, dual-group, principal-slice form of the same picture, with global cohomology in place of \(\Psi\). Raskin supplies the non-spherical, same-group, affine-\(W\) form.


De Sole–Kac remains the statement that the Zhu algebra of the *vertex* affine \(W\)-algebra is the finite \(W\)-algebra. Affine Skryabin is a statement about *modules* for the topological associative algebra underlying that vertex algebra, and it sees many more modules than Zhu’s ordinary positive-energy ones — in particular the generalized vacua \(W^n_\kappa\) and, at critical level, all quasi-coherent sheaves on opers.


## 7. What it does not do


It does not classify ordinary modules of the vertex \(W\)-algebra in the sense of Zhu; those are still finite-dimensional modules of \(U(\mathfrak g,e)\). It does not produce a projective generator whose endomorphism ring is \(W_\kappa\). It does not, by itself, compute characters or associated varieties of simple \(W_\kappa\)-modules (Arakawa’s theorems remain the source of those). It is an identification of DG categories that lets geometric methods — \(\mathcal D\)-modules on loop groups, opers, compact open approximations — act on affine \(W\)-modules, and that is its content.
Categorical Feigin–Frenkel is Raskin’s upgrade of the Feigin–Frenkel isomorphism of principal affine \(W\)-algebras to an equivalence of Whittaker categories of affine Kac–Moody modules. At critical level it becomes an identification of the Whittaker category with quasi-coherent sheaves on opers for the dual group.


## 1. The algebra-level theorem


Feigin–Frenkel duality is an isomorphism of vertex algebras
\[
W^k(\mathfrak g)\;\cong\;W^\ell(\check{\mathfrak g})
\]
for the principal nilpotents of a simple Lie algebra and its Langlands dual, with levels related by
\[
r\,(k+h^\vee)\,(\ell+\check h^\vee)=1,
\]
where \(r\) is the lacing number. The same formula with topological associative algebras in place of vertex algebras is the isomorphism of the underlying affine \(W\)-algebras. When \(k\to k_{\mathrm{crit}}=-h^\vee\), the dual level \(\ell\to\infty\), the algebra \(W^{k_{\mathrm{crit}}}(\mathfrak g)\) becomes commutative, and the isomorphism specialises to
\[
Z\bigl(U(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}})\bigr)\;\cong\;\mathbb C\bigl[\mathrm{Op}_{\check G}(\mathring D)\bigr],
\]
the Feigin–Frenkel theorem on the critical-level centre: functions on \(\check G\)-opers on the punctured disc. Opers are connections of the form \(\partial_t+\check f+\check{\mathfrak b}((t))\) modulo \(\check N((t))\)-gauge. Beilinson–Drinfeld and Raskin’s 2012 geometric proof relate this isomorphism to geometric Satake: global sections of spherical sheaves on \(\mathrm{Gr}_G\) at critical level are free over the centre, with fibres the representations of \(\check G\).


## 2. What “categorical” adds


An isomorphism of algebras \(A\cong B\) induces an equivalence of naïve module categories, but \(W_\kappa\text{-mod}\) in Raskin’s sense is not the naïve discrete modules of a topological algebra. It is the ind-completion of a derived category generated by generalized vacua \(W^n_\kappa\). Affine Skryabin identifies that category with the Whittaker category of \(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\). Transporting Feigin–Frenkel across affine Skryabin therefore yields an equivalence that does not mention \(W\)-algebras at all:
\[
\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa\text{-mod}\bigr)\;\simeq\;\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\check{\mathfrak g}}_{\check\kappa}\text{-mod}\bigr).
\]
Whittaker coinvariants for \(N((t))\) on one side match Whittaker coinvariants for \(\check N((t))\) on the other. That is **categorical Feigin–Frenkel**. The bounded-below derived statement follows from the abelian equivalence of hearts \(W_{\mathfrak g,\kappa}\text{-mod}^\heartsuit\simeq W_{\check{\mathfrak g},\check\kappa}\text{-mod}^\heartsuit\) plus affine Skryabin; the full DG statement requires matching compact generators, which is the unique vacuum-preserving isomorphism \(W^n_{\mathfrak g,\kappa}\cong W^n_{\check{\mathfrak g},\check\kappa}\).


At critical level the dual Whittaker category is quasi-coherent sheaves on opers:
\[
\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}}\text{-mod}\bigr)\;\simeq\;\mathrm{QCoh}\bigl(\mathrm{Op}_{\check G}(\mathring D)\bigr).
\]
The adolescent filtration \(\mathrm{Whit}^{\le n}\) matches the subscheme of opers with poles of order at most \(n\). This is the derived, Whittaker form of “the critical-level category lives over the space of opers.”


## 3. Why one cannot skip affine Skryabin


Feigin–Frenkel as an isomorphism of vertex algebras already gives an equivalence of ordinary (positive-energy, grading-restricted) module categories, and Zhu’s algebra of both sides is \(Z(\mathfrak g)\cong Z(\check{\mathfrak g})\). That is not categorical Feigin–Frenkel. The Whittaker category contains many objects that are not ordinary vertex-algebra modules: generalized vacua \(W^n_\kappa\), modules with poles, and, at critical level, all quasi-coherent sheaves on opers, including those supported at irregular opers. Affine Skryabin is what identifies those objects with Whittaker Kac–Moody modules rather than with naïve \(W\)-modules. Raskin notes that categorical duality at critical level was the original motivation of the paper, and that there is no more direct route.


## 4. Place in local geometric Langlands


Frenkel–Gaitsgory propose that a \(\check G\)-local system \(\sigma\) on the punctured disc should produce a category of \(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}}\)-modules with an action of \(G((t))\), the local geometric Langlands fibre at \(\sigma\). When \(\sigma\) is an oper the expected category is a Whittaker category with central character the point of \(\mathrm{Op}_{\check G}\) corresponding to \(\sigma\). Categorical Feigin–Frenkel says that this fibre, as a category, is the same as the Whittaker category for the dual Kac–Moody algebra at the dual level. At critical level it is \(\mathrm{QCoh}\) of a formal neighbourhood of that oper.


The Fundamental Local Equivalence of the geometric Langlands programme (Arinkin–Beraldo–Campbell–Chen–Faergeman–Gaitsgory–Lin–Raskin–Rozenblyum) is the critical-level, full derived upgrade of this picture, comparing Kac–Moody localization on \(\mathrm{Gr}_G\) with ind-coherent sheaves on opers. Categorical Feigin–Frenkel is the Whittaker slice of that equivalence.


## 5. Neighbouring dualities


| Statement | What is identified |
|---|---|
| Feigin–Frenkel 1991 | vertex algebras \(W^k(\mathfrak g)\cong W^\ell(\check{\mathfrak g})\) |
| FF at critical level | \(Z(U(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}}))\cong\mathbb C[\mathrm{Op}_{\check G}]\) |
| Arakawa–Frenkel | duality of certain *representations* of \(W\)-algebras, for quantum geometric Langlands |
| Creutzig–Linshaw et al. | hook-type and subregular dualities, including superalgebras; convolution enhancement |
| Categorical FF (Raskin) | \(\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa)\simeq\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\check{\mathfrak g}}_{\check\kappa})\) |
| Critical categorical FF | \(\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}})\simeq\mathrm{QCoh}(\mathrm{Op}_{\check G}(\mathring D))\) |


Hook-type and Feigin–Semikhatov dualities are vertex-algebra isomorphisms for non-principal nilpotents (or superalgebras) at dual levels. They have not been rewritten as equivalences of Whittaker categories for the corresponding affine Kac–Moody data; that would be a non-principal affine Skryabin plus a non-principal Feigin–Frenkel, neither of which is in Raskin’s paper.


## 6. What travels and what does not


What travels: the compact generators \(W^n_\kappa\), the t-structure on \(\mathrm{Whit}\), the Drinfeld–Sokolov functor as the composite to \(\mathrm{Vect}\), and at critical level the stratification by pole order of opers.


What does not travel automatically: Zhu’s ordinary modules (those still match via \(Z(\mathfrak g)\cong Z(\check{\mathfrak g})\), independently of Raskin), finite-dimensional modules of non-principal finite \(W\)-algebras, and logarithmic modules of lattice-orbifold \(W\)-algebras. Categorical Feigin–Frenkel is an equivalence of Whittaker DG categories of affine Kac–Moody algebras at dual levels, and that is the whole statement.


The 39-screen remains unrelated. There is no Langlands dual of a rotation algebra, no space of opers whose functions could be slip coboundaries, and no Whittaker category of \(A_\alpha^\infty\)-modules.
The Fundamental Local Equivalence is the local, categorical substitute for geometric Satake in the quantum and critical-level settings. It identifies a Kac–Moody (or Whittaker) category for \(G\) at level \(\kappa\) with a Whittaker category of \(\mathcal D\)-modules on the affine Grassmannian of the dual group at the dual level. The 2024 geometric Langlands series proves the critical-level form that the global theorem uses; Campbell–Dhillon–Raskin proved most of the quantum form earlier.


## 1. Why Satake is not enough


Geometric Satake is
\[
\operatorname{Perv}_{G(\mathcal O)}(\operatorname{Gr}_G)\;\simeq\;\operatorname{Rep}(\check G).
\]
It is the spherical, abelian, *critical-to-infinity* statement: \(G(\mathcal O)\)-equivariant perverse sheaves match representations of the dual group. Quantum geometric Langlands needs a statement that remembers a level \(\kappa\) on both sides, and local geometric Langlands needs a statement about categories with a \(G((t))\)-action, not just spherical sheaves. Gaitsgory–Lurie proposed the replacement that later acquired the name FLE.


## 2. The spherical statement


Write \(\mathrm{KL}_\kappa(G)\) for the category of Kac–Moody modules at level \(\kappa\) that are integrable for \(G[[t]]\) (the spherical block), or equivalently the category of \(G(\mathcal O)\)-equivariant \(\mathcal D\)-modules on \(\mathrm{Gr}_G\) at that level. Write \(\mathrm{Whit}(\mathcal D_{\check\kappa}(\mathrm{Gr}_{\check G}))\) for the spherical Whittaker category of the dual group: strong \(\check N((t))\)-invariants on \(\mathcal D\)-modules on \(\mathrm{Gr}_{\check G}\) at the dual level, with a nondegenerate character.


**Spherical FLE.** For a level \(\kappa\),
\[
\mathrm{KL}_\kappa(G)\;\simeq\;\mathrm{Whit}\bigl(\mathcal D_{\check\kappa}(\mathrm{Gr}_{\check G})\bigr)
\]
as factorization categories (unital, and compatible with convolution). At \(\kappa=\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}}\) the dual level is infinity and the right-hand side becomes \(\operatorname{Rep}(\check G)\) after a careful limit, recovering a derived form of Satake. At \(\kappa=\infty\) one obtains the opposite identification. The levels are dual in the Feigin–Frenkel sense: \(r(\kappa-\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}})(\check\kappa-\check\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}})=1\) up to the usual normalisation.


There is an Iwahori (or affine-flag) enhancement, replacing \(\mathrm{Gr}\) by \(\mathrm{Fl}\) and spherical blocks by Iwahori-integrable modules, with Arkhipov–Bezrukavnikov as the critical-level special case.


## 3. Quantum FLE, good levels


Campbell–Dhillon–Raskin proved the quantum FLE — both spherical and affine-flag — at **good** levels: after restriction to every simple factor, \(\kappa\) is either irrational, or rational with denominator coprime to the bad primes of the root system. The method is Soergel-theoretic: both sides are identified with modules over an affine Hecke algebra (or a Soergel bimodule category) attached to the integral Weyl group at that level, and the two Hecke algebras match by Feigin–Frenkel duality of the corresponding \(W\)-algebras. Bad rational levels, including some positive levels, remain exceptional in that paper. Critical level is not “good” in their sense; it is the classical limit, treated separately.


## 4. Critical FLE in the 2024 series


Paper II of the geometric Langlands proof (Arinkin–Beraldo–Campbell–Chen–Faergeman–Gaitsgory–Lin–Raskin–Rozenblyum) formulates and proves FLE **at the critical level**, in the language the global theorem needs: factorization categories, ind-coherent sheaves in infinite type, and Kac–Moody localization.


The critical spherical FLE compares


- Kac–Moody localization of \(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}}\)-modules on \(\mathrm{Gr}_G\),
- ind-coherent sheaves on the space of \(\check G\)-opers (or, equivalently, the Whittaker category of \(\mathcal D\)-modules on \(\mathrm{Gr}_{\check G}\) at infinite level).


This is the statement Frenkel–Gaitsgory conjectured as the local input to Hecke eigensheaves: a regular oper should produce a category of critical-level modules that localizes onto \(\mathrm{Gr}_G\) as a Hecke eigensheaf with that eigenvalue. Raskin’s \(\mathrm{GL}_2\) localization theorem was the rank-one case. Paper II gives the general critical FLE and studies how that local equivalence interacts with the global Langlands functor constructed in Paper I.


Categorical Feigin–Frenkel sits inside this: the Whittaker slice of critical FLE is
\[
\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_{\mathrm{crit}}\text{-mod})\;\simeq\;\mathrm{QCoh}(\mathrm{Op}_{\check G}(\mathring D)),
\]
which is exactly Raskin’s critical corollary of affine Skryabin plus Feigin–Frenkel. FLE is the *spherical* (and Iwahori) enhancement, with a \(G[[t]]\)-action on the left and a factorization structure on both sides.


## 5. How the pieces nest


| Statement | Sides |
|---|---|
| Geometric Satake | \(\operatorname{Perv}_{G(\mathcal O)}(\mathrm{Gr}_G)\simeq\operatorname{Rep}(\check G)\) |
| Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg | \(D_{G(\mathcal O)\rtimes\mathbb G_m}(\mathrm{Gr})\simeq D(\mathrm{HC}(U(\check{\mathfrak g})))\) |
| Affine Skryabin | \(\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa)\simeq W_\kappa\text{-mod}\) |
| Categorical FF | \(\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\mathfrak g}_\kappa)\simeq\mathrm{Whit}(\widehat{\check{\mathfrak g}}_{\check\kappa})\) |
| Quantum FLE (good \(\kappa\)) | \(\mathrm{KL}_\kappa(G)\simeq\mathrm{Whit}(\mathcal D_{\check\kappa}(\mathrm{Gr}_{\check G}))\) |
| Critical FLE (GLC II) | KM localization on \(\mathrm{Gr}_G\) \(\simeq\) \(\mathrm{IndCoh}\) on opers / spherical Whittaker at \(\infty\) |


Satake is FLE at \((\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}},\infty)\) on hearts of perverse t-structures. Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg is a mixed, Koszul-dual form of the same spherical identification, with HC bimodules in place of \(\operatorname{Rep}(\check G)\). Affine Skryabin and categorical Feigin–Frenkel are the *Whittaker* rather than spherical columns. FLE is the statement that those two columns, after factorization, are the same.


## 6. Role in the global theorem


The global geometric Langlands equivalence is supposed to identify \(\mathcal D\)-modules on \(\mathrm{Bun}_G(X)\) with (ind-coherent) sheaves on \(\mathrm{LocSys}_{\check G}(X)\). The Hecke action on the automorphic side is factorization homology of the spherical category \(\mathrm{KL}_\kappa(G)\); the spectral action is factorization homology of \(\operatorname{Rep}(\check G)\) or of \(\mathrm{Whit}(\mathcal D(\mathrm{Gr}_{\check G}))\). FLE says those two local factorization categories match, so the Hecke and spectral actions match. Without FLE one can still construct a functor (Paper I); with FLE one knows the functor intertwines the two actions and, after temperedness and restrictedness comparisons, is an equivalence.


That is the precise sense in which FLE is “local Satake for the global theorem.” It is not a theorem about finite \(W\)-algebras, Zhu algebras, or Goldie-rank polynomials. Those remain the representation theory of a single associative algebra. FLE is a theorem about factorization categories attached to loop groups at dual levels.
